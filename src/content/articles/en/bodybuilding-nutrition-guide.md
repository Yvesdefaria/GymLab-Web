Bodybuilding nutrition comes down to four decisions: how much you eat, how much protein you take, how you spread carbohydrates and what you do with fat. Everything else is detail that changes with the goal, appetite and schedule. This guide orders those decisions and links the page that develops each one.

## Calories: the starting point

Total calories decide whether you gain weight, hold it or lose it. Before arguing about foods, it helps to know how much you spend. A maintenance estimate based on weight, height, age and activity gives a reasonable starting figure, and the weekly weight average over two or three weeks shows whether that figure works.

If the average rises faster than you want, cut 200 to 300 kcal a day. If it falls when you wanted to maintain, add the same amount. Small changes are easier to read than large ones, and one week is not enough to judge any of them. The guide to [basic nutrition rules](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-basics/) explains how the starting point is calculated and adjusted.

## Protein: how much and in how many meals

The reference for anyone training strength is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day. Above that range no extra muscle appears, and below it recovery gets harder when the deficit tightens. For a 75 kilo person that is 120 to 165 grams a day.

Distribution matters almost as much as the total. Between 25 and 40 grams per meal, across three to five meals, stimulates muscle protein synthesis repeatedly. Eggs, chicken, fish, lean meat, dairy, legumes and soy cover the range without powders. Anyone who does not eat meat finds the sources and adjustments in the guide to [building muscle on a vegetarian diet](/en/vegetarian-muscle-gain/).

## Carbohydrates and glycogen

Carbohydrates are the fuel that produces energy fastest when intensity rises. They are stored as glycogen in muscle and liver, and that reserve shapes the quality of the session. For regular strength training, the practical range is 3 to 5 grams per kilo a day.

Fibre falls inside the same sum: 25 to 35 grams a day, spread across vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains. Raising it suddenly causes gas, so it is worth doing over weeks. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) details amounts and types, and the one on [insulin in bodybuilding](/en/insulin-and-bodybuilding/) explains how the hormone carries glucose to muscle.

## Fat: the minimum that matters

One gram of fat provides 9 kcal, more than twice a gram of protein or carbohydrate. That density explains why it is easy to overshoot with fat and why it is worth weighing at first. What gets stored is the surplus of the day, whatever nutrient it comes from.

The reference stays between 0.8 and 1 gram per kilo a day. Below that, the diet loses satiety and covering essential fats becomes harder. Olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish and eggs cover the range with ordinary food.

## Meal size and frequency

Three to five meals are enough. The exact number depends on appetite and schedule, not on a law. What does deserve attention is size: a very large meal before training sits badly, and a very small one leaves hunger by mid afternoon.

Every meal should carry protein, a carbohydrate source and vegetables. Fat and fibre slow digestion, so the meal before a session becomes lighter in both. The rest of the day does not need that precaution. The guide to [meals around training time](/en/meal-timing-around-training/) orders morning, midday and evening schedules.

## Water: 2 to 3 litres and a little more

The starting reference is 2 to 3 litres a day, rising with sweat. A long session can add half a litre or more, especially in heat. Thirst and urine colour are better signals than a fixed number.

Drinking more than that does not improve performance and can sit badly during training. Spread water across the day and take a bottle to the session.

## Supplements: few and with a reason

Food covers almost everything. Protein powder solves a meal in a hurry, creatine monohydrate has support for strength and size at 3 to 5 grams a day, and vitamin D offsets the lack of sun in winter. Caffeine before training is optional and depends on tolerance.

High-dose antioxidants are not a recovery tool and can blunt training adaptations. The page on [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) reviews what is unnecessary and why.

## Adjusting with data

The plan is reviewed with three figures: the weekly weight average, the loads you move and the waist. If weight holds and loads rise, nutrition works. If weight rises and loads fall, there is too much food. If weight falls and loads stall, there is too little food or rest.

Change one variable every two weeks and let the body respond. Rushing to adjust everything at once turns the diet into a lottery. With four clear references and a calm review, nutrition stops being the weak point of training.
