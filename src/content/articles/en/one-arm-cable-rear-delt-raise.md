The cable has an advantage over the dumbbell: resistance does not vanish when the arm changes position. It also has a trap. The direction it pulls depends on the height of the pulley, and that height decides which muscle does the work.

## Setup: low pulley, hinged torso and the free hand braced

Set the pulley to its lowest position and clip on a single handle. Stand in front of the machine with the feet at shoulder width and the torso hinged at the hip until it is almost parallel to the floor.

The free hand rests on a bench or on the machine frame. That brace does two things: it takes load off the lower back and lets the arm work without the torso moving. The knees stay slightly bent and the back stays flat.

Take the handle with the palm facing in and start with the arm hanging, hand at knee height and a soft bend in the elbow.

## The path

From the knee, the arm sweeps out in a wide arc to shoulder height. At the end point the forearm is perpendicular to the floor.

It stops there. Going higher forces the torso to turn or the shoulder to shrug, and neither adds work to the rear deltoid.

## Pulley height picks the line

The cable pulls in the direction it leaves the pulley. With a low pulley, that direction is almost horizontal relative to the shoulder, and the rear deltoid works in the plane where it abducts.

If the pulley is high, the cable pulls up and in. The arm takes the shortest path towards the body and the lats join the movement: the exercise looks more like a pulldown than a raise.

A low pulley keeps the line horizontal, and with it the range of the rear deltoid. Any higher setting tilts the cable upwards and shortens that range. The height of the handle at the start also counts: at knee height the arc is long; if it starts at hip height, the bottom part is lost.

## The scapula stays quiet

The shoulder moves inside the joint and the scapula stays in place. Squeezing the shoulder blades on the pull is the most repeated mistake: the work goes to the rhomboids and the mid traps, and the rear deltoid is left watching. The guide to the [rear delts](/en/rear-delt-training/) develops the idea in more detail.

## Mistakes that change the exercise

- **Turning the torso on the pull.** The cable comes closer and the deltoid stops following the arc. The free hand on the bench is there to feel that turn.
- **Going above shoulder height.** The elbow loses its perpendicular and the trapezius takes over.
- **Using the high pulley.** The gesture becomes a one-arm pulldown.
- **Bending the elbow.** The forearm stops being a fixed lever and the biceps joins the movement.
- **Loading too much.** The pulley lets you move more than the rear deltoid controls, and the body compensates by turning.

## Dose and where it goes in the session

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps per arm, with 45 to 60 seconds of rest. The load is light and only goes up when 15 reps hold the same arc with a still torso.

It is an exercise for the end of the session, once the presses are done. It also works as a first movement for the weak side, before fatigue forces a compensation.

It is not an exercise for chasing [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/). When technique breaks, the rear deltoid stops working and the torso moves the weight. The set ends on the rep before that.

## Where it fits in the family

The [side-lying one-arm rear delt raise](/en/side-lying-one-arm-rear-delt-raise/) removes the cable and the brace, and isolates one side with the bench supporting the torso. The [reverse pec deck](/en/reverse-pec-deck/) makes the same path with the chest supported and lets you get closer to failure. The [dumbbell rear delt raise](/en/dumbbell-rear-delt-raise/) with the torso bent over remains the bilateral reference.

The [shoulder routines](/en/shoulder-routines/) place this exercise after the presses, and [shoulder anatomy](/en/shoulder-anatomy/) explains why the rear deltoid answers to a horizontal plane and not a vertical one.
