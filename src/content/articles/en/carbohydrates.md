The debate about carbohydrates mixes two different questions: how many to eat and what quality. The first decides performance and weight; the second decides satiety, fibre and digestive health. Both have an answer without extremes.

## What carbohydrates are and how much energy they provide

Carbohydrates are sugar molecules joined in chains of different lengths. The body breaks them down into glucose, uses it for energy and stores the surplus as glycogen in muscle and liver. One gram provides 4 kcal, against 9 for fat and 4 for protein.

During intense effort, glucose is the fuel that produces the most energy per unit of time. When glycogen falls, intensity drops and the session holds up worse. That is the practical reason to watch the amount.

## Simple, complex and fibre

The classic division separates sugars of one or two units, such as glucose, fructose, sucrose and lactose, from complex carbohydrates, made of long glucose chains. Starch in rice, potato, bread and pasta belongs to the second group. Fruit and milk fall into the first and remain useful foods: sugar does not make an orange bad.

Fibre is the carbohydrate the intestine does not digest. It adds volume, retains water and feeds the gut microbiota. The practical reference is 25 to 35 grams a day, spread across vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and nuts. Raising it suddenly causes gas, so it is worth doing over weeks.

## How many carbohydrates a day

The amount depends on training volume and intensity, not a universal rule. For regular strength training, the reasonable range is 3 to 5 grams per kilo of body weight per day. For aerobic endurance with long sessions, it rises to 6-10 grams per kilo. They are ranges, not prescriptions.

The rest of the diet is organised around them. Protein stays at 1.6-2.2 grams per kilo, fat covers the gap and total calories decide whether weight is lost or gained. Total calories give the starting point, and the guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) explains how to increase without accumulating too much fat.

## Carbohydrates, weight and body fat

A gram of carbohydrate does not turn into fat by magic. The body fills glycogen first and uses glucose for energy; when total calories exceed expenditure, the surplus is stored, whether it comes from carbohydrates, fat or protein. Fat is the densest nutrient and accumulates excess more easily, but the final sum is the same.

Low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets produce similar loss when calories and protein are matched. The difference usually lies in adherence and performance: anyone training hard notices the lack of glycogen sooner. Choosing one or the other is a practical decision.

## Timing around training

A meal with carbohydrates three or four hours before training reaches the muscle in time. If the session is long, adding carbohydrates during training keeps glucose stable and delays fatigue. Afterwards, the next meal replaces what was used; there is no need to run to the fridge in the first minutes.

The daily total matters more than the exact minute. Anyone training in the morning can spread carbohydrates between breakfast and lunch; anyone training at night places them in the meal before and at dinner. The guide to [carbs at night](/en/carbs-at-night-and-fat-gain/) dismantles the fear of the clock.

## Ketosis without the scare

When carbohydrates fall very low, the liver produces ketone bodies and the body uses fat as its main fuel. It is a real metabolic state, not a disease, and for a healthy adult it is not dangerous in itself. It can help lose fat when total calories fall, and it can also complicate high-intensity sessions and social life.

A ketogenic diet is not the only way to lose fat. A diet with carbohydrates and controlled calories works just as well and is easier to sustain over time. [Intermittent fasting](/en/intermittent-fasting-and-weights/) is another way to organise meals that also depends on energy balance.

## How to build the plate

Half the plate can be vegetables, a quarter protein and a quarter carbohydrate. On hard training days, the carbohydrate quarter grows; on rest days, it stays at a moderate portion. Potato, rice, oats, pasta and wholemeal bread cover the energy side, and legumes add fibre and protein at the same time.

Quality matters in the details: whole grains, whole fruit instead of juice and few products with added sugar. None of that requires eliminating food groups or counting every gram.
