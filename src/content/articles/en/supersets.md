There are many ways to group sets. The best known are supersets, usually explained as two exercises for opposing muscle groups with no rest beyond moving to the next exercise. One example is a set of biceps curls followed by a set of triceps pushdowns, or leg extensions followed by leg curls.

## What a superset is

A superset is one set of exercise A immediately followed by one set of exercise B. The rest comes after the pair, not between the two exercises. Then you rest one to two minutes before the next superset.

A superset uses two exercises. With three it is a triset, and with four or more, a [giant set](/en/giant-sets/). The difference is the number of chained movements.

The classic example is a set of 10 biceps curls, followed without rest by 10 French presses, then rest. Repeat the pair for the number of sets you need.

Picking two random exercises and doing one set of each is not enough. A superset needs some logic to be effective.

## Types and when to use each one

Depending on how you pair the exercises, the superset changes name and effect. These are the main types.

### Pre-fatigue

You pair two exercises for the same muscle group. The first is an isolation movement, with the muscle working alone; the second is a compound movement in which the muscle works with others. Use it when the target muscle is hard to feel or when it does not fail before its helpers.

### Post-fatigue

This is the reverse order: the compound movement first, then the isolation one. Use it when you want to move the heaviest weight on the exercise that builds the most mass.

### Compound supersets

Two compound exercises for the same muscle group. They work the muscle from several angles and recruit more auxiliary muscles along the way.

### Isolation supersets

Two isolation exercises for the same muscle, chosen to work different areas. They demand care: if the two movements overlap, the superset loses part of its point.

## Pre-fatigue and post-fatigue

The pre-fatigue example is dumbbell flyes followed by the bench press. The bench press uses pecs, triceps and shoulders. Because the pec is the strongest of the three, you may reach failure in the triceps before the pec has worked to its maximum. Pre-fatiguing it with flyes means the pec arrives tired for the press and fails before the other muscles.

It also helps you feel the muscle. Many beginners do not feel their pecs on the bench press, and the previous superset makes it easier. Another biceps example is a machine preacher curl followed by a straight-bar curl.

The downside of pre-fatigue is clear. The main muscle arrives tired for the compound, so you cannot use your maximum weight on the movement that builds the most mass.

Post-fatigue reverses the order with the same exercises: bench press followed by dumbbell flyes. You can use your maximum weight on the compound, which is why it is usually the more recommendable option. A common back example is a barbell row followed by straight-arm pulldowns.

## Antagonists

This type pairs opposing muscle groups. The basis is that, when one muscle works, its antagonist relaxes. That saves time: while one muscle rests, you train the opposite one.

After each superset there is a rest, so the first muscle gets extra recovery and you can work it hard again without stretching the session. These are the pairs you can alternate:

## Benefits and limits

There are three advantages over a conventional system: time saved in the session, more blood flow to the muscle or muscles worked and a higher demand for intensity.

Working one muscle and moving to another increases blood flow in the area, [muscle pump](/en/muscle-pump/) and vascularity. Alternating biceps and triceps, for example, stretches one while the other works, which speeds recovery and helps clear next-day stiffness and soreness.

The limits start with your level. These advantages are meant for a non-beginner with average knowledge of weight training. That is why you do not put a beginner or a very thin person on supersets.

## How long to rest

Between the two exercises of the superset there is no rest: only the time it takes to move from one to the other. When the pair is done, rest one to two minutes before the next superset.

That rest is what keeps quality high. Cut it too short and the next superset becomes half a set; stretch it too long and you lose the time saving you were after. The guide to [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) helps you adjust the number to your goal.

Supersets are a tool, not a permanent format. Use them in blocks and alternate with conventional training. If you want a concrete example to close your back day, the [back superset finisher](/en/back-superset-finisher/) applies the pattern with two exercises. And if you want to push intensity further, [high-intensity training](/en/high-intensity-training/) techniques are the next step. That is where [forced reps](/en/forced-reps/) come in, which need a partner who measures their help.
