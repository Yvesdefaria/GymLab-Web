Your goal when you start aerobic exercise is to finish the session without running out of gas in the first minutes and, above all, to arrive at the next one recovered. To meet that goal, the machine has to satisfy three conditions: it must be comfortable enough to hold the load for the required time, it must use movements that do not raise your heart rate too much, and it must be gentle on your joints during the first sessions.

## How to choose an aerobic machine

Those three conditions rule out heroic solutions. If you are coming back after a long break, the priority is to accumulate minutes without punishing your knees or your lower back. When intensity is well controlled, [LISS cardio](/en/liss-cardio-guide/) sets the pace for the first weeks: steady, easy effort with the talk test as your reference. To fit the machine into a full plan, [cardiovascular training in your programme](/en/cardiovascular-training-in-your-program/) orders the blocks.

## Stationary bikes: adjustable intensity, simple movement

Bikes are a classic in fitness rooms. They offer plenty of advantages and work for beginners and advanced users alike, because you can adjust the workload. Pedalling is a cyclical movement that needs no technique.

The recumbent version is the star cardio machine for anyone starting out: the posture is comfortable, your back is supported, the lumbar area carries no load, pedalling has no impact, the seated position does not push your heart rate up too far and you can adjust intensity without trouble.

**Precautions.** Set the saddle height so your knee almost reaches full extension while pedalling. With the saddle too low, the spine tends to flex excessively; with it too high, you will feel your hips rocking on every pedal stroke.

**Contraindications.** In late pregnancy it can be uncomfortable for the pelvic floor.

**Suitable for.** Any type of user: intensity is adjustable and the movement is simple.

## Rowing: back work, venous return and low impact

The rower is the great forgotten machine in fitness rooms and one of the most interesting. The seated position keeps your heart rate from rising too high and supports venous return. The muscular work lands on the back, which usually needs more attention: the rowing stroke recruits the whole extensor chain and encourages better posture.

**Precautions.** Move correctly to avoid lumbar overload. Take the handle towards your hip, open your chest and bring your shoulders and elbows back. Avoid pulling the handle to your chest by flexing the spine and flaring your elbows out.

**Contraindications.** Almost none. People with lumbar overload should not overdo the rower.

**Suitable for.** Keeping up cardiovascular work without fatiguing the lower body. Also for people with a slightly kyphotic posture (a hunched back) who spend long hours sitting.

## Ellipticals: upper and lower body in one movement

The movement traces an ellipse and mimics the skiing stride. It engages the upper and lower body, which is a big advantage for reaching high calorie expenditure. Because it recruits a lot of muscle mass, effort is spread out and local overload or fatigue is less likely, so you can keep going longer. The arms let you push or pull, which brings other muscle groups into the work.

Some ellipticals add a lateral shift to the foot platforms. That sideways movement is not natural, and joints such as the knee, especially in women, take excessive tension on the inner side. Anyone with knock-knees (X-shaped legs) should avoid it.

**Precautions.** Do not hyperextend the knee at the extension phase: keep a slight bend throughout. Activate the deep abdominal muscles to avoid lumbar overload, and the pull will also be more efficient.

**Contraindications.** Some people with circulation problems feel tingling in their toes because of the constant pressure on the metatarsal area.

**Suitable for.** Anyone who wants high calorie expenditure. It is ideal for skiers, cyclists and runners for indoor training.

## Treadmills: a natural stride with real impact

Like the bike, the treadmill uses a very natural movement pattern. The big drawback of running is impact, especially for older or overweight people. On a gym treadmill you can walk with increasing incline and play with speed and gradient. Modern cushioning systems make each footstrike quite comfortable.

**Precautions.** If your balance is poor or you get dizzy easily, take extra care: a stumble or a loss of balance can turn into a serious fall. Do not jump onto a moving belt. Do not drift too far from the front. Some treadmills include a safety cord clipped to your waist that stops the belt if it comes loose. Always wear a proper running shoe.

**Contraindications.** Ankle, knee, hip and lumbar injuries or conditions: impact can make them worse. Heart rate climbs too high and venous return suffers, which adds load to the heart, so people with cardiovascular conditions or poor circulation should avoid long treadmill sessions. It is also not the best choice if you are overweight, because the landing impact absorbs your full body weight and stress fractures and overload can appear.

**Suitable for.** Intermediate and advanced users, although beginners can walk on an incline. Also for runners, hikers and endurance athletes.

## Steppers: high frequency, short range

Stepping on small stairs at a high rate tends to bring more drawbacks than benefits. It recruits very little muscle mass over a very short range, and local fatigue shows up quickly in the soleus and calves.

**Precautions.** Almost none: it is an easy machine to use.

**Contraindications.** Not very suitable for people with poor lower-body circulation, because it does not support venous return and makes circulation harder. If you are overweight, the calf muscles take excessive work, and overload and local fatigue appear in the soleus and calves. Also unsuitable for anyone with a shortened Achilles tendon.

**Suitable for.** Advanced users or athletes who need that movement in their sport, such as mountaineers.

## Arm ergometers: the upper body as the engine

It began as a machine for people with a functional impairment in the lower body: the seat can be removed so a wheelchair user can reach it. It is becoming more popular because, done standing, it forces the upper body to work and the rest of the body to stabilise. That means great cardiovascular work and a very functional stimulus, since it activates the deep muscles of the trunk.

**Precautions.** Set the handle distance to match your joint levers.

**Contraindications.** None.

**Suitable for.** Any type of person: it improves stability and trains the upper body. It is ideal for adding work without loading the legs. Swimmers, rowers and climbers use it.
