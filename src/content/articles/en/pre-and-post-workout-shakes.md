## What decides the result

A shake is a liquid meal. Its value comes from what it adds to the daily total, not from the exact minute it goes down. For anyone training for strength, protein runs from 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight, and carbohydrate from 3 to 5 grams per kilo when training volume is high.

An 80 kilo lifter with four weekly sessions needs around 144 grams of protein and between 240 and 400 grams of carbohydrate. If the shake helps you get there, it does its job. If it lands on top of a diet that already gets there, it only adds calories. The [two best supplements for muscle growth](/en/best-supplements-for-muscle-growth/) guide works through the full arithmetic.

## A shake is a meal

Made with milk, oats and fruit, a shake covers protein and carbohydrate in a format you drink in two minutes. Made with water and powder, it is a protein snack, useful when food falls short and poor as a replacement for a full meal.

The [protein supplements](/en/protein-supplements/) guide explains the types and the cost per gram. If the problem is that there is no time to eat, the [meal replacement](/en/meal-replacement-shakes/) page covers the complete version with label and numbers.

## Before training

Two ingredients have support for the session, and neither is obligatory.

**Beta-alanine** works by accumulation. 3.2 to 6.4 grams a day for four weeks raise muscle carnosine and help in efforts of one to four minutes. Taking it fifteen minutes before does nothing; the tingling skin is not a sign of effect.

**Caffeine** improves performance at moderate doses, up to about 3 milligrams per kilo of body weight. A single espresso covers much of that figure in a 70 kilo person. The [creatine and caffeine](/en/creatine-and-caffeine/) guide covers the combination.

A shake with protein and carbohydrate an hour before training makes sense when the last meal was long ago. If the meal before was complete, the pre-workout shake adds nothing.

## After training

The thirty-minute window does not exist as a deadline. Protein synthesis stays elevated for hours after a strength session, and the daily total matters more than the exact split. Drinking the shake as you leave the locker room is convenient, especially if the next meal is four hours away, and it is not urgent.

What does matter is not stacking two protein-free meals after training. A shake fixes that part of the day, not recovery itself.

## Ingredients to skip

**Glutamine** shows up in many blends as a recovery aid. In a healthy adult who eats enough protein, the body makes it and trials show no extra muscle mass or less soreness.

**BCAAs** already come inside the protein in the shake. Adding five extra grams of leucine, isoleucine and valine to a meal that already carries 30 grams of complete protein has shown no advantage, and the shake already brings the nine essential amino acids. The [branched-chain amino acids](/en/bcaa-supplements/) guide reviews the studies cited in their favour.

**Gymnema** and the blends that promise an insulin spike belong to another era. Insulin is not the growth switch, and forcing it up with a plant extract adds an ingredient with no measured benefit.

**Casein** does not build more than whey. It is a slower protein, useful when many hours pass until the next meal, and not a requirement of the post-workout shake.

One example that covers both halves: 300 millilitres of milk, 40 grams of oats, a banana and 25 grams of protein powder. That lands near 36 grams of protein and 55 of carbohydrate, at a lower cost than a bought meal.
