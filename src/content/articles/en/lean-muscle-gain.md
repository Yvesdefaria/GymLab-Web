Gaining lean means gaining slowly. Muscle grows at a rate limited by training, genetics and years of experience. When the calorie surplus exceeds what the body can turn into muscle tissue, the rest is stored as fat. No food escapes that sum; what changes is the amount and the context.

## What lean gaining means

A lean bulk looks for the highest proportion of muscle per kilo gained. No food builds muscle on its own and no food makes you fat just by its name. The difference lies in total calories, protein and the training stimulus.

The practical consequence is that the plan is measured with the scale, the waist and strength. If weight rises and the waist does not, the split is good. If the waist rises faster than the reps, the surplus is too large.

## The rate: 0.25-0.5 % of body weight per week

That percentage is the reference for a lifter with some experience. At 80 kilos, between 200 and 400 grams a week, which is 0.8 to 1.6 kilos a month.

A beginner can sit at the high end for the first months. An advanced lifter usually moves at the low end, because every kilo of muscle costs more. If weight rises above 0.5 % a week in a sustained way, fat takes the lead.

## The 200-300 kcal surplus

It is the margin that covers digestion, the cost of training and the construction of new tissue. A surplus of 500 or 1,000 kcal does not double the gain: it brings fat forward and forces a longer cut afterwards.

The surplus applies to real maintenance. The guide to [maintenance calories](/en/maintenance-calories/) explains how to calculate it and how to correct it with two weeks of weight data.

## Protein: 1.6-2.2 g/kg

At 80 kilos, between 128 and 176 grams a day. Spread across three to five meals of 25 to 40 grams, protein synthesis gets a stimulus several times. Protein does not build muscle on its own; training gives the signal and food supplies the material.

The guide to [what to eat to build muscle](/en/what-to-eat-to-build-muscle/) sorts the sources, and the one on [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) compares cuts per 100 grams.

## Two bowls on the scale

A large bowl of cooked rice weighs a lot and provides few calories. A handful of nuts weighs little and provides many. A hundred grams of cooked rice is around 130 kcal, a hundred grams of oats around 380 and a hundred grams of walnuts around 650.

Sight is misleading because it measures volume, not energy. Weighing food for two weeks teaches those differences better than any list. After that you can eat by eye with a real idea of what is on the plate.

## The weekly trend rules

Weight is recorded daily while fasted and the weekly average is compared with the previous one. Two weeks without a rise call for 100-200 more kcal. A gain above the planned rate calls for cutting the same amount, starting with carbohydrates.

Waist and photos complete the picture. A bulking phase that adds strength and keeps the waist in place is going well even if the scale rises slowly.

## Training and rest

Without progressive overload there is no reason to gain tissue. Three to five strength sessions a week, with more weight or more reps on the main lifts, sustain the gain. The [volume training tables](/en/volume-training-tables/) offer structures that are already organised.

Sleeping seven to nine hours and keeping rest days is part of the plan. A surplus does not make up for lost sleep or a programme that changes every week.

## When to review the plan

Every four to six weeks, look at weight, waist and the main lifts together. If strength rises and the waist holds, the direction is right. If the waist grows faster than performance, trim the surplus.
