A slow tempo changes the stimulus of a set without changing the exercise. It works best when the rest of the routine stays at normal speed and the slow work is reserved for a couple of movements.

## What slow training is

In a normal rep, the lifting phase is usually faster than the lowering phase. Slow training makes both phases equal and longer: you lift and lower the weight with the same control and at a pace you can count.

The idea behind it is time under tension. By lengthening each rep, the muscle spends more seconds under the load. Normal speed remains the base; slow work is a tool for one or two exercises.

## How long each rep lasts

The standard is 4 to 6 seconds per phase, with both phases controlled. A full rep lasts 8 to 12 seconds. A set of 10 reps takes 80 to 120 seconds, more than twice as long as at normal speed.

The rep range does not change from a normal set: if your usual set is 10 reps, the slow set is 10 reps too. The guide to [reps and sets](/en/optimal-rep-range/) explains how to choose that number for your goal.

There is also a more extreme variant: 10 seconds up and 4 seconds down, 14 seconds per rep. That version is reserved for one specific exercise and one point in the plan, not for the whole routine.

To write down the tempo you use and compare it with others, the guide to [rep tempo](/en/rep-tempo/) explains the three-digit notation.

## What weight to use

You use less weight. To do the same number of reps as in a normal set, you need a lighter load. The slow set is not about moving more kilos; it is about holding tension for longer.

Pick exercises where you can drop the weight or stop safely at failure, because you will not always have a partner. The technique fatigues the muscle very quickly.

If you cannot hold the tempo until the last rep, the weight is too high.

## What the research says

The evidence is not unanimous. Some slow protocols improve strength, and other studies find no difference from traditional training or even observe less progress when the tempo is very slow.

No tempo is backed by research as superior for everyone. The result depends on the exercise, the length of the rep and the goal. The practical conclusion stays the same: use it as a variation, not as a replacement.

If you want to understand which fibres come into play with light loads and long sets, the guide to [muscle fibre types](/en/muscle-fiber-types/) describes them.

## Advantages and limits

Advantages: you add variation without changing the exercise, you control technique and the muscle spends more time under tension. It is useful when the routine has become monotonous or when you want to work an exercise with less load.

Limits: sets take a long time, the session slows down and the stimulus does not replace a normal set. It is a way to modify the routine, not a system for the whole year.

It also competes with other [high-intensity](/en/high-intensity-training/) techniques that chase the same goal by another route. Alternating techniques is more sustainable than using one all the time.

## How to fit it into your routine

Use it occasionally. Pick one or two exercises and do three or four slow sets. The rest of the session stays at normal speed.

Choose exercises where you can let go of the weight safely at failure. [Exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) matters more than ever: slow tempo demands control through the whole range.

When the stimulus stops being new, return to normal speed and save the slow work for another exercise or for later.
