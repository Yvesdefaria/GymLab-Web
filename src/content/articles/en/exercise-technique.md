When you train for hypertrophy, the goal is to create the greatest possible stimulus in the muscle structure and then give the body time to repair it. That stimulus comes from choosing the loads and the way you perform each rep, rather than from punishing the muscle with intensity and no judgement.

## Why exercise technique matters

There are two ways to provoke muscle catabolism during a session. The first is to use very heavy loads that generate maximum tension in the muscle. The second is to combine loads with high-intensity techniques that concentrate tension and stimulation on the area you want to work.

Both routes are valid and both are necessary. The difference is the cost. Maximal loads carry a higher injury risk and recruit other muscle groups. Localised work with selective technique lowers that risk and sends the effort to the muscle you chose.

Even so, it is rare to see someone training in a localised way. The usual scene is a lot of weight moved with poor technique, full of momentum and muscles that should not be involved, all to lift more. The rule that sums it up is simple: do not just train hard, train smart.

## Prioritise compound exercises

Compound movements that involve large muscle groups, such as the squat, the row or the deadlift, demand more energy and have a greater metabolic impact than single-joint movements or those that work small groups.

That is why the session is built around them, while you are fresh and can give them full attention. Isolation work comes afterwards, with the muscle already warm and no need to move heavy loads.

## Speed and the eccentric phase

Execution speed is key to increasing the total work of a set. The practical rule is simple: lower the weight more slowly than you lift it. The eccentric phase, in which the muscle resists the load while it stretches, is performed more slowly than the concentric one.

There is evidence that this eccentric phase plays a part in muscle fibre development. The tension the muscle bears while it lengthens is part of the stimulus, so letting the weight drop without control cuts the work of the set. Exercise technique is measured on that way down too.

## Do not lock the joint

Locking the joint at the end of the range makes tension disappear. The clearest example is fully extending the legs on the leg press. The joint takes the load, the muscle stops supporting it and stimulation falls.

With the lockout you can lift more kilos and tire less, which is the opposite of what you want. Effective work makes itself felt during the set, and it is not less intense for that. Lockouts belong in maximal strength training, where the goal is to move the weight. Locking a loaded joint also raises the injury risk in that area.

## The right number of sets per muscle group

Volume has a practical ceiling. Alternating sets drain glycogen and increase protein breakdown, and that effect appears with a maximum of 10 to 16 sets per large muscle group and 6 to 12 sets for small ones.

Adding sets beyond those numbers does not seem to improve stimulation. The session turns into strength-endurance work with heavy fatigue, recovery stretches out and, if you keep the scheme for weeks, overtraining appears. You can fine-tune the split with the guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/).

## How to apply technique to every set

Applying exercise technique to every set means repeating the same pattern with a weight you can control. Before the first set, decide which muscle you want to train and which range you will respect. During the set, check three things on every rep: that you lower more slowly than you lift, that you do not lock the joint at the end and that the weight moves through the muscle rather than through momentum.

If technique breaks on rep 8 of 12, the set is over. Lowering the weight and completing the range leaves more useful work than adding reps with momentum. The guide to the [optimal rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/) sets the target for each set, and the guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains how to get close to the limit without overshooting.

The last check comes the next day: if you cannot tell which muscle you trained, the session did not land where it had to.
