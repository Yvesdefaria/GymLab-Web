The original article asked for six meals a day to keep metabolism active. Meal frequency does not change energy expenditure in any meaningful way; the daily total does. Eating every three hours is useful for something else: reaching the next meal without raiding the cupboard.

## The job of the snack

A useful snack carries enough protein to count and enough fibre to fill. With 20 or 25 grams of protein it covers about a fifth of what a 70 kg person needs while cutting, and the fibre stops hunger coming back twenty minutes later. The rest is logistics: it has to fit in a bag and need no kitchen.

## The egg arithmetic

Six egg whites and one yolk provide 24.3 grams of protein, 1.8 grams of carbohydrate and 4.5 grams of fat. It is the cheapest portable snack there is, and the original numbers are correct.

Boil a dozen on Sunday and keep them in the fridge. In the morning separate the whites you need, season with salt and pepper and take them in a container. If the taste gets old, a spoon of mustard or a little paprika fixes it without adding calories.

## Peanuts are not protein

Twenty grams of unsalted peanuts provide 5.16 grams of protein, 3.22 grams of carbohydrate and 9.84 grams of fat. The original count is right again, and that is exactly why peanuts cannot carry a snack: they are a fat source with a little protein thrown in.

They work as an add-on, not as the base. Cashews follow the same path and add about 175 kcal per quarter cup, so they get measured.

## Cottage cheese, vegetables and fruit

Half a cup of cottage cheese has 14 grams of protein and 1.15 grams of fat. With an apple or a pear it completes the snack and adds fibre. If the flavour falls flat, fruit fixes it better than salt.

Raw vegetables bring fibre, water and volume, not slow-digesting carbohydrate. They are something to chew alongside, and they help you reach five servings a day without effort. Carrot, celery, pepper, tomato and broccoli all work when cut the night before.

Fruit goes in whole: apple, strawberries, blueberries, banana or raisins. Juice does not, because it removes the fibre and disappears in two sips.

Preparation happens in two batches: one for the fridge and one for the bag. The fridge holds boiled eggs, cheese and cut vegetables. The bag takes fruit, the tuna tin and a measured bag of nuts.

## When to eat it

Mid-morning and mid-afternoon, or only in one of the two gaps if your main meals are large. If you train three hours after eating, a snack with protein and some carbohydrate lands better than a coffee on its own; the [pre-workout meal](/en/pre-workout-meal/) guide has the amounts.

Splitting meals has another use: it lets you raise total protein without bingeing. The [how to split meals](/en/how-to-split-meals/) guide covers the distribution without turning it into a rulebook.

## What was cut

The metabolism myth about eating six times. The body spends the same with three meals as with six if calories and protein match.

The sugar crash that supposedly pushes you towards biscuits. In a healthy person there is no such collapse; there is hunger, and it is solved with the snack in question.

Vegetables as slow carbohydrate. They are fibre and water, and their role is filling space, not fuelling glycogen.

And the fear of butter. A wholemeal sandwich with tuna, lettuce and tomato takes a thin layer of butter if the rest of the day adds up; the filling spoils it, not the fat.
