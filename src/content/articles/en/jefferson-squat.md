The bar sits between the legs, on the floor, and the body straddles it. From there the movement looks like a squat on the way down and a deadlift on the way up, and that mix is why almost nobody trains it.

## How to set it up

Load the bar on the floor with large plates, the ones that leave room to descend. The feet go at shoulder width or a little wider, toes slightly out, and the bar sits between the thighs.

The body leans over as if mounting, chest facing one side of the bar and back facing the other. One hand passes in front of the body and the other behind, both gripping the bar with straight arms. The chest stays high and the eyes look forward.

## The grip runs the show

The arms stay straight through the whole range. If the elbow bends, the bar comes closer to the body and the descent gets shorter. The grip is the first limit of the exercise: when the fingers do not reach or the bar slips, the weight is too high or the bar is in the wrong place.

That limit is part of the exercise. The Jefferson squat trains grip strength and hip mobility at the same time, which is why it is dosed as a practice drill and not as a loading basic.

## Depth is set by clearance

The archive asked for the deepest possible flexion while keeping the arms straight. That instruction has two problems: maximum hip flexion is not the goal, and the back cannot hold its position when the body goes below what the clearance allows.

The practical reference is the plates. Go down until they touch the floor or until the pelvis starts to roll forward, and keep whichever comes first. The second limit is the ankle: if the heels lift, the body is already below its useful range.

## The neutral spine

The torso stays upright and the spine neutral through the descent and the way up. A rounded back under load is the signal that the descent is over.

The guide to [squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) sets out the full framework, and the guide to [full range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) explains why range is measured by control and not in centimetres.

## Breathing and tempo

You breathe in on the way down and out on the way up, with the same logic as any loaded squat: the pressure supports the spine in the hard part. The descent is controlled; bouncing the plates off the floor sends the impulse into the back, which is exactly the area to protect.

## Common errors

The first is rounding the back to reach the floor with the plates. If the back curves, the range ended earlier.

The second is standing too wide to gain height. A wide stance brings the bar closer to the floor and pushes the grip away; if the setup does not come together, the problem is the bar, not the stance.

The third is bending the elbows on the way up. The load moves to the biceps and the bar drifts off the line of the legs.

The fourth is treating it as a max-load lift. Three light sets already leave the legs and the grip worked.

## Sets, reps and rest

Three sets of six to ten reps with a light load and ninety to a hundred and twenty seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this is the guide's recommendation. An empty bar with large plates is already a useful exercise and enough for most lifters.

## Where it fits

It is a mobility and grip drill with a leg load. It fits at the start of the session as preparation, or at the end as an accessory, and it also works as a test: if the descent is clean and the spine stays neutral, the hip and the ankle have what other movements need.

The [old-school squat leg routine](/en/old-school-squat-leg-routine/) already prescribes it inside a three-week block. The [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/) is the reference pattern, the [barbell hack squat](/en/barbell-hack-squat/) shares the start from the floor and the [sissy squat](/en/sissy-squat/) shares the taste for positions almost nobody trains.
