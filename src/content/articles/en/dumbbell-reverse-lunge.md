The reverse lunge is the quiet version of the exercise. There is no forward travel, the torso does not move over the front foot, and the movement looks more like lowering and rising in place than walking. That modesty is exactly what makes it useful: it removes the part of the movement where most people lose balance.

## What changes when the step goes back

The muscles are the same as in the forward lunge: the quadriceps, glutes and hamstrings of the front leg. What changes is how the load is shared and where the body sits during the descent.

In the reverse version the torso does not travel forward on the way down. The front knee stays over the foot with less shin angle, and the rear leg lands behind without pushing the body. For anyone who loses balance easily, that detail is enough to prefer it.

The quadriceps still work. The front leg slows the descent and pushes back up; the rear leg follows along.

## How to do it

1. Stand with the feet hip-width apart and a dumbbell in each hand at your sides.
2. Take a breath and step back a medium distance, keeping the torso upright and the eyes forward.
3. Lower by bending the front knee while the rear knee approaches the floor. The front shin stays close to vertical and the front foot stays flat.
4. Stop when both knees are near 90 degrees and the rear knee touches the floor without resting on it.
5. Push through the front foot to return. Exhale as you pass the hardest point.

The step has to be long enough that the front knee does not travel backwards on the way down. If the rear foot lands too close, the lunge turns into a narrow squat and the work is shared badly.

## The rear leg is a kickstand

The archive says the rear leg carries most of the weight and, two lines later, describes the push coming from the front leg. Both sentences cannot be true at once. The front leg carries the load; the rear leg balances, touches the floor with the knee and helps you return.

The difference matters. If the weight stays behind, the lunge becomes a hip stretch, the front knee loses stability and the quadriceps stops receiving the load. The test is simple: at the end of the set, the fatigue should sit in the front thigh.

## How deep to go

Ninety degrees at both knees is the comfortable reference, not a border. Going a little deeper is possible when the torso stays upright and the pelvis is quiet; the limit is the point where the trunk starts to tip forward.

The archive said as much in its own way: if hip flexibility does not allow you to keep the body upright, lower only until you stop leaning. That rule is worth more than any angle.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose is three sets of eight to ten reps per leg, with ninety seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this is the guide's recommendation and it matches the rest of the unilateral leg work.

You can alternate legs within the set or finish all reps on one side and switch. Alternating keeps the heart rate up and unloads the leg between reps; going side by side concentrates the work and lets you correct position without rushing.

Progression runs through load: when ten reps come out with an upright torso and no swaying, add weight. If you have no heavier dumbbells, add a one-second pause with the rear knee close to the floor.

## Where it fits

The reverse lunge works after the main lift of leg day, once the squat or the leg press has taken the heaviest work. It also works as the only unilateral movement in a home session, with a pair of dumbbells and room for one step.
