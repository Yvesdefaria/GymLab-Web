> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a health professional. If you have a diagnosed scoliosis, a disc herniation or pain that runs down the leg with numbness or loss of strength, get advice before you train.

The back tolerates a lot when you load it in a reasonable position and complains when the curve has already changed and training stays the same. Both of these shapes are trainable, and both share the same criterion: strengthen what holds the curve and take load off what exaggerates it.

## Two curves in the same spine

Seen from the side, the spine describes a curve at the neck, another in the thoracic area and another in the lumbar area. That shape shares out the load of every step and absorbs impact. The exact shape varies from one person to the next; the problem arrives when one of the curves goes past certain limits. The muscles that hold the structure are covered in the guide to [back anatomy](/en/back-anatomy/).

When one curve goes too far, the body compensates with the next one. That is where the two shapes you see most in a weights room come from: lumbar hyperlordosis and thoracic hyperkyphosis.

No exercise straightens a spine. What training does is strengthen the muscles that hold the curve, reduce pain and improve the way you move. The other conditions, such as scoliosis or a herniation, are not solved by this page.

## Lumbar hyperlordosis: what it is

It is an increase in the lumbar arch. The pelvis tilts forward, the sacrum shifts back and the lower back is left with more curve than it needs to share out weight.

The causes keep repeating: low abdominal tone, short hamstrings pulling on the pelvis, pregnancy and abdominal surgery. The result is pain in the lower back, both at rest and in movement.

## Hyperlordosis: postural habits and training

Postural work aims at the pelvis. When you sit and when you stand, draw the abdomen in to offset the arch. If you spend a long time on your feet, bend the knees slightly and tuck the glutes under. The compensation comes from the pelvis and takes strain off the lumbar arch.

The training criterion follows from that:

- **Abdominal work from the top.** Trunk curls that flex the thoracic spine. Hip curls stay out of the plan.
- **Glutes, abductors and adductors.** A toned pelvis corrects its own position and drags the lower back less.
- **Hamstrings on a seated leg curl, not a lying one.** Seated keeps you from arching the lower back under load.
- **Daily stretching** for hamstrings and hip flexors, the chain that usually pulls the pelvis forward.
- **Nothing that loads the lower back in extension.** Deadlifts with an arched back at the top, sloppy good mornings or rows with the torso almost horizontal add arch exactly where there is too much.

The loaded hip crunch machine deserves its own warning. The psoas starts on the lumbar vertebrae and runs down to the femur, so loading it in hip flexion pulls the lower back forward. If the problem is an excessive arch, that is the last work you need.

## Thoracic hyperkyphosis: what it is

It is an increase in the thoracic curve, the one that gives a rounded back. It can come from birth, from habits held for years, from joint degeneration or from trauma.

It shows up as thoracic and head pain, frequent muscle knots and poor flexibility in the trunk and arms, which complicates movements as basic as reaching overhead. It almost always travels with lumbar hyperlordosis, because the spine compensates one curve with the other.

## Hyperkyphosis: postural habits and training

Day to day, bring the shoulders back to their natural position and keep your gaze forward. If there is hyperlordosis as well, draw the abdomen in too.

Training looks for what opens the thoracic curve and supports the shoulder blade:

- **Middle and lower trapezius, rhomboids and rear delts.** The [barbell row](/en/barbell-row/) is the basic pattern; cable, dumbbell or machine rows work as well.
- **Controlled thoracic extension.** [Back extensions](/en/back-extensions/) done with a short range and no weight train the area without forcing it.
- **Pressing with controlled volume.** You do not have to drop the press: cut the sets and avoid ranges that make you crane the neck or lose the shoulder position.
- **Shoulder and thoracic mobility** before you press, which is when the area is stiffest.
- **Hips and legs.** The hip connects to the trunk, and a strong hip holds the position better.

## What holds for both

- **Load goes up slowly.** The tissue that holds the curve takes months to change, and shortcuts are paid for in the same area.
- **A movement with sharp pain gets changed, not endured.** If it hurts when you push or extend, shorten the range or change the variation.
- **A strong back holds up everything else.** A [strong lower back](/en/strong-lower-back/) carries the squat, the deadlift and almost any row.
- **Everyday posture does the other half.** The [posture guidelines](/en/correct-posture-for-training/) cover what happens outside the gym.
- **If you are coming out of a bad spell**, the [back pain exercises](/en/back-pain-exercises/) are a gentle starting point before you return to heavy loads.
- **Scoliosis, herniation or neurological signs** are another conversation. The guide on [when to see a sports doctor](/en/when-to-see-a-sports-doctor/) covers the signals that do not wait.

Progression does not change either: start with what you can do without pain, raise one variable per week and treat range of motion as one more load. If an exercise forces you to compensate with the lower back, change the position before you add kilos.

The back does not train on its own. If the knee also complains when you bend it, [patellar tendinopathy](/en/patellar-tendinopathy/) has its own page.
