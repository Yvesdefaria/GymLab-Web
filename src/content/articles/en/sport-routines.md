## Why a gym routine does not fit every sport

Strength training is general; transfer is specific. A bench press improves upper-body strength, but it does not teach you to throw, punch or swim. What it does is raise the strength ceiling from which your sport movement works, and that ceiling shows once technique is settled.

The body does not need the same balance in every sport either. A cyclist builds legs and offsets the back; a swimmer shares the work between shoulder and back; a boxer needs hips and a core to rotate. The routine is chosen by what the sport demands and by what the sport does not train.

## Rule 1: the movement pattern

Start with the pattern that repeats in your sport: push, pull, squat, hinge, lunge or rotation. A sport that pushes its arms overhead calls for a vertical press; one that pulls through water calls for a pull; one that runs calls for a hinge and a strong single leg.

Then cover the opposite pattern to compensate. The runner who only runs adds glute and hamstring strength; the cyclist adds back and core; the athlete who punches adds pulling and anti-rotation. That compensation stops the sport from leaving you strong in one direction and fragile in the other.

## Rule 2: the joint angle

Every sport works in a specific range. The swimmer takes the shoulder overhead; the tennis player rotates the trunk with the hips locked; the cyclist flexes the hip for hours. Training the angle the sport uses improves transfer and protects the joint at the point where it is most demanded.

There is no need to copy the movement with weight. Include one exercise that reaches that range and control it: overhead press for the shoulder above the head, Pallof press for anti-rotation, leg curl for the bent knee.

## Rule 3: the season

The same routine is not performed the same way in pre-season and in a competition week. In the base you accumulate volume and build strength; in season you maintain it with one or two sessions; before competing you lower the load and arrive fresh. The [annual training plan](/en/annual-training-plan/) orders those blocks and the guide to [training routine](/en/five-month-training-plan/) explains how to fit the days you have.

If the calendar holds a race every weekend, strength is maintained, not built. Two short sessions and a rest day before the race are enough. With one race a year, the pre-season is the time to add kilos.

The family does not replace the specific sport. A distance runner and a sprinter share a family and do not share a plan. The sprinter needs more maximum strength and power; the distance runner needs strength-endurance and a leg that survives the volume.

## The six published routines

Each one starts from its original material and publishes the session with sets, reps and a progression:

- Tennis and padel: [tennis and padel routine](/en/tennis-padel-training/), three days with squat, clean and incline press, plus wrist and adductor work.
- Football: [football routine](/en/football-strength-routine/), two upper-body sessions and two lower-body sessions, with a defined speed week.
- Boxing: [boxing strength](/en/boxing-strength-training/), two sessions with push, pull, hinge and anti-rotation core.
- Cycling: [cycling strength](/en/cycling-strength-training/), five blocks of 3x15 at a slow tempo.
- Running: [running strength](/en/running-strength-training/), two days of 1 to 3 sets of 10 to 15 reps and the load kept away from the long run.
- Swimming: [swimming strength and diet](/en/swimming-strength-and-diet/), one session of seven groups and the protein that supports volume.

## How to choose and when to change

Choose by season and by the days you have, not by the routine that promises more. If you come from a season without weights, start with two sessions and a basic scheme; the guides to [strength routines](/en/strength-routines/) and [volume routines](/en/volume-routines/) have plans by level. When the plan stalls for two weeks, change the block, not the sport.

A strength plan for a sport is reviewed at the start of each season. What worked in the base does not work the same in race week, and what works for tennis does not work for swimming.

## The protein behind the plan

Strength training needs enough protein to repair and hold muscle mass. A practical reference is 1.5 to 2 g per kilo of bodyweight per day. The guide to [how to gain weight](/en/how-to-gain-weight/) explains how to reach that figure with normal food; this page does not translate grams of protein into grams of chicken because the equivalence depends on the food and on how it is cooked.

## The cardio you already do

Most sports already include aerobic work. Adding 45 to 60 minutes of cardio before or after weights takes energy from the session and from technique. The guide to [aerobic exercise](/en/aerobic-exercise/) explains when to add cardio and when not to.
