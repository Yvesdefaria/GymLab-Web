The movement is also called the lying side jackknife. It is the sideways version of the jackknife: the body closes like a book and opens again. It needs no equipment, and that is part of its appeal and part of its limit.

## One exercise, two movements

The first half is a lateral crunch: the trunk lifts off the floor in the lateral plane, not forward. The second is a straight-leg raise with the body on its side. Both happen together and meet at the top.

That meeting is what sets the exercise apart. A normal side crunch leaves the leg still; here the leg travels too, and the oblique has to brake the trunk while the hip moves. It is more demanding than the first rep suggests.

The obliques do two things: they bend the trunk sideways and they rotate it. This movement stays with the first one. Everything happens in the lateral plane, and that is why it works so well alongside the rotation exercises.

## Starting position

Lie on your side with the body extended and a slight bend in the hip and knee. Stack one foot on top of the other. The head rests on the lower hand, with the elbow in line with the trunk.

The free hand goes on the top hip. That hand does not push or help: it only marks the position and reminds you that the hip must not roll towards your back.

The elbow of the arm supporting the head stays in line with the trunk, not ahead of it. If the elbow drifts forward, the neck flexes and the work starts to spread the wrong way.

## Execution

Crunch the trunk in the lateral plane and rise as far as you can. The head stays aligned with the spine and the hand under it does not pull.

At the same time, raise the straight leg that is not touching the floor. Lift as high as the side of your body allows, without chasing an impossible angle.

At the top, both ends reach: the shoulder towards the hip and the leg towards the trunk. Hold for a second and lower under control instead of dropping.

## Load: ankle weights and short rests

With bodyweight alone the exercise runs out of room early. The most direct progression is a pair of ankle weights or weighted shoes: they add kilos to the leg, which is the long end of the movement, and they change the exercise more than they look like they would.

The second route is rest. In a bodyweight exercise it is common to shorten the pauses between sets so the oblique is not fully recovered before the next one. The original article calls it the short-rest trick and it works: less time between sets, same technique.

You can combine both progressions, but not at the same time. Add ankle weights first and check that the range stays complete; then cut the rest.

## When the set ends

The set ends when the trunk stops rising or when the leg loses its straight line. It also ends when the body starts rolling onto its back: at that point the exercise has changed and it is time to stop.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling on the head with the supporting hand.
- Rolling onto your back and turning the movement into a normal crunch.
- Swinging the leg to gain height.
- Rotating the hip backwards to lift the trunk higher.
- Shortening the range once fatigue arrives.

## Where it fits

This is a bodyweight exercise for the end of a session, when the goal is accumulating work without external load. It also works as a finisher after a heavier oblique movement, because it changes the angle and demands stability.

If you want the same pattern without rotation, this is the most direct of the three. The [side crunch](/en/side-crunch/) shortens the range: the legs barely leave the floor.
