The bench's advantage is the angle. Changing the angle of the backrest adjusts the difficulty without touching the weight, which lets you fit the set to the day without plates or ankle weights. The drawback is that the support makes compensating easy, so technique matters more than it does on the floor.

## What a reverse crunch is

It is the bench version of the floor reverse crunch. The pelvis curls towards the abdomen and the knees travel to the chest while the lower back stays on the backrest.

The active movement is trunk flexion from the bottom: the rectus abdominis brings the pelvis towards the rib cage and the obliques follow the turn. The hips move, but the abs are in charge.

## The angle sets the difficulty

The bench adjusts through several positions. The higher the head end, the harder the set, because gravity works against the pelvis instead of helping it.

Angle is the easiest lever in this exercise, and it is worth noting which position each set was done in.

## Starting position

- Pick an angle you can hold with the pelvis under control on every rep.
- Grip the top edges of the bench with the hands, arms almost straight.
- Stabilise the body in that position before moving the legs.
- Lift the feet off the bench and bend the knees to about 90 degrees.
- Keep the feet together and breathe in.

## Execution

With a smooth movement, contract the abs to bring the knees towards the chest. The pelvis curls and lifts off the pad; that is the range that counts.

The lower back does not arch or leave the backrest more than the crunch itself demands. If the lower back lifts before the pelvis, the movement has become a swing.

Breathe out at the top, at the point of maximum contraction, and lower slowly. The knees stay bent at 90 degrees for the whole set; the knee angle does not change, the pelvis position does.

## Common errors

- Swinging the body up. Leg momentum replaces the abs and the bench moves.
- Moving only the knees and leaving the pelvis on the pad. Without lifting the pelvis, the range stays in the hip.
- Doing half the range to avoid the hard part. The full contraction is what trains.
- Gripping the edges with bent elbows and pulling with the arms to help.
- Letting the air out at the start and arriving at the top with nothing to exhale.

## Sets and progression

Three sets of 10 to 15 reps is a starting point. When twelve clean reps come out, move the bench up one notch and start again at that step.

The next step is weight at the ankles: one or two kilos are enough to feel the change. Then the same scheme: twelve clean reps, more weight.

The bench also allows a [drop set](/en/drop-sets/) without moving plates: at [muscular failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/), drop the angle one notch and keep going with whatever reps are left. The reduction is immediate and the set continues with the same technique.

## The floor version

If the bench is not available or the easiest angle still costs, go back to the floor. Lying face up, hands on the floor or holding a low support, bring the knees to the chest and lift the pelvis just enough.

The floor removes the angle and forces you to control the range without help. It is the natural regression before returning to the bench, and also the version for the first weeks.

## Where it fits

It is a flexion exercise for the end of an abs session, after the loaded movements and the isometrics. It works well as a second or third exercise, when the abs are warm and the range is easier to control.

The [decline bench crunch](/en/decline-bench-crunches/) is the relative with the feet anchored and the torso working the other way; comparing the two shows where the work lands.
