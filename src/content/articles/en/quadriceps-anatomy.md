The quadriceps forms the front of the thigh and is the group that holds the body upright. The name sums it up: four muscle bellies that end in one tendon and reach the knee.

## The four quadriceps muscles

**Rectus femoris.** It is the only one crossing two joints. It starts at the anterior inferior iliac spine and the upper margin of the acetabulum, in the pelvis, and runs down the middle of the thigh.

**Vastus medialis.** It sits on the inner side of the thigh, forming the teardrop shape above the knee. It starts at the linea aspera of the femur and the intertrochanteric line.

**Vastus lateralis.** It covers the outer side of the thigh, the most visible one in a developed physique. It starts at the greater trochanter, the intertrochanteric line and the lateral lip of the linea aspera.

**Vastus intermedius.** It lies deep, between the medial and lateral vastus, in front of the femur. It starts on the front and lateral surface of the bone and is barely visible from outside.

All four run down and converge into the quadriceps tendon, which wraps the patella and continues as the patellar tendon to the tibial tuberosity.

## The rectus femoris and the hip

The rectus femoris does two jobs at once. It extends the knee, like the three vastus muscles, and it also flexes the hip, because it starts on the pelvis rather than on the femur.

That double function has a practical consequence: in a seated leg extension the hip is flexed and the knee extends, so the rectus femoris shortens at both ends. In a squat the knee also bends on the way down and the muscle stretches at that end.

## What the group does

The main action is knee extension: moving the leg from bent to straight. That happens when you stand up from a chair, climb stairs, run and jump.

The quadriceps also brakes the knee when the foot lands, because it controls flexion during the support phase. A strong, stable group protects the joint in that braking moment, which is where it takes the most load.

## How the work is shared between heads

Stance width and foot position change the emphasis slightly and change which muscles share the work: a wide stance brings in the adductors, and feet placed high on the leg press load the hamstrings and glutes more.

What they do not do is isolate one head of the quadriceps. All four converge on a single tendon, so any exercise that extends the knee recruits them together. The [quadriceps training myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) guide develops this point and explains why turning the toes out does not shift the work as much as it is claimed.

## What posture does change

Changing your feet has real effects, though more modest than they are usually sold as. Toes turned slightly out can feel more comfortable if the hip asks for it. A wider stance recruits the adductors more and changes the hip angle. A very narrow position raises the control demand at the knee.

The sensible choice is the one that lets you descend with the knee tracking over the foot and stand up without it caving inward. The [squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) guide insists on that point: the knee travels forward, follows the line of the foot and does not collapse.

## How to train it

Since the group shares one tendon, the way to train it is to cover the pattern with exercises that load the knee through its range. The squat, the leg press and the leg extension are the three usual pieces, and each has its advantage.

## Names and corrections

The vastus medialis is the one on the inner side of the thigh, and the vastus lateralis the one on the outer side. Calling them inner and outer vastus mixes position with name and confuses any technical conversation.

It is also worth not exaggerating the group's size. The quadriceps is large and powerful, and its role in walking and running is central, but it does not make up half the body's muscle mass and it is not the largest structure in anatomy. It is four muscles of one thigh, and that is enough reason to train them with judgement.
