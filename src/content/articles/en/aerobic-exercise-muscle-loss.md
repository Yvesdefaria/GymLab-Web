The deeper question is this: to improve your cardiovascular fitness or lose weight, do you need strength training or weights? The answer is clearly yes. Pairing aerobic exercise with toning or muscle-building work is well worth it.

## Aerobic exercise and muscle loss: why you need strength

People who start an aerobic exercise programme sometimes miss their goals, burn out and quit. In those cases the problem is not a lack of cardiovascular adaptation but muscular fatigue. When you run, ride a bike or take a step class, your muscles contract continuously, so strength work is already there. Better strength levels beforehand ensure better adaptation and more success in those early stages.

An active muscle mass also carries a high metabolic cost, which translates into greater calorie expenditure, because muscle is a big calorie burner. If you are overweight or simply want to lose body fat, adding a muscle-toning plan to aerobic exercise is a good option.

## How to combine aerobic exercise and weights

If you combine both qualities, do your best to perform strength training **before** aerobic exercise. That order offers the most advantages.

In strength training, the body gets energy almost exclusively from breaking down muscle glycogen stores, meaning stored sugar. In the aerobic exercise that follows, those stores are partly depleted and the body reaches fat deposits faster to obtain energy.

If you do it the other way round, cardio first and then strength, glycogen stores are broken down by the aerobic work with barely any involvement of fat. Fatigue then shows up in the weight session for lack of energy supply, because intense muscle contractions cannot run on energy from fat. By contrast, you can always do aerobic exercise, even when tired.

That closing cardio also has a regenerative function and helps muscle recovery: the increase in blood flow after effort carries away waste substances and delivers nutrients.

## Burning fat with the weights and cardio combination

The lipolytic or fat-burning hormones are catecholamines and growth hormone. Catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline) play a significant role in breaking down fat stores and are released when the sympathetic-adrenal system is stimulated. You have several ways to stimulate that system:

- Drink an energy or stimulant beverage.
- Exercise for more than 30 or 40 minutes.
- Stimulate the nervous system with high-intensity work.

Strength training triggers that mechanism: sympathetic-adrenal stimulation occurs and the hormonal flow is far more appropriate when you then apply aerobic exercise. Catecholamines are already acting, fat cell breakdown has started and fatty acids are in the blood, ready to be used as fuel in the oxidation process.

## Weights first, aerobic exercise second

If you start with cardiovascular training, you finish with muscular fatigue and nervous system fatigue. That feeling limits muscle contractions, intermuscular coordination and motor unit synchronisation, and strength levels are not enough to produce a sufficient stimulus. All you get is more fatigue and slower recovery.

If you start with strength, you can always finish the cardiovascular work. And, as already noted, reactivating blood flow produces that muscle-cleaning effect which supports recovery.

## The fear of losing muscle: when you really burn muscle

That fear is neither true nor false. It depends on how you do cardio: intensity, frequency, duration and type of activity. If you control those parameters, cardio should not be a problem for building muscle; it is more of a help when you want to define it.

Cardio is predominantly aerobic, so by nature it burns mostly fat and carbohydrates. Protein amino acids are not exempt from being burned for energy either, although the proportion is minimal, especially when the session does not exceed 40 minutes.

> Muscle only gives up amino acids during cardio when it sees that the other fuels are running out.

It works like a kind of self-cannibalism: the body sees that energy is gone and uses its own muscle structure to keep moving. Muscle is used for energy mainly when carbohydrate and fat stores or intake run low, which takes a fair amount of cardio time before muscle loss becomes significant.

If you do cardio a couple of days a week, sessions last no more than an hour and intensity is moderate, the amount of muscle you burn is probably negligible. Not every metabolism is the same and personal situations differ: some people tend to burn amino acids sooner because of their metabolic traits, but they still need a good stretch of cardio to do it noticeably.

The picture changes if you go to the gym at night, have eaten nothing since midday and have had a busy afternoon. In that situation your available carbohydrate and lipid stores are likely on the floor, and if you stretch the cardio out, you will burn more amino acids.

In short, do not fear cardio when you want to build muscle. It helps you clear fat out of the way and makes your muscles more visible. Just pay attention to when and how you do it.
