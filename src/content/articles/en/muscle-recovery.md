**Muscle recovery** is the part of training that turns effort into size and strength. You can plan every rep and guard your diet, but without rest the body does not repair what you broke.

Recovery brings together rest, repair and rejuvenation. Many people focus on lifting technique, scientific programmes and disciplined eating, and give it little importance. After hard sessions you have to recover well to gain size and strength, and to arrive at your next gym visit willing and able.

## The recovery diet

Without the right amount of macronutrients you cannot fuel the metabolic processes that support recovery. A meal high in low-glycaemic carbohydrates and protein before the session is vital, as is the high-protein, moderate high-glycaemic carbohydrate meal after it.

To make sure you get enough protein for repair, eat at least 2 grams per kilo of body weight per day. And review your nutrition often: adjusting the diet is part of the work.

## Cool-down after the session

The cool-down happens when the session is over and you are still in the gym. Programmes with high reps and long time under load encourage the buildup of metabolic waste, such as lactic acid and hydrogen ions, that has to be cleared. That is why a slow cool-down matters.

An effective cool-down is walking on a flat treadmill at a reasonable pace, between 4 and 6 kilometres per hour, for 5 or 10 minutes while moving your arms in a controlled way so blood flows to both arms and legs.

Moderate aerobic exercise, about 20 or 30 minutes at a medium pace after training, also helps clear those substances. Just do not do too much and compromise muscle gain.

## Stretching

After the cool-down you can stretch for 10 or 20 minutes. Include static flexibility work for the most important muscles you used.

One method: move through the full range of motion, hold the position for about 30 seconds and release the tension little by little. Repeat two or three times per muscle group.

## Heat and cold

The hot and cold technique is common among athletes in many sports and countries. You move from a hot environment to a cold one. It improves circulation, helps clear waste and increases the nutrients reaching the muscles, which supports repair and reduces pain and fatigue.

Ideally you have two small baths or pools, as in some gyms with a spa area. One with water between 4 and 12 °C and another between 35 and 43 °C. First you get into the hot water, then the cold. Times can vary: two minutes hot and 30 seconds cold is enough, or three minutes hot and one cold. If you do not have two baths, use the shower with the same periods.

## Ice baths

You need a bath full of water and plenty of ice, like the ones some gyms have. They are used less than hot and cold contrast for two reasons: filling the bath takes time, and the first minutes are very hard, with discipline needed to stay in for 10 to 20 minutes.

An ice bath has the same effect as an ice pack on a damaged area. The cold narrows the blood vessels to reduce muscle inflammation and other knocks from training.

## Massage

Massage matters a great deal in recovery. There are many types: medical massage, manipulative therapy, shiatsu, deep tissue and others. If a professional therapist performs it, every discipline plays a part in recovery, though they need to know the demands of weight training and the pain it causes.

Massage relaxes the muscles you worked because it encourages blood flow. That activation helps clear waste and brings nutrients to the tissue.

## Rest and sleep

Rest is the least considered factor. The body needs time to recover from any tension. That means sleep, which should last at least eight hours, and also deep sleep. Relaxation during the day adds to it.

More and more people practise yoga and meditation. Any system that relaxes you helps recovery: you gain strength and size and train more intensely.

## In short

Recovery is a process with many steps and all of them matter. Recovery increases energy and the muscle's capacity to respond to exercise, and it prevents injuries. If you want to reach your goals, be disciplined about it.

> **Note:** some of these techniques are standard practice for elite athletes and unknown to many. Consult a doctor before adding any complementary activity to your programme.
