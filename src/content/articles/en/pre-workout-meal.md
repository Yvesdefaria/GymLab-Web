The question comes up every week: what to eat before training and whether it is needed at all. The short answer is that a well-placed pre-workout meal improves the session because energy reaches the muscle and the stomach stays calm. The long answer depends on the hour, the size of the portion and what each person tolerates.

## How long before eating

A full meal needs between one and three hours to leave the stomach. Three hours out, a normal plate with protein, carbohydrate and vegetables fits. One hour out, a small portion fits; half an hour out, only a light, easy-to-digest intake works.

The exact margin varies with the person and the food. A large, fatty meal takes longer; yogurt with fruit leaves sooner. The practical reference is to start at one and a half to two hours and correct according to how training feels.

When the session is very early, the schedule compresses: the pre-workout meal moves to the night before and the morning intake shrinks to something easy to digest.

## How much carbohydrate and protein

The useful range is 20 to 40 grams of carbohydrate and 20 to 30 grams of protein. That combination fills the glycogen the session will burn and supplies amino acids without loading the stomach. When the meal is far out, the portion can rise; when it is close, stay at the low end.

Fat and fibre drop as the session approaches. A plate with plenty of oil or a lot of legumes empties slowly and can cause heaviness. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) explains why this portion matters more in long, high-intensity sessions.

When the session runs past ninety minutes, the pre-workout meal falls short and a carbohydrate drink during training helps. For a one-hour weights session, the portion before and the one after are enough.

## Fibre slows gastric emptying

Fibre delays the passage of food into the intestine, and that changes the calculation depending on the distance to the session. Three hours out, a portion of vegetables and whole grains goes down without trouble. Thirty minutes out, the same portion sits in the stomach and complains.

That is why a close pre-workout meal leans on refined, fast-digesting foods: white bread, rice, banana, honey or a carbohydrate drink. There is no need to remove them from the rest of the day; they are simply kept for that slot.

Liquid options solve busy days. A shake with milk, fruit and protein powder goes down fast and delivers what a small solid portion does, as the ideas in [shakes to improve your physique](/en/pre-and-post-workout-shakes/) recall. What does not help is trying a new recipe on the day of the hardest session.

## Training fasted

Training without eating is a valid option for short sessions at moderate intensity, especially for people already used to it. In long or very demanding sessions, performance tends to drop and perceived effort rises.

If you choose fasting, the protein afterwards and the daily total remain the priority. Covering the figures matters more than the pre-workout meal itself. The guide to [meal timing around training](/en/meal-timing-around-training/) shows how fasting fits without breaking the rest of the plan.

## What to avoid

A pre-workout meal does not speed up the calories the session burns; its job is to arrive with energy and no digestive trouble. Very fatty meals, heavy sauces and alcohol before training work against that.

Eating a huge amount at the last minute to make up for a weak day does not help either. If you reach the session hungry, the fix is in the previous meal, not in a five-minute binge. The guide to the [post-workout meal](/en/post-workout-meal/) covers the next intake, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) prevent spending on what is not needed.

## Adjusting it to your stomach

Try the same pre-workout meal for two weeks and note how you feel in the session. If there is heaviness, move the meal earlier or cut the fat. If energy is missing, raise the carbohydrate portion or add a light intake half an hour out. The guide to [bodybuilding nutrition](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-basics/) keeps the daily total in place while you make those adjustments.
