I have seen many men with impressive arms, chest and shoulders who, when they turn around, have nothing on their back. My opinion of that physique changes completely: the athlete only cares about the muscles he can see in the mirror.

Training chest, shoulders and arms is not as hard as the demanding back exercises: deadlifts, weighted pull-ups and barbell rows. Even so, a standout back is worth the effort.

If you ignore the back or, so to speak, turn your back on the lats, traps and erectors, you lose a huge amount of **mass**: it is the largest muscle group and you condemn yourself to showing only half your torso.

Nobody starts from zero and gets the back of their favourite athlete; it usually takes years. In this article you will learn to train it properly so you can stand out even among regular gym-goers.

> **Note:** every tip here assumes your main goal is to build your back.

## Genetics and your back: what you can and cannot change

**Genetics** influence how your back looks. If you have wide clavicles and a narrow waist, that V shape, the mass you gain will look more impressive: your bone structure already provides width.

Another factor is **lat insertion**. The latissimus dorsi starts between the waist and the middle of the back. Bodybuilders such as Lee Haney, Dorian Yates, Ronnie Coleman and Franco Columbu had very low insertions, which let them build muscle over a larger area.

Wide clavicles, a narrow waist and long lats are the recipe for an impressive back. If you are in the vast majority without that combination, do not despair: with continuous effort over time you can build a good back.

## How to design an effective back routine

More than laziness, it is **ignorance** that stops many people from following an effective back routine.

They do a few sets of pulldowns and cable rows because they do not understand the complexity of the group or what it needs to grow. The largest muscles are the lats, and training them requires **vertical** and **horizontal** pulls.

These exercises also train the small muscles of the upper back and shoulder blades, such as the [rhomboids](/en/back-anatomy/) and the teres major and minor.

**Vertical movements**: they bring the weight down to the waist from overhead, such as pull-ups and pulldowns. Beginners should do only one; advanced lifters, two, preferably with different grips for a different stimulus.

**Horizontal movements**: they bring the weight from the front to the navel. Examples: seated cable or machine rows with an upright torso, and bent-over rows with a barbell, dumbbell or T-bar, even if the line looks more vertical. Beginners should do no more than one; advanced lifters, two.

**Deadlifts**: a compound exercise that works the whole back and was key to gaining mass and strength for men such as Columbu or Jackson. Here, trap and lower-back training is added to back training, although they can also be trained with other muscle groups.

Deadlifts are especially demanding: even advanced lifters do them every two or three sessions. If you squat one week, which punishes the legs, hips and lower back, it is too much to ask the body to deadlift as well.

Complete back sessions are as long and demanding as leg sessions; that is why some people prefer to train traps and lower back on another day. If you train your back well, you will have no time or energy left for anything else. Organise your programme accordingly.

## Which day to schedule your back session

Back training is very **hard**, so the weekly split must let the area recover. You will make a mistake if you train biceps the day before or after back, if the back is your main goal: you need them for pulling. The rear **delts** and **traps** should also go on another day, because the traps take part in overhead presses.

Back training drains the **nervous system** and your energy, so there should be no more than one session per week, unless you follow a specialisation routine. In general, two sessions will lead to overtraining.

## Mind-muscle connection: how to feel your lats

Some muscle groups grow when you feel them working. You do not need to squeeze and focus on the quads during a 225-kilo squat: it is obvious they are working. Deadlifts are similar. In rows, pull-ups and pulldowns, however, the biceps and rear delts easily take over.

One of the most common mistakes is **cheating**. I have seen many people move enormous weights because they use momentum, but their backs get no tension and never grow. Many bodybuilders can complete a 140-kilo barbell row by bouncing the weight to touch their abs, but few can lift it slowly, squeeze the shoulder blades and contract the lats for a second. Slow movement builds a more developed back. If you cannot feel your lats working, you are not training them well: reduce the resistance, learn the execution and build the weight back up gradually.

There are two methods to improve the connection: **stretching** and **poses**. I recommend stretching the lats after every set of every exercise. One way is to hold a bar or a machine and stretch to one side, then the other. Poses work too: hit a lat spread and a back double biceps several times between sets and at home, and you will feel the lats contract. Hold each pose for at least several seconds.

## Free weights or machines?

The most effective exercises for back hypertrophy are the basic free weights: deadlifts, barbell or dumbbell rows, pull-ups, shrugs and barbell good mornings. You can also include cables or machines to vary the stimulus and train the back from angles that are almost impossible with free weights.

Many **Hammer Strength** back machines are designed to pull from special angles, some halfway between the vertical and horizontal planes. Machines also support the torso more, so people with lower-back injuries can work with fairly heavy loads.

Some movements, such as pulldowns, are easier on a machine than with free weights. The best back sessions combine both types to stimulate every muscle.
