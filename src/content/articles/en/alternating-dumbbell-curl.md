Almost everyone starts here and almost nobody does it well. The alternating curl has three pieces you can check on every rep: the still elbow, the turning palm and the free arm that returns to hanging. All three are easier to see here than in any other curl.

## The position

Stand with the feet at hip width and the knees slightly bent. One dumbbell in each hand, palms facing each other, arms hanging. The elbows stay at the sides.

Start with one arm. The dumbbell rises while the palm turns up, the elbow flexes and the forearm ends near the shoulder. Squeeze for a second at the top and lower under control to full extension. The other arm repeats the path before the first one starts again.

## Why it alternates

The waiting arm does not guarantee the deltoid stays out: when the elbow drifts forward, the shoulder pushes just as it does in a two-arm curl. What alternation does is leave one rep of space to return to the start, and that return is what exposes the mistake. If the torso swings to get the weight moving, it shows on every switch of arms.

Alternation does not shorten the elbow's range either. Pinning the elbow to the side changes the angle of the humerus; the range of the joint stays the same, and the elbow still flexes and extends the same amount. The difference is where the arm sits relative to the torso.

What the alternating curl does offer is a longer set with the same load. Both hands work, one at a time, and one side's rest is not enough to recover it fully. It is a comfortable way to add reps without adding weight.

## Supination is the second job

The biceps flexes the elbow and supinates the forearm: it turns the palm up. The alternating curl uses both functions in the same rep, and that is its advantage over the hammer curl, which keeps the palm neutral.

The palm starts facing the body and ends facing up. The forearm rotates and the hand follows; the wrist stays in line. If the wrist bends to finish the rep, the work escapes into the forearm. [Wrist position](/en/wrist-position-biceps-curl/) marks the difference between a biceps set and a grip set.

[Grip types](/en/grip-types/) order the options: supinated for the biceps, neutral for the brachialis and pronated for the brachioradialis. The alternating curl is the one that teaches you to move from one to another within the same movement.

## The mistakes

- **Swinging the torso.** Momentum moves the dumbbell, and it also moves the work out of the biceps.
- **Letting the elbow drift.** Once the elbow leaves the side, the front delt starts pushing.
- **Not turning the palm.** Without supination, the alternating curl looks like a hammer curl with more weight than needed.
- **Bending the wrist at the top.** The hand breaks and the set ends in the forearm.
- **Alternating too fast.** If the lowering arm does not return to full extension, half the range is lost.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The number is a recommendation from this page; the original does not give one. Add weight when both hands complete 12 reps with the palm turned and the elbow still.

It is a good first exercise for a beginner and a good finisher for someone with strength. With light weight, technique holds; with heavy weight, the body finds the swing before the biceps does.

## Where it fits

The [seated dumbbell curl](/en/seated-dumbbell-curl/) is the version that removes leg drive and forces you to curl what is actually there. The [barbell curl](/en/barbell-curl/) remains the reference position and the exercise that moves the most weight with both hands.

The [anatomy of the biceps and forearm](/en/biceps-forearm-anatomy/) explains the biceps' second function and why supination belongs to the technique. The guide to [bigger biceps](/en/bigger-biceps/) places the alternating curl in the shape phase, when the goal is work per rep with a moderate load.
