Vitamin D behaves more like a hormone than a vitamin. The skin makes it with ultraviolet B radiation and food adds a small fraction. That double route explains how a decent diet can coexist with low levels in February, and why summer sun is not a luxury.

## What vitamin D is and what it does

There are two forms. D3, or cholecalciferol, is made by the skin and appears in fish and egg yolk. D2, or ergocalciferol, comes from plants and fortified foods. Both reach the liver in an inactive form, where they become 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the marker a blood test measures. The kidney takes the last step and builds the active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D.

That active form opens calcium and phosphorus absorption in the gut and takes part in bone mineralisation. Its known job ends there: having it above the reference builds no extra bone and no extra muscle.

## How much vitamin D you need

The reference for adults is 600 international units (IU) a day, which is 15 micrograms. From age 70 it rises to 800 IU (20 micrograms), because skin makes less from the same exposure. The tolerable upper limit is 4,000 IU (100 micrograms) a day: above that, the excess accumulates and can raise blood calcium.

The guide to [vitamins and minerals for the gym](/en/vitamins-and-minerals-for-the-gym/) collects the other references and explains why megadoses are not harmless.

Covering 600 IU from food alone asks for oily fish almost daily or several fortified products. In practice, summer sun covers most of it and food holds the rest of the year.

## Sunlight: how much, with what limit

Skin produces D3 when ultraviolet B radiation arrives at a high enough angle. In spring and summer, fifteen to twenty minutes on the face and arms, two or three times a week, is enough for most light skin. More time does not make more vitamin: production saturates and what grows is cumulative damage.

Sunscreen reduces synthesis on the covered area, and it is still worth using, because the real risk is the burn. In winter, above 35 degrees of latitude, the sun angle stays too low for months and skin makes little or nothing. Clothing, skin tone and age change the arithmetic.

## Who ends up with low levels

Working indoors, going out little, wearing clothes that cover arms and legs, or living through a long winter all reduce production. Dark skin at high latitudes synthesises less from the same exposure. After 70 the skin thins and makes less. Obesity, coeliac disease and bariatric surgery complicate absorption, and some seizure medicines speed up the metabolism of the vitamin.

A deficit does not show up day to day. There is no clear symptom until bone has been losing mineral for a while, which is why it is found with a test, not a hunch.

## The test that settles it

The test is 25-hydroxyvitamin D in blood. Many labs mark 20 nanograms per millilitre (50 nanomoles per litre) as the sufficiency threshold, and the doctor makes the final call. Measuring makes sense with risk factors or a diet that does not cover the nutrient; repeating it without a reason adds nothing.

Interest in the immune system drove widespread supplementation. In well-nourished adults, trials have not turned this nutrient into a preventive treatment, and [vitamin D does not replace the defences](/en/immune-system-vitamins/) a varied diet provides.

## Supplement: how much and when

When sun and food fall short, a supplement covers the gap with 600 or 800 IU a day. D3 raises the marker slightly better than D2 and is absorbed with a meal that contains fat. The 4,000 IU limit still applies: a weekly dose of 50,000 IU is a medical prescription, not an over-the-counter purchase.

Before adding jars, it pays to sort out the rest of the plan. The guide on [how to start taking sports supplements](/en/how-to-start-taking-supplements/) sets out what makes sense and in what order, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) prevent purchases that correct nothing.

## What vitamin D does not do

Trials in healthy adults with sufficient levels found no benefit for cancer, mood or muscle strength. Vitamin D supports bone and calcium metabolism; the rest is advertising.

[Vitamin B6](/en/vitamin-b6/) takes part in protein metabolism and [vitamin B3](/en/vitamin-b3/) in the reactions that power the cell. Neither one compensates for a disordered diet or replaces training.
