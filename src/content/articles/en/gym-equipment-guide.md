The range of gym equipment grows every day. The variety adds to training, and each piece opens up different ways to execute an exercise.

## Free weights and machines: the two kinds of gym equipment

Bodybuilding equipment exists to move weight in a suitable way. The kit is divided by the degree of freedom of movement it allows: with free weights, the path and balance depend on you; with a machine, the trajectory is guided and the effort goes into pushing or pulling.

## The straight bar: sizes, advantages and limits

The bar forces the simultaneous, synergistic work of the symmetrical muscle groups involved in an exercise.

When it is used for biceps, both arms have to flex at the same time. When it is used for chest, both sides of the pectoral press at once while both arms extend in coordination. When it is loaded on the shoulders, as in the squat, both legs work in unison.

The bar is rarely used to work symmetrical groups independently. When it is, as in the one-arm barbell row, it looks out of context and forces the nature of the exercise.

On one hand, the bar takes away freedom of action. It forces fixed wrist positions, allows only the pronated and supinated grip, and often limits the range because it hits the lifter's own body: in the bench press, the downward arc ends where the bar meets the chest; in the seated barbell curl, when the bar hits the legs; in the behind-the-neck press, when it hits the base of the neck.

On the other hand, by forcing the coordinated work of a whole muscle chain, the bar brings more balance and power to the execution. Almost every great bodybuilding exercise owes something to it.

In that coordinated work, the stronger limb assists the weaker one when there is a marked asymmetry between the two. That can slightly reduce the stronger limb's performance in later exercises, which shows when symmetrical muscles are worked independently.

If, after a Scott barbell curl, the right arm reaches fewer reps than the left in a seated dumbbell curl, it put in more effort in the previous exercise.

Straight bars come in several versions and sizes: from the smallest bar, 1.20 m and 6 kg, to the big Olympic bars of more than 2 m and around 25 kg. The bars usually found in gyms come in three sizes: 1.20, 1.50 and 1.80 m.

The smallest size is ideal for medium and close-grip triceps work because it makes the movement easier to balance, and balance is more precarious with long bars. It is also very useful for forearm work and close-grip biceps. The medium size responds well in common biceps exercises with a medium or wide grip. The largest size is essential in every chest and shoulder press, and in the big power exercises such as the deadlift and the squat, which demand a heavy load.

In forearm work, the bar imposes a precise direction on the wrist movement and makes flexion and extension exercises clean. In biceps work, it gives the exercises hardness, but dumbbells, with their freedom of rotation and longer range, are what give them quality. In shoulder work, it brings balance to every lift above the head.

For the lower back, the bar is practically mandatory, because resistance to trunk flexion has to be spread evenly across the shoulders. In the middle and upper back it is a lesser tool. The characteristic movements for the lats are arm adduction and retropulsion, and for the bar to offer suitable resistance the torso has to be parallel to the floor. That position is unstable and invites lumbar injury, especially with the heavy weight the back muscles demand.

For the legs, the bar is subsidiary too: it is essential only in the many versions of the squat, an exercise widely debated because of the brutal compression it puts on the spine. For the chest, by contrast, the bar is queen, because heavy presses are essential for pectoral development.

## The EZ bar: what it is for and when to use it

The EZ bar is an alternative to the straight bar designed to free the wrists from the tension they suffer in biceps and triceps work because of the forced position. Its angles allow a semi-supinated and semi-pronated grip, so the forearm does not have to turn all the way up or down and works in a more natural position.

In biceps curls with a semi-pronated grip, the EZ bar favours the brachialis and the brachioradialis. In triceps work, its grip shape makes it easier for the long head to contribute.

However, many EZ bars on the market are failures. On some, the angles are excessive or badly calculated, so in triceps work the weight of the bar bends the wrists outwards.

The EZ bar is practically limited to the arms. Outside them, it can serve as an alternative in barbell shrugs when it is long enough for the elbows not to hit the plates as they open. You grip it right where the inner angles begin, so the wrists suffer less on the pull.

## Dumbbells: rhythms, grips and range of motion

Dumbbells have three main advantages: more freedom in the rhythm of the active muscle groups, more freedom in the grip, and, in general, a longer range of effort. In some exercises, that longer range means a greater flexibility demand for the area you are working.

If the bar forces symmetrical groups to work simultaneously, dumbbells allow that rhythm plus three more: alternate, successive and independent.

- **Alternate:** the pull of one dumbbell is parallel to the lowering of the other. Both arms move at once, both muscle groups are active at the same time, but through opposite arcs.
- **Successive:** the pull of one dumbbell follows the complete rep of the other. When one limb is active, the other rests under some isometric tension. The exercise is slower, but each limb's power is higher thanks to the small recovery between reps, and concentration is more precise and local.
- **Independent:** the complete work of one limb follows the other. Each set done with a simultaneous, alternate or successive rhythm is equivalent to two independent-rhythm sets, with one limb fully active and the other completely passive throughout.

Not every exercise allows every rhythm. Balance or space demands force one option or another. Dumbbell chest presses and flyes require the simultaneous rhythm for balance; the dumbbell row requires an independent rhythm for the same reasons. The concentration biceps curl does not allow two active limbs at once because of posture and space.

In other exercises, even when all rhythms are possible, some are less advisable. In forearm work, the long sets this muscle usually needs, done independently, would slow the routine too much and force excessive rest between sets for what this muscle group needs.

The grip is freer with dumbbells too. You get the extreme positions of the bar and the intermediate positions of the EZ bar, plus every degree of wrist rotation and even some dynamic rotation during the rep, which makes certain exercises easier or richer.

In the Zottman biceps curl, the wrists turn to a supinated position on the pull and to a pronated position on the lowering. In the dumbbell shoulder press there is a variation in which the wrists turn slowly to a pronated position as they rise from their starting point in front of the deltoids, where they are supinated. In chest flyes there is a version with the wrists rotating towards forearm pronation that changes the tension angle on deltoids and pectorals, allows more flexibility in those areas and a longer range of effort.

The longer range is most obvious in biceps, shoulders (presses with the arms raised above the head) and pectorals (press exercises). It produces a greater stretch and forces effort at angles beyond the bar's arc of action.

However, chest and shoulder presses are heavy exercises and symmetrical muscles rarely have identical strength and endurance. With dumbbells, balance is more precarious and there is an extra chance of injury if one arm fails without the other stopping in time.

Beyond the common use, with one dumbbell in each hand, there are two extraordinary uses: when both hands hold the same dumbbell, as in the pullover, behind-the-neck triceps extensions with one dumbbell and front raises for the deltoids; and when the dumbbell is loaded on only one side, as in ulnar and radial forearm flexions or the supination-pronation exercise.

Dumbbells are essential for biceps; they add variety to triceps and trapezius work; they enrich back, chest and shoulder work; they are subsidiary for forearms, abs and legs, and practically dispensable in lower-back routines.

## The pulley: the most versatile piece of equipment

What sets the pulley apart from the rest of the bodybuilding machines, almost all of which also rely on the pulley principle, is that it is a highly versatile work station.

It allows very different attachments: bar, EZ bar, short bar with rotating handles, fixed short bar, single handle, rope, various triceps and lat V bars with different angles, double D bar.

It is useful for any muscle group, but very limited in leg routines. It is especially interesting for triceps and essential for lat development.

Compared with free weights, the pulley changes the quality and direction of the movement. It imposes a specific pull angle and creates, in both the pulling and the lowering arc, a sense of continuity and uniformity by channelling resistance through a cable that runs through the pulley groove, that is, the channel in the wheel or in the fixed wheels on their axles.

Good bodybuilding pulleys have moved towards greater purity of movement: they reduce friction as much as possible and replace the old deformable ropes with inextensible steel cables.

The pulley's main contribution is resistance from above for muscles such as the lats and triceps, which need the downward pull to develop.

Although the pulley greatly enriches triceps work, that muscle has a catalogue of free-weight exercises varied enough to do without it. The lats, by contrast, without the pulley would be reduced to the various forms of row with a bar, dumbbells and T-bar, in which the body is in an awkward position and it is hard to concentrate on the movement.

For forearms, traps, shoulders and biceps, the pulley mostly imitates exercises that generally work better with free weights. Only in the supine lying curl on a flat bench, with resistance acting from above against biceps that the body position lets you concentrate on fully, does the pulley bring an original execution.

For the pectorals it offers a single original exercise, the crossover, traditional in chest routines and ideal for adding quality to this musculature. For legs and abs, the pulley is completely dispensable: the abdominal crunch and the leg pull are minor exercises.

## Machines: where they help and where they fail

Machines have evolved a great deal too. Their variety has grown, their biomechanical design and operation have improved, and electronic features have been added.

In high-tech lines, the old traction systems based on pulleys, pinions and chains have been replaced by hydraulic pressure systems. In general they are expensive, bulky machines, and their results are not always up to the expectations they create.

It is too early to say how far they are a first step towards a futuristic concept of training. Today it is hard to imagine a bodybuilder leaving the epic kit they use for devices that look like small space capsules.

> Machines are essential for leg training.

They offer resistance to knee extension and flexion, to leg pressing at different push angles (from the gently inclined position of the hack squat to the strictly declined posture of some leg press types), to leg retropulsion for glutes and hamstrings, and to ankle extension for the calves.

There is even a machine for the adductor and abductor muscles of the legs: a device that combines bulk, showiness and the most perfect uselessness.

Machines enrich chest training and add variety to triceps work, although the pullover and behind-the-neck extension machines fail in almost every catalogue and are ideal for getting injured.

The natural movement of these two exercises, chest first and triceps second, is too irregular to submit successfully to the rigid structures of machines.

Machine use is limited for shoulders, traps, back and lower back, and practically nil for biceps and abs. The abdominal crunch machine is today, along with the pullover, behind-the-neck extension and adductor-abductor machines, one of the four great fiascos of bodybuilding mechanics.

## In short: which tool to choose

The bar is queen for the chest and practically mandatory for the lower back. The EZ bar frees the wrists in arm work. Dumbbells bring rhythm, grip and range. The pulley is essential for the lats and very useful for the triceps. Machines are essential for the legs, with exceptions worth knowing.
