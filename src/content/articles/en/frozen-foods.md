Fresh vegetables lose quality from the moment they are picked. They travel, wait in a cold store, sit under lights on a shelf and take days to reach the plate. Freezing stops that clock: the product is processed within hours and stored at a temperature that halts almost all enzyme activity.

## The cold chain starts in the field

Industrial freezing is a process with stages. Vegetables are washed, sorted and blanched briefly before the temperature drops. The less time between harvest and freezer, the better the product arrives.

The cold chain continues in transport, in the shop and at home. A product that thaws and is frozen again loses texture, releases water and loses part of its value. That is why the shop should be the last stop and the freezer should sit below minus 18 degrees.

## Blanching protects colour and vitamins

Blanching means dipping the vegetable in hot water for one or two minutes and cooling it afterwards. That step deactivates the enzymes that darken the product and change its flavour during storage.

The nutritional cost of blanching is small compared with what is lost when fresh vegetables wait days on a shelf. The loss concentrates in vitamin C, which is water-soluble, and shrinks when the water heats fast and the cooling does too.

## Frozen versus fresh held for days

The frozen pack blocks light, and that protects sensitive compounds such as carotenoids. In several analyses, frozen spinach has beaten fresh spinach that had spent days on the shelf and in the fridge.

That does not make frozen food always better. A vegetable picked ripe and cooked the same day wins on flavour and texture, and keeps its vitamins. The advantage of frozen appears when the alternative is a product that has already travelled for a week.

Cooking method decides too. Boiling vegetables in plenty of water for a long time leaches water-soluble vitamins; steamed or sautéed, the loss is smaller. That difference matters more than whether the vegetable was frozen.

## There is no three-day rule

The claim that vegetables lose their value three days after purchase is repeated often and is not a law. Broccoli and green beans lose quality fast, while apples, oranges, carrots and grapefruit hold well for weeks.

The useful rule is the state of the product: if the vegetable is firm, coloured and free of soft spots, it is still a good option. Frozen food steps in when fresh produce no longer arrives in good shape or when there is no time to shop every other day.

## Canned food: heat instead of preservatives

A can is sealed and heated to destroy bacteria. Heat and the airtight seal do the work, which is why the can needs no added preservatives. From packing to opening, the product keeps its nutritional value.

What does change is salt and sugar. Many canned vegetables carry added salt, and fruit usually comes in syrup. Choosing no-salt versions or fruit in its own juice solves most of the problem.

## What to check on the label

The label on frozen and canned food reports what was added before the pack was closed. These are the four things that change the result most.

The glaze protects fish from drying out, so it is not a defect. It is worth knowing that part of the weight is water and that the price per real serving rises.

## How to thaw and cook

Frozen vegetables go straight into the pan or the pot, without thawing first. They keep a better texture that way. Fish and meat do need thawing in the fridge, never at room temperature.

A thawed product is not frozen again. Bacteria that may have multiplied during thawing are still there, and a second freeze does not remove them. If food is left over, cool it fast and store it in the fridge.
