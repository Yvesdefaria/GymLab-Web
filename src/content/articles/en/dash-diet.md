The name comes from Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension. It is a way of filling the plate that was tested on real people, which is why it can be explained with figures.

## What the DASH diet is and where it comes from

The trial was published in 1997 in the New England Journal of Medicine. It recruited 459 adults with systolic pressure below 160 mmHg and diastolic pressure between 80 and 95 mmHg, none of them on blood pressure medication. For eight weeks it compared three diets: a typical American one, another rich in fruit and vegetables, and a third that added low-fat dairy and cut saturated fat. Weight and sodium were held constant in all three.

That third pattern is what is now called the DASH diet. It leans on vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, legumes, nuts, fish, poultry and skimmed dairy, and leaves red meat, sweets and salty products in the background.

Sodium does not appear in the table because it is a limit, not a group. 2,300 mg a day is the standard ceiling and 1,500 mg the strict version. The guide to [limiting sodium](/en/how-to-limit-sodium/) explains where it hides.

## What lowered blood pressure in the 1997 trial

Against the control diet, the DASH pattern lowered systolic pressure by 5.5 mmHg and diastolic by 3.0 mmHg on average. The effect was larger in the 133 participants with hypertension: 11.4 and 5.5 mmHg. In the 326 without hypertension, 3.5 and 2.1 mmHg.

These are figures from an eight-week trial where all food was provided, not from a clinic visit. The DASH diet does not replace treatment and it does not respond the same way in everyone: it depends on starting blood pressure, body weight and how much salt you eat. What the trial shows is that the whole pattern matters more than a single nutrient.

Potassium is the other half. The plan supplies between 3,500 and 5,000 mg a day, mostly from vegetables, fruit, legumes and dairy. Raising potassium and lowering sodium at the same time is what moves blood pressure.

The day lands near 2,000 calories, with under 1,500 mg of sodium if no salt is added in cooking and about 30 g of fibre. Protein sits around 105 g. The numbers are an estimate: they change with the cut, the brand and the hand that dresses the salad.

Salmon brings omega-3 fat and olive oil completes the fat for the day. The pattern keeps total fat between 25 and 30 % of calories, with saturated fat below 10 %. A lower version is also possible, and the guide to a [low-fat diet](/en/low-fat-diet/) shows how to go below 30 % without falling short.

## How to start without changing the whole shop

The first week you add a vegetable to lunch and a piece of fruit to breakfast. The second, you swap commercial sauces for spices, garlic and lemon. The third, you read the labels on three products you buy every week and pick the version with less sodium.

Sliced bread, breakfast cereals, cold cuts and packet soups hold most of the sodium that slips in unnoticed. You do not have to throw them out: reduce how often they appear and balance the rest of the day.

## What the DASH diet does not promise

It does not promise weight loss on its own. The pattern is filling because it has fibre, water and protein, and many people eat less without counting, but fat is lost when there is a [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/). If the goal is to change body composition, the guide to [body recomposition](/en/body-recomposition-guide/) explains how the two fit together.

It does not promise results in two weeks and it does not replace medication. And it does not require cutting out food groups: fruit, bread and dairy all fit, just in measured servings. That is the part you can sustain, and the part the trial measured.
