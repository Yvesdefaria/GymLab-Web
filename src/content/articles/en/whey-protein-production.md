Whey is a by-product. For centuries it was waste fed to livestock or poured away, until the dairy industry learned to dry it and sell it as sports protein. That history matters because it dismantles two opposite ideas: that the powder is a chemical invention and that it is milk with a bow on it. It is neither.

## From milk to whey

Milk contains two large protein families: casein, which makes up about 80%, and whey, which makes up the rest. When milk is curdled with rennet or acid, casein forms the curd that becomes cheese. The liquid left behind is whey.

That liquid is almost all water, with protein, lactose and some fat dissolved in it. Turning it into powder means removing the water and separating the components you do not want. That is where the different grades on the shelf come from.

Sweet whey comes from rennet-set cheeses; acid whey comes from casein and from strained yoghurt. The second is harder to filter and is sometimes diverted to other uses before it reaches a protein tub.

## The filtration ladder

Whey concentrate is made by removing water and part of the fat and lactose. It lands around 80% protein and keeps 4 to 8 grams of lactose per serving. It is the most common form and the cheapest.

Isolate goes through finer filtration, with microfiltration or ion exchange, which removes almost all the lactose and leaves protein at 90% or more. Hydrolysate is a different step: the proteins are treated with enzymes to break them into short peptides before drying. It absorbs sooner and costs more.

## Lactose, allergy and intolerance

Lactose is the sugar in milk, and it is not the same thing as the protein. Anyone with lactose intolerance produces little of the enzyme needed to digest it and notices gas or discomfort with concentrate, but may tolerate isolate because it keeps less than 1 gram per serving.

Allergy is different: the immune system reacts to milk protein, and whey is milk protein. No degree of filtration makes it suitable for someone with a milk allergy. Hydrolysate is not suitable either, even though its peptides are smaller.

## What the powder is not

It is a fraction of milk separated by physical processes, and its value lies in convenience and protein concentration, not in an impossible effect. Milk powder with vitamins added is a different product. An honest tub says milk protein and little else.

It is not essential either. Whey protein does the same job as a steak, an egg or a yoghurt: it supplies amino acids. The difference is that it mixes in water, digests quickly and travels without a fridge. It is not a meal replacement, and it does not carry the fibre, vitamins and minerals of the foods it stands in for, so the rest of the diet still has to do that work.

## How to use it without myths

The daily reference for strength is 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight. Whey helps you get there when food falls short, in servings of 25 to 40 grams. You do not have to take it within thirty minutes of training: the daily total and its distribution decide.
