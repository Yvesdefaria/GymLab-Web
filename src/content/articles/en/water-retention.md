Body water makes up about 60 % of an adult's weight. Two thirds sit inside the cells and the rest fills the extracellular space: the blood and the fluid that bathes the tissues. Retention appears when that last compartment grows: the sock leaves a mark and the ankle loses its shape by the end of the day.

## Puffiness or oedema: the difference that matters

Transient puffiness stays in the feet and ankles, gets worse with heat and hours on your feet, and settles overnight. It is the swelling of a long flight or the days before a period.

Oedema is something else. It is there in the morning, climbs up the leg and leaves a mark that takes a while to fade when you press with a finger. That mark is called pitting and it separates a habit problem from one that needs a check.

## Why the legs swell

Gravity rules. Veins return blood to the heart against the slope, helped by valves and by the calf and soleus pump as you walk. Sit or stand for hours and the pump does little work, so fluid collects where pressure is highest.

Sodium is the second factor. A diet heavy in processed food holds water because the kidney adjusts volume to keep salt concentration stable. The guide to the [DASH diet](/en/dash-diet/) explains how to cut sodium without emptying the salt cellar.

The menstrual cycle adds a variable. In the luteal phase, oestradiol and progesterone change sodium and water retention, and many women notice thicker ankles before their period. The guide to [strength training and the menstrual cycle](/en/strength-training-and-menstrual-cycle/) adjusts training to those days.

Some causes do not respond to habits. Kidneys, liver and heart regulate volume and pressure, and when they fail the swelling also shows in the face or in both legs equally. Cortisone and some thyroid conditions do the same. That is diagnosed with blood tests and an examination.

## What actually helps

Walking switches on the calf pump, and a leg session adds the same effect. The guide to [cardio and weights for women](/en/cardio-and-weights-for-women/) splits the two across the week.

Elevation works when it is constant: fifteen or twenty minutes with the legs above the heart, two or three times a day.

Sodium comes down in the kitchen more than in the salt shaker. Cooking, reading labels and cutting ultra-processed food do more than leaving the plate unsalted, because most salt arrives inside the product.

Compression is the best-supported option for mild venous oedema. Pressure is measured in mmHg: 15 to 20 for everyday swelling and 20 to 30 for persistent cases. A professional should measure the leg and choose the size; a random pair presses where it should not.

Drinking water does not remove oedema, but dehydration pushes the body to hold sodium. The guide to [hydration for training](/en/hydration-for-training/) works out what an active person needs.

## What does not help

Parsley, juniper, olive leaf and horsetail appear on many home remedy lists. They are diuretics: they increase urine and with it potassium loss. They do not treat oedema and can upset blood pressure or kidney function if taken daily.

Toxins and drainers have no physiological meaning. The liver and kidneys already filter, and an infusion does not speed that up. Lymphatic massage does not correct oedema or replace compression. Vascular creams do not either.

## Training, water and the cycle

An active person tends to hold more water inside the muscle, as glycogen, and less in the interstitial space. That is why weight rises a little after a lifting day without new fat.

A leg session can leave the legs swollen for a few hours, and the effect goes with the next day's movement. ## How to track it at home

Weigh yourself in the morning, after the bathroom and before breakfast, and compare the week's average rather than a single day. Measure the ankle with a tape at the same point and the same time. If it rises for two weeks with no change in diet or training, mention it to your doctor.
