A standing curl accepts two kinds of help without announcing them: the legs pushing through the last inches and the torso swinging on the first rep. The bench removes both, and with them the doubt about how much of the weight the arm actually lifted.

## The position

A flat bench, with or without a backrest. A dumbbell in each hand and the feet flat on the floor, knees at hip width.

From the bottom, the palms face forward and the arms hang at the sides of the bench with the elbows pinned to the torso. Back straight, chest up.

The grip is supinated from the start. That is the difference from the [alternating curl](/en/alternating-dumbbell-curl/), which begins neutral and turns the palm on the way up: here the hand is already facing up at the bottom and the biceps starts the range with its supinating job done.

## What changes when you sit

Standing, the hips and knees can push through the last inches, and the torso can lean back to start. Neither of those moves the elbow: they add momentum the biceps never has to pay for.

Seated, the hips are supported and the feet cannot push. What lifts the dumbbell is elbow flexion, and the set stops when the elbow stops. That is the real difference, and it has nothing to do with which joints take load: a standing curl does not load the hips or the knees either.

Seated, the exercise becomes measurable, which is what you need to know whether the plan works. It is not a therapeutic variant and does not replace an assessment.

The third row is another exercise, not a variant of this one: the [incline dumbbell curl](/en/incline-dumbbell-curl/) extends the shoulder and stretches the long head before the rep starts. The backrest of a flat bench does not do that, even when it is tilted a little.

## The range and the tempo

At the bottom, the arm extends fully without locking the elbow back. At the top, the dumbbell rises to shoulder height without the elbow moving forward. The lowering takes two or three seconds.

The wrist stays in line with the forearm. If it bends back at the bottom, the lever gets longer and the weight escapes the biceps, which is the last thing a short set needs.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The number is this page's recommendation; the original gives none.

Add weight when all three sets reach 12 reps with the back on the bench and the lowering under control. The weight of the standing curl is no reference: with the momentum removed, the same dumbbell feels heavier.

## The mistakes

- **Using the backrest as a lever.** If the back pushes against it to start, the torso is helping exactly as it did standing.
- **Letting the elbows drift forward.** Once the elbow leaves the torso, the front delt comes in and the biceps loses the end of the range.
- **Pushing with the feet.** Even seated, the legs look for the floor to add momentum.
- **Cutting the descent.** Without reaching extension, the long head never gets to its long position.
- **Picking the standing weight.** The first set ends in a swing and the measurement is lost.

## Where it fits

The seated curl is the reference of a biceps session: the exercise that says how much you can move without help. It goes after a heavy barbell movement and before the variants that add detail.

The [barbell curl](/en/barbell-curl/) is still the position reference and the exercise that takes the most weight with both hands. The [biceps and forearm anatomy](/en/biceps-forearm-anatomy/) explains why supination is part of flexion and not a style detail.

The [biceps routines](/en/biceps-routines/) place the seated curl in the plans that start with a bar, and the guide to [grow the biceps](/en/grow-biceps/) orders the weekly volume when this exercise is the main one. The [incline dumbbell curl](/en/incline-dumbbell-curl/) adds the long head to the same session.
