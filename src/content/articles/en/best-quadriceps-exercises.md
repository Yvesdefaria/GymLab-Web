For years the lower body was bodybuilding's poor relation, and plenty of people still avoid it for one simple reason: training legs hard is more tiring than any other group. The energy cost is high, the position is uncomfortable and the sweat is plentiful.

The result is that quadriceps work comes late, with few sets and no thought. This list orders the exercises that actually build the muscle, with the technique that makes each one worth the time.

## The squat

The squat is the reference for the lower body: the longest range, the greatest transfer to other movements and the one that involves the most muscle. It can be done in three ways, and each has its place.

### The free squat

Bar on the traps, inside a power rack with safety pins. Feet a little wider than the hips, toes slightly out, torso upright and eyes forward. The hips go back without the back rounding.

The knee travels forward, over the line of the foot. That path is normal and necessary; what must not happen is the knee collapsing inward. Descend as far as you can while keeping a neutral spine and the heel down. Parallel is the reference, and lower only if you keep that control; the [squat safety guide](/en/squat-safety/) takes the technique point by point.

If ankle mobility falls short, two or three centimetres under the heels keep the torso upright while you gain range. With a history of hernia, low-back pain or scoliosis, check the exercise with a professional first: the load falls on the lumbar region.

### The Smith machine squat

The bar runs on two rails, so the back stays more upright and the lumbar area takes less load than in the free squat. The hips are not fixed: if you do not stabilise them, they sway and the advantage is lost.

It is a good entry point for someone leaving the beginner period who wants to master the pattern with less risk of losing balance.

### The hack squat

The back rests fully against a pad and the hips are locked back, so the torso does not sway. Of the three, it isolates the quadriceps most, although it is not the one that builds the most mass: the weight it takes is lower than the free squat.

If the platform is large, set the feet far enough forward that the knee does not suffer in the low position. It fits the second half of the beginner period.

## The leg press

The leg press lets you move a lot of weight with the back supported, which makes it useful when the squat is not yet mastered or when the lower back wants a break. It works the same muscles with less demand on balance.

### The 45-degree leg press

It is the most used version and the one that takes the most load. Place the feet a little wider than the hips, with the knees in line with the feet and not collapsing inward at the lowest point.

Lower under control as you pass the right angle between thigh and torso; the bounce at the end of the range is what punishes the knee. It is not the best option with low-back problems, and with knee problems avoid full flexion and full extension.

### The horizontal leg press

On the 180-degree press the weight does not fall on the lifter: the load moves horizontally. That lack of gravity effect makes it safer and turns it into an ideal machine for beginners. The 45-degree version takes more weight, so it wins when the goal is hypertrophy.

## The leg extension

It is the exercise many people start the lower body with, and it keeps a place later on. It isolates the quadriceps almost completely and does not load the spine. The backrest can be tilted slightly back to stretch the rectus femoris, and many machines let you adjust the angle if the knee asks for it.

For an advanced lifter it becomes second choice, because the weight it takes is lower than the press or the squat. It serves to add volume at the end of the session and to train when the back will not tolerate anything else.

## Lunges and the sissy squat

### Lunges

They are one-leg squats: one foot forward, the other back, and a descent until the rear knee almost touches the floor. The back stays upright and the eyes forward.

The split between quadriceps and glutes does not change much compared with the squat, so as a hypertrophy exercise they are second choice. Their value is unilateral work: they even out differences between legs and teach you to stabilise the hip.

### The sissy squat

It is an old-school exercise, now rare. The knee travels forward while the torso leans back, with the hip in extension.

That detail loads the rectus femoris more than other movements, because the muscle works from its origin on the pelvis. The benefits it used to be credited with, such as defining the thigh or strengthening the knee, have no basis. With body weight and control it works as a complement; with excessive weight it punishes the knee.

## How to order these exercises in a session

An order that works: a heavy movement first (squat, press or hack), one or two accessories afterwards (extension, lunges) and unilateral work at the end. The quadriceps tolerates a fair amount of volume, but it arrives at the accessories exhausted if the first exercise takes all the effort.

Progression matters more than selection. If the squat goes up every two weeks and the press stays flat, the problem is in how the work is distributed. The [exercise technique guide](/en/exercise-technique/) marks what to fix before adding plates, and [training volume](/en/training-volume/) sets how many sets make sense.

[Quadriceps anatomy](/en/quadriceps-anatomy/) explains what each head does and why they all converge on the same tendon. If the question is what changes when you move your feet, [leg press foot position](/en/leg-press-foot-position/) goes through the variants and [quadriceps myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) clarify what a stance change can deliver.

None of these exercises is mandatory and none is enough on its own. With two or three chosen well, clear progression and careful technique, the quadriceps grows without an endless list.
