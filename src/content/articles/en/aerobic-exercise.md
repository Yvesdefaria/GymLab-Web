Health improves across the board. The heart gets stronger, circulation improves and lung capacity rises. It also lowers the risk of chronic disease and raises endurance so you face every day with more strength.

The best part is that you can adapt it to your level and your lifestyle. From a quiet walk to demanding workouts, what matters is taking the first step.

## What aerobic exercise is

Aerobic or cardio exercise is any activity that demands a continuous supply of oxygen, so the muscle uses your fat reserves as its main fuel.

If the activity is very intense, the body does not need oxygen to perform it, and that is called anaerobic exercise, which lasts very little for that same reason. Aerobic exercise, by contrast, goes on much longer.

Some examples are aerobics, brisk walking, running, cross country skiing, swimming, long distance cycling, dancing and monitored weight training.

The changes aerobic exercise makes to your metabolism do not stop when the session ends: they last several hours afterwards.

## Benefits of aerobic exercise

With fat as the main fuel, plus a balanced diet and a healthy lifestyle, the benefits are clear.

### Heart and blood vessels

It improves cardiovascular function both in healthy people and in those who have had a heart attack, angina or heart surgery, an angioplasty or heart failure. It also stimulates the formation of new coronary vessels.

### Body fat and definition

It reduces body fat and removes subcutaneous fat in people with excess weight or obesity. To burn a large amount of the calories stored in fat tissue, exercise must be regular, long, moderate in intensity and use the largest possible muscle mass: legs, glutes and lower back.

It also reduces the subcutaneous fat found between muscles. Anyone who wants definition has to practise it, together with a correct diet, so muscles look lean and healthy instead of bulky and clumsy.

### Blood pressure, cholesterol and glucose

In the medium term, it lowers blood pressure in hypertensive people by up to 7 mmHg systolic and 4 mmHg diastolic, which reduces the need for medication.

It lowers total cholesterol, LDL or bad cholesterol and triglycerides, and raises HDL or good cholesterol, which reduces the risk of a heart attack.

It also lowers blood glucose in diabetics: the muscle uses glucose that arrives from the blood, so levels drop and diabetics can benefit from the practice.

### Lungs, bones and mood

It improves lung capacity, general circulation and oxygen use, not only in the muscles (including the heart) but also in internal organs and skin. That shows up as more capacity for effort and better digestive, kidney, immune and endocrine function, mood, sleep and higher mental functions.

It firms tissues and the skin recovers part of its lost freshness, so it helps you be and feel younger, and look it too.

It increases calcium reabsorption by the bones, strengthens them and lowers fracture risk.

It lowers circulating adrenaline, the stress hormone, and raises endorphins and other brain substances. The result is less emotional tension and a better mood, with a sense of physical, emotional and social wellbeing.

## Brain capacity: the extra benefit

On top of the known benefits of running and aerobic activity comes the effect on the brain: exercise encourages an increase in grey matter and has a positive effect on mental agility.

Research has found that after a few days of running, new brain cells are created in an area linked to memory. Running or other aerobic exercise therefore helps memory, learning and other cognitive tasks, and slows the mental decline that comes with age.

Studies link it to increased blood flow and to higher levels of hormones released during exercise.

## How to add aerobic exercise to your training

Keep a balance between burning fat and building muscle, because too much of the first can damage the second. Aerobic work does not prevent gains in strength or muscle, but it competes with the skeletal muscle traits that favour size and strength.

To avoid that, vary your aerobic work across 4 to 6 sessions a week: alternate easy light aerobic sessions, the more traditional cardio, with high performance interval work.

HIIT, the alternation of sprints and slow recovery paces, relies mainly on carbohydrates during exercise, but it also burns more fat afterwards because of excess post-exercise oxygen consumption.

Light aerobic work, where you stay between 50% and 70% of your maximum heart rate, tends to use more fat as fuel during the session because the session lasts longer.

Although HIIT has earned a reputation as the best way to lose fat and keep muscle, it is hard to do regularly, which is why its sessions are shorter.

The recommendation is 2 or 3 weekly HIIT sessions of 20 to 30 minutes, plus another 2 or 3 moderate aerobic sessions of 30 to 45 minutes. That way you take the best of both worlds: the light days work as active recovery and HIIT handles the intense stimulus.
