For years it was a fixture in every routine, then it fell out of fashion because of the injury risk. The risk is real and it lives in the range: lowering the legs too far with the back off the bench. With a controlled range, the exercise is as effective as it ever was.

## What it trains

The legs rise through the hip flexors. The abs do not lift them: they hold the pelvis so it does not travel with the thigh and tilt it back once the legs pass vertical.

That final tilt is small, and it is why so many people credit the exercise to a lower region of the abs that does not exist as a separate muscle. The rectus abdominis is one muscle, from pubis to sternum, and here it works top to bottom: first braking the pelvis, then shortening as the trunk flexes a little.

The bench changes one important thing. On the floor the back rests on a surface and the body has a reference; on the bench, the legs hang off the pad and the pelvis works without it. That freedom gives more range and more control, as long as the lower back stays down.

## Starting position

Lie face up on the bench with the hips near the end and the legs hanging off it. The head stays at the far end and the whole back rests on the pad.

Put the hands where they give you stability: under the glutes or gripping the bench edge behind the head. The arms push nothing; they only fix the trunk.

Straighten the legs as far as you can without locking the knees completely and bring them together. Before the first rep, press the lower back into the bench and feel the pressure. That pressure is the abs working and it should never disappear.

## Execution

Raise the legs until they are perpendicular to the trunk, without going past it. The reference is the vertical from the hip, not an invented height.

Lower slowly, with the same control as on the way up, and exhale as you go down. The air leaving helps keep the pressure on the bench and avoids the mistake of holding your breath for the whole set.

The range ends before the lower back comes off. That is the exact point where the exercise stops training the abs and starts loading the lower back.

## The end of the range is the set

The lower back arches when the hip flexors pull on the femur and drag the pelvis forward. If the abs cannot brake that pull, the back lifts off the bench and the arch appears.

On the floor the warning comes earlier, because the back hits the ground. On the bench the gap forms quietly, which is why you should only lower as far as the pressure holds. The set ends when the abs let go of the pelvis, not when the legs reach the lowest point.

## Common mistakes

- Moving the legs fast, especially on the way down.
- Pulling on the bench with the hands to help the abs.
- Lowering the legs below horizontal and arching the back.
- Holding the breath for the whole set.
- Bending the knees halfway through to keep going.

## How to progress

The first progression goes backwards: bent knees and feet closer to the body. That is the version that teaches you to hold the pelvis without the lever taking over.

Once the straight version is clean, add a light dumbbell between the feet. The extra weight lengthens the lever and raises the demand, and it also multiplies the risk if the back comes off, so the range rule becomes stricter.

You can also hold for one second at the top, with the legs vertical, before lowering. It is the cheapest way to add tension with no equipment.

## Where it fits

It is a bodyweight movement for the middle of the session, after the loaded work and before the finisher. At home it can be done on the floor with the hands under the glutes, and the range is controlled the same way.
