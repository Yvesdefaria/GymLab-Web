The movement takes two seconds and one session to learn. What takes longer is to stop compensating with the lower back.

## How to do the glute bridge

Lie on your back on a mat with the knees bent and the feet flat at hip width. The arms rest at your sides, palms down. Before lifting, brace the abs and set the pelvis in neutral: not arched, not forced into a tuck.

Push through the heels and lift the hips until the body forms a straight line from the shoulders to the knees. Squeeze the glutes at the top and hold the contraction for one or two seconds. Lower slowly and start the next rep without letting the hips rest on the floor.

The gluteus maximus does the work and the hamstrings assist the extension. The map of the area is in [hip and glute anatomy](/en/hip-anatomy/).

## The shoulders, hips and knees line

That line is the reference that prevents both height errors. If the hips go above it, the lower back arches and the movement stops being hip extension to become spine extension. If they stop short, the glutes never finish the range.

The pelvis decides. At the top, the glutes are contracted and the abs stay braced; the lower back keeps the same curve it had at the start. One way to check: if you feel pressure in the lower back before you feel work in the glutes, you have gone past the useful height.

The feet count too. The heels stay down and the toes do not peel off the floor. If the heels lift, the knee travels forward and the load shifts.

## Bridge and hip thrust: two hip angles

Both exercises extend the hip with the back supported. The real difference is the hip angle and where the torso rests.

In the bridge, the back is on the floor and the hips start almost fully extended. In the hip thrust, the upper back rests on a bench and the hips start from more flexion, so the range is longer. The knee angle sits near 90 degrees in both and is set by moving the feet.

The bridge is for learning the pattern and accumulating reps. The hip thrust moves more weight because the range is longer and the bench frees the back from the floor. The reasons to pick one or the other are in the [hip thrust benefits](/en/hip-thrust-benefits/).

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose for this page: three sets of 12 to 15 reps, resting 30 to 60 seconds between sets. Bodyweight is enough at first; once all three sets are clean, add load.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Floor bridge | 3 | 12-15 |
| Single-leg bridge | 3 | 8-10 per side |
| Bridge with a dumbbell | 3 | 10-12 |

The pause at the top is not cut to add reps. It is the part that makes the glutes work and keeps the back out of it.

## Mistakes that break the line

The most common one is starting with the hips high and the back arched. The fix is to lower the height and drive the hips towards the ceiling.

The second is placing the feet too far away. The further they are, the more the hamstrings stretch and the less the glutes reach the top. A practical reference: with the hips up, the knee sits near 90 degrees.

The third is lowering without control: if the hips drop, the set becomes a bounce.

## Variations that add difficulty

The single-leg bridge doubles the load on the support side and forces the pelvis to stabilise. A dumbbell or barbell across the hips adds resistance without changing the pattern. A band around the thighs activates the abductors and reminds you not to let the knees collapse.

When the bridge stops being enough, the natural progression is the standing or cable [hip extension](/en/hip-extension-glutes/), which works one side at a time with a longer range.

## Where it fits in the routine

Two or three sessions a week are enough, with at least a day between them. The bridge works at the start of the session as activation, with two light sets, or at the end as high-rep work.

Before you start, the [glute warm-up](/en/glute-workout-warm-up/) gets the hips ready for the loaded movements. After that, the bridge combines without issue with squats and the leg press, as explained in [squat versus leg press](/en/squat-vs-leg-press/).
