No macronutrient carries as many beliefs. That you need 3 grams per kilo, that the body only absorbs 30 grams per meal, that protein damages the kidneys. The available evidence allows a calmer and more useful answer: there is a range, there is a ceiling and there is a list of foods that covers it without supplements.

## What protein does in the body

Proteins are chains of amino acids and serve structural and signalling functions. They build muscle, skin, enzymes, hormones and antibodies. They provide 4 kcal per gram, the same as carbohydrate and less than half of fat, although their energy role is secondary when there is enough food.

The body does not keep a protein store available for emergencies. When intake falls short, it breaks down tissue to obtain amino acids. That is why the daily total matters more than the exact moment of each meal.

## How much protein a day

The reference for a sedentary adult is 0.8 grams per kilo. That figure covers basic needs and is not designed for anyone training with weights regularly. For hypertrophy, trials that measured muscle gain place the optimum between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo.

In a calorie deficit the figure rises because part of the protein is used for energy and because muscle needs protecting when total calories fall. In a surplus, the top of the range adds nothing extra: 1.6 grams per kilo already supports gain in most cases.

## The 2.4 grams per kilo ceiling

Above about 2.4 grams per kilo, the anabolic response stops growing. The body oxidises the surplus amino acids and eliminates them, and the cost shows up in the fridge and in the budget. The figure marks a point of diminishing returns.

That ceiling explains why diets of 3 or 4 grams per kilo are not better. They work if total calories and training support them, and they work worse if they displace fibre, vegetables and healthy fat. The guide to [whether athletes need more protein](/en/do-athletes-need-more-protein/) reviews the studies behind the range.

The table shows why chicken and white fish are the base of many training diets: more protein per calorie. Egg and cottage cheese provide less per 100 grams, but they are eaten in larger portions and add up well. Legumes complete the picture with fibre and carbohydrate, and in a vegan diet they are the centrepiece.

Protein density is not everything. Salmon brings omega-3 fat and egg brings choline and vitamins; beef provides iron and zinc. Choosing only by the protein-calorie ratio leaves out nutrients that also count.

## Quality: what the DIAAS score measures

Protein quality is assessed by amino acid profile and digestibility. The DIAAS score replaced the rodent-based method and measures real digestibility in the human intestine. Animal foods usually score above 100; wheat falls well below and soy comes close.

For anyone eating a varied diet, quality resolves itself: a mix of grains and legumes covers the amino acid profile without calculation. Where it does matter is in strict vegan or very monotonous diets, and there soy, dairy and supplements do the work. The guide to [protein pros and cons](/en/protein-pros-and-cons/) compares sources one by one.

## Spreading it across the day

Protein synthesis responds to each meal with a peak lasting a few hours. Spreading the total across three or four meals of 25 to 40 grams keeps the stimulus going through the day better than concentrating it in one huge dinner. There is no need to eat every three hours: the main meals just have to arrive.

Timing around training matters less than it used to be said. The wide window of several hours covers most cases, and the daily total weighs more than the exact minute. The guide to the [post-workout meal](/en/post-workout-meal/) organises that meal without obsession.

## Supplements: when they make sense

A protein shake is conveniently processed food. It helps when the total cannot be reached with food, when the schedule is tight or when the price per gram of protein is competitive. It has nothing that chicken breast does not have, except convenience.

The guide to [protein supplements](/en/protein-supplements/) compares the formats, and the one on [casein and whey](/en/casein-and-whey/) explains the speed difference between the two milk sources. For most people, two well-built meals and an optional shake cover the day.

## Kidneys and excess protein

In healthy adults, a high protein intake has not been shown to damage the kidney. Studies of up to 2.4 grams per kilo over weeks found no change in kidney function. The kidney filters more nitrogen and adapts; the effect is extra work, not injury.

The exception is diagnosed kidney disease, where protein must be adjusted under medical supervision. Anyone with a single kidney, a family history or altered blood tests should ask before raising the figure. The guide to [excessive protein intake](/en/too-much-protein/) develops that limit.

Excess does not turn into muscle either. If calories exceed expenditure, the surplus contributes to body fat like any other nutrient.
