For example, your shoulders may be a **weak muscle group**. Since they work in most chest exercises, do not train them after chest in the same session: they may already be fatigued by the time you give them your attention.

## How to prioritise a weak point in your routine

Train your shoulders on **different days** from chest, either alone or with another group that is not a weak point. If your triceps are a strong group, training shoulders and triceps on the same day makes sense, with shoulders first.

Chest and back are usually more developed than the arms. In that case, the simple solution for building the arms is not to train triceps on the same day as chest, or biceps on the same day as back, as so many lifters do. Give the arms a **full session** of their own and make sure they are not fatigued by the chest or back session from the day before.

## Rest and frequency for weak points

Notice in the tables how the weakest part of the body in each **routine** gets priority in the working session, or a separate session of its own, and also plenty of rest before the day it is trained.

For example, the shoulders may be **fatigued** the day after training chest, but they will be well recovered if you train legs the day before.

You will also see that in some examples the **weak muscle groups** are trained more often than the strong ones. The aim is to train the weaker areas with **higher volumes** across the week.

To fit these plans into your calendar, read the guide to [workout splits](/en/workout-split-guide/) and the one on [training frequency](/en/training-frequency/). If the lagging group is your back, the guide to [back routines](/en/back-routines/) expands this scheme with eleven plans.
