Getting in shape means three things at once: a body composition that serves you, energy to train and a diet you can keep without counting every gram. The five rules below aim at that, in that order.

## The five rules in one table

Each rule has a reference and a way to check it. When one fails, fix it before touching the others.

## Calories: the starting point

Daily expenditure is estimated with a formula and corrected with the scale. Over two or three weeks, average weight tells you whether the estimate works. If it rises more than intended, cut 200 or 300 calories; if it falls when it should hold, add them.

No calculator knows your exact expenditure. The trend does. Weigh yourself under the same conditions and look at the average, not one day's reading.

## Protein: how much and from where

Between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo covers muscle building and retention in almost any situation. For a 75 kilo person, that is 120 to 165 grams a day, spread across three to five meals of 25 to 40 grams.

The source matters less than the total. Eggs, chicken, fish, lean meat, dairy, legumes and soy cover the quota. Anyone eating little animal protein can combine legumes and grains across the day. The guide to [what to eat to build muscle](/en/what-to-eat-to-build-muscle/) compares sources per 100 grams.

## Fibre: satiety and digestion

Fibre supports satiety when calories drop and feeds the gut microbiota. The reference is 25 to 35 grams a day, with vegetables at every main meal, whole fruit and legumes several times a week.

Raising fibre suddenly causes gas and discomfort. Do it over two or three weeks, with enough water. Vegetables are the easiest route. At 300 to 400 grams a day they add volume without many calories, and the guide to [vegetables for muscle](/en/vegetables-for-muscle/) explains how to fit them in without the plate turning dull.

## Adherence: the rule that decides

A plan you cannot follow loses to a worse one you can. Adherence is built with food you like, realistic schedules and room for exceptions. Skipping a meal ruins nothing. Spreading intake across three or six sittings is a preference, not an obligation.

Eighty per cent is a useful reference. If the plan holds from Monday to Friday and the weekend relaxes without excess, the average stays good. The guide to [diet mistakes](/en/diet-mistakes-to-avoid/) reviews the ones that repeat most often.

## Sleep: the part you do not eat

Sleeping little changes hunger and your willingness to train. On five or six hours a night, appetite rises and strength falls, two things no diet fully compensates for. Seven to nine hours is the sensible range for an adult who trains.

Sleep also orders meals. Anyone sleeping badly tends to snack more and pick quick foods. Fixing the sleep schedule usually does more for the diet than adding a new rule.

## Fat: neither enemy nor miracle

Fat provides 9 calories per gram, more than twice protein and carbohydrate. Between 0.8 and 1 gram per kilo covers hormonal functions and the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Eating a meal with saturated fat does not lead to disease or ageing. The whole pattern counts: favour olive oil, fish, nuts and avocado, and keep fried food and processed meats in their place. The guide to the [eight-week diet](/en/eight-week-bodybuilding-diet/) applies these rules to a plan with dates.

## How to start without changing everything

Pick one rule and work on it for two weeks. Protein is usually the first profitable step: raise it to 1.6 grams per kilo and watch energy and hunger. Then sort calories, then fibre, and adherence last.

The rest settles on its own.
