## What the guided bar buys and what it costs

The guided bar buys stability. There is no path to correct on each rep, so attention stays on the chest and the press. It also lets you rack the bar at any point in the range, which a free rack does not offer.

The price is the arc. The bar travels where the machine sends it, and that path adapts neither to your arm length nor to the shape of your shoulder. If the bench is badly placed, the shoulder works in a forced position for the whole set.

The machine is neither better nor worse than a free bar: it is another tool. It accumulates volume without spending attention on balance, and it can attack a specific sticking point, as covered in [Smith machine sticking points](/en/smith-machine-sticking-points/).

## Setup: bench at 30 degrees and safeties in place

Set the bench to about 30 degrees. Forty is workable, but beyond that the exercise looks more like a shoulder press than a chest press: the front deltoid takes the work and the chest gets less range.

Sit with your back on the pad and check that the bar lands over the upper chest, not the neck and not the abdomen. Move the bench forward or back until the bar touches that line without the shoulders coming off the pad.

Feet are flat on the floor with the knees slightly bent. The wrists stay stacked over the forearms, thumb around the bar.

Set the safeties at chest height. On this machine they are the net that lets you take the last rep without a partner: if the bar will not rise, it rests on the safeties and you slide out from under it.

## Execution

Unhook the bar and lower it over two or three seconds until it touches the upper chest. The elbows stay at about 45 degrees from the trunk, never flared to 90.

Press without bouncing and without slamming the elbow into lockout. The bar rises on its track and the chest stays high: once the shoulders creep towards the ears, the range is gone.

Breathe: take air before the descent and release it past the hardest part of the press. Holding your breath for the whole set adds pressure, not stability.

## Common mistakes

- **Bench at 45 degrees.** The exercise becomes a shoulder press with the chest as an assistant.
- **Bar over the neck.** It happens when the bench sits too far forward. Fix it by moving the bench, not the head.
- **Safeties too low.** If the bar cannot rest on them, they do nothing.
- **Half reps.** A short range on this machine adds nothing a full press does not give.
- **Bouncing off the chest.** With a guided bar the bounce is easier and more useless.
- **Legs in the air.** Without the feet planted the hips move and the chest loses its angle.

## Dose and programming

Three or four sets of 8 to 12 reps with 90 to 120 seconds of rest, a two or three second descent and the last set taken to the safeties. That last set does the work: lower until the bar stops moving and let it rest.

Load goes up when 12 reps hold the same path with no bounce. If the bar stalls at forehead height in the third set, the weight is too high for that day.

In the week, this press works as a second exercise after the bench press, or as a first exercise in a session where the shoulder wants a guided path. The [Smith machine routine](/en/smith-machine-routine/) pairs it with other movements on the machine, and the [chest routines](/en/chest-routines/) order the whole block.

## When to use it and when not to

It earns its place when you train alone and want to get close to failure without a spotter, when the shoulder prefers a fixed path, or when the upper chest needs extra volume. Also when the free press has stalled and another stimulus is needed without rewriting the week.

It does not replace the free barbell press as the main lift. The free bar demands balance and stabilisation, and that demand is part of the training.
