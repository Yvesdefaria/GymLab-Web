For years fish was recommended without much enthusiasm, and research over recent decades changed that reputation. The question is now how much, at what size and from which species. All three answers fit into one weekly shop.

## What fish supplies

Protein makes up 15 to 20 percent of its weight, so a 100-gram serving supplies about 18 to 20 grams of high-quality protein. Fish also carries iodine, selenium, vitamin B12 and, in oily species, vitamin D.

Fat is what separates the species. White fish stays under 2 percent fat, while oily fish reaches 8 to 25 percent. That fat supplies EPA and DHA, the two omega 3 fatty acids the body does not make efficiently and which are associated with lower cardiovascular risk.

## How much fish and at what size

The practical recommendation is two to three servings a week, of 100 to 150 grams each. That leaves 300 to 450 grams a week, a figure that fits into two dinners and one lunch. One of the servings should be oily fish.

The serving is measured by weight, not by the size of the fillet on the counter. A 150-gram salmon fillet sits around 300 calories and supplies more omega 3 than a week requires.

## Mercury decides the species

Mercury accumulates along the food chain, and large, long-lived fish that eat other fish concentrate more of it. Species matters more than quantity when fish is eaten several times a week.

The species in the last row are reserved for exceptional occasions in the general population and avoided entirely in pregnancy, breastfeeding and childhood. King mackerel is sometimes confused with common mackerel, a small species that is suitable for frequent meals.

## Pregnancy and children

In pregnancy the two or three weekly servings stay, always from low-mercury species. Omega 3 takes part in the development of the fetal brain and retina, and mercury crosses the placenta, so the choice of species matters twice as much.

For young children, start with small portions and mild species, and avoid the same ones as in pregnancy. Texture and bones need more attention than the table.

## Omega 3: the figure that matters

The European reference for adults is 250 milligrams of EPA and DHA a day, about 1.75 grams a week. A 100-gram serving of salmon supplies 1.5 to 2 grams, and the same amount of sardines sits around 1 to 1.5 grams. Two servings of oily fish a week meet the target without supplements.

White fish supplies little fat and therefore little omega 3, but it remains a good source of protein and iodine. The guide to [fats](/en/dietary-fats/) sorts the other sources and the [anti-inflammatory diet](/en/anti-inflammatory-diet/) places fish inside a complete pattern.

## Frozen, canned and grilled

Frozen fish keeps its nutritional value if the cold chain is unbroken. Thaw it in the fridge, on a rack, and do not freeze it again. Industrial blast freezing, below minus 40 degrees, forms fewer crystals than a home freezer and preserves texture better.

A tin of sardines or tuna in water is a practical, cheap option. Light tuna carries less mercury than bluefin, and the oil in the tin can be drained when calories matter.

Grilling, baking and steaming respect the good fat. Batter and frying add oil and calories, and hide the taste of the fish. The guide to [high-protein lunches](/en/high-protein-lunches/) proposes combinations to take to work and the one on [sushi](/en/sushi-and-protein/) clarifies what raw fish contributes.

## The weekly shop in four lines

Two or three servings a week, of 100 to 150 grams. One or two of small oily fish. No shark, swordfish or king mackerel, and none at all in pregnancy. The rest is decided by price, season and taste, because the best fish is the one that gets cooked.

The [food pyramid](/en/food-pyramid/) placed fish in the protein group, and [diet myths](/en/healthy-diet-myths/) remind you that oily fish does not make you fat on its own. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) close the topic: fish oil capsules do not replace two servings a week.
