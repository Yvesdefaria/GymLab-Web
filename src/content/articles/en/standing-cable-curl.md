The low pulley is sold as constant tension. The phrase sounds good and is half false: the load's moment arm still shortens as the forearm approaches vertical. What does stay fixed is the direction of the resistance, and that is the real difference you can use in a set.

## The setup

The pulley sits low, with a handle or a straight bar. Stand facing the machine, take the grip with the palms up and step back until the cable is taut and the stack lifts a little. That step is the adjustment almost nobody makes: without it, the first part of the pull goes into taking the slack out and the bottom of the rep is dead.

Feet at hip width, knees soft, torso upright. The elbows stay at the sides and do not move during the set. The weight rises to near chest height, pauses for a second at the top, and lowers under control to full extension without the stack slamming.

## The line of pull

On a dumbbell curl, the resistance hangs from the hand and turns with the wrist. At the bottom the weight falls in line with the forearm and the biceps holds little; as the hand rises, the moment arm grows and then shortens, so the work changes through the whole path. The cable does not remove that path, but it flattens it: the cable pulls from the floor in the same direction the whole time, so the bottom keeps more load than it does with a dumbbell.

The cable offers a flatter profile than a dumbbell: tension still changes with the angle, and the biceps keeps an easy point and a hard point. The difference is that the easy one is less empty.

## Why the one-arm version exists

With both hands on a bar, the bar decides the path and both wrists turn together. With a single handle, the hand can rotate from neutral to supinated as it rises, and the cable keeps its direction through that turn. That combination is what justifies the one-arm exercise.

Stand far enough from the pulley that the arm starts fully extended and the stack is barely lifted. Raise the handle turning the wrist up and keep the elbow at the side. The wrist ends in line with the forearm, without bending backwards. [Wrist position in the biceps curl](/en/wrist-position-biceps-curl/) explains why that detail decides whether the set ends in the biceps or the forearm.

The one-arm version also shows whether one side does more work. With a bar, the difference hides; with a handle, it shows.

## The mistakes

- **Short step.** If the cable has slack at the start, the first part of the range loads nothing.
- **Elbows drifting.** The elbow moves forward, the shoulder pushes and the curl becomes a pulldown.
- **Torso joining in.** Swinging adds momentum and spreads the work into the lower back.
- **Bent wrist.** The hand breaks backwards and the lever escapes the biceps.
- **Dropping the return.** The stack comes back fast, the cable bounces and the bottom is lost.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The dose is a recommendation from this page; the original gives no numbers. Add weight when all three sets reach 12 with the pause at the top and a controlled descent.

It works as the second or third exercise of the day, after a heavy curl and before an isolation movement. The [high cable lying curl](/en/high-cable-lying-curl/) uses the same cable from another direction and loads the short head; this one keeps the shoulder neutral and the full range.

## Where it fits

The standing cable curl adds quality work at the end of the day, when the weight matters less than the direction of the resistance.
