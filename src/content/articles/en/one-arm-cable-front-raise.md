## The low pulley moves the dead spot

A dumbbell front raise has a dead stretch at the start. With the arm hanging, the dumbbell pulls toward the floor and the shoulder has almost nothing to beat: the gravity line runs through the joint. Tension shows up as the arm nears the horizontal, and the work stays there.

With a low pulley, the cable pulls from below. At the start there is already a component the shoulder has to brake, and that tension does not disappear until the arm is up. The cable loads the beginning of the range; the dumbbell loads the middle.

It is the complement. Dumbbell and cable load different points of the same arc, and the choice depends on where you want the tension. Anyone who wants it at the hardest point picks the dumbbell; anyone who wants it while the arm hangs picks the cable.

## Why crossing the midline does not work

The archive suggests taking the hand diagonally toward the midline to work the middle deltoid better. That cue changes the exercise: a front raise moves in the sagittal plane, in front of the body, and its target is the anterior deltoid.

Taking the hand to the centre turns the path into cross-body adduction. The arm loses leverage in the plane that matters and the chest joins earlier. The middle deltoid gains leverage when it takes the arm away from the body; the cross removes that leverage.

Keep the hand in the shoulder line for the whole rep. If the cable pulls inward, the problem is body position, not the direction of the arm.

## One arm at a time

The one-arm version lets the shoulder work without a reference from the other side. The free hand rests on the stack and the torso stays still; the working arm competes with nothing.

With two hands and a rope, the hands tend to drift together at the top. That gesture is the cross this page rules out. The unilateral version avoids it by design, because there is no other hand to cross toward.

## The position

Low pulley with a D handle. Stand side-on to the stack or facing away from it, with the foot on the working side slightly forward. The arm hangs with a soft elbow and a neutral wrist.

The free hand rests on the stack at chest height. The torso stays still: if it leans back to help, the cable stops being the limit of the movement.

The wrist stays neutral, with the thumb closed around the handle. The cable pulls forward and down, so a loose wrist bends back under load and moves the line of pull away from the shoulder.

## The rep

Raise the arm forward to shoulder height without crossing the body and without opening the elbow. The cable stays taut through the whole path, the descent included. Lower slowly until the arm hangs again.

The endpoint is shoulder height. Higher than that, the shoulder changes plane and the anterior deltoid stops being the main worker. The elbow never locks.

The hand travels from the hip line to the shoulder line. Nothing in between needs a pause: the cable keeps the load on, so the arm moves without hunting for tension. A pause at the top is optional; the descent is not.

## Dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps per arm with 45 or 60 seconds of rest. Start with the weak side and match the other arm to that number.

The load goes up when the last rep ends at shoulder height and the torso is still. If the cable drags the arm back on the way down, the weight is too heavy.
