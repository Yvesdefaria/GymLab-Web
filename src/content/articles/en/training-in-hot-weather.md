When the temperature rises, heat-related illness rises with it, especially among physically active people. Sitting on the sofa with the air conditioning on full is not the answer: learn to move with some sense when the thermometer climbs.

When body temperature rises, you sweat, your lungs work harder and your heart rate speeds up. Sweat evaporation lowers your internal temperature and cools you when the body overheats.

Men tend to sweat heavily, and that volume makes evaporation less effective. The smaller the sweat droplets, the better you cool down.

## Who is at greater risk when training in hot weather

All men, whatever their age, are at risk of heat-related injury because their sweating process is less efficient than women's. You also join the risk group if you are not fit, if you carry a few extra kilos or if your body is not used to hot, humid climates.

Think of your body as an engine. If you keep driving after the engine has overheated, it will eventually seize. During periods of high temperature and humidity, your body does the same.

Everyone responds differently to heat. If you are used to it, it takes longer for the consequences to show.

## Cramps, exhaustion and heat stroke

There are three types of heat-related health problem: **cramps**, **exhaustion** and **heat stroke**. They are not necessarily progressive, so stay alert. Exhaustion and heat stroke can arrive with or without warning.

### Cramps

The first symptoms can range from small twitches to real **spasms** in the arms, wrists, legs and belly.

When you sweat, you lose **electrolytes**, which are needed for correct muscle function. The less conditioned you are, the more water and electrolytes you lose through sweating, and that loss is what causes cramps.

When cramps come with heavy sweating, heat is the most likely cause.

When you start showing signs of overheating, take a **break**. Go to a cool place in the shade and drink plenty of liquid. An isotonic drink is very useful for replacing electrolytes. Try **massaging** the affected area to ease the pain. After one or two hours, it will disappear. In the meantime, you may need to move your training indoors.

Heat also forces you to adjust your pauses. If the session drags or your body does not recover, check the [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) before forcing the next one.

### Exhaustion

Exhaustion is more serious, but still treatable. Imagine what happens when your body starts running out of water. You get tired, dizzy and may end up with a headache. You could also develop a skin rash and feel chills. Other symptoms include extreme thirst and nausea.

Stop exercising immediately. Go to a cool place, preferably a dry one. Drink plenty of liquid. Use a wet sponge to apply water to your skin. In normal cases, the symptoms disappear after one or two hours.

### Heat stroke

Your body's air conditioning has stopped working. The temperature keeps rising and there is no way to bring it down. You feel confused, paranoid, even violent. You will probably stop sweating and feel chills and nausea while your skin stays very hot and dry.

**This problem is serious.** Call an ambulance or go to hospital immediately, because it can have terrible consequences. Move to a cool area and apply water to your body.

You can prevent this situation by exercising with someone else. Develop a system with a partner so you can watch each other and spot potentially serious symptoms.

## How to stay cool while training

Prevention is the key to coping with intense heat. Before you train, answer four questions: how to stay cool, what time is best for exercise, what to eat or drink and what clothes to wear.

Summer is not the ideal season to start an outdoor fitness routine. People who are fit sweat more effectively, so they lose less water and fewer electrolytes.

Train early in the morning or late in the afternoon. The hottest moment of the day is usually around one in the afternoon.

Keep your skin wet to help the body's natural cooling system. Apply water constantly or use wet towels. Evaporation cools you without using up your water and electrolyte reserves.

Heat also changes your breathing and your pulse. If you want to understand how the body responds to effort, the guide to [muscle oxygenation training](/en/muscle-oxygenation-training/) explains what happens when oxygen demand rises. If the heat leaves you short of breath, the guide to [breathing while lifting](/en/breathing-while-lifting/) sets the pattern that sustains the set.

## The weather forecast: humidity and heatwaves

Do not ignore the weather forecast. Avoid training outdoors on very humid days or during a heatwave. On days with a high humidity percentage, the body struggles more to cool down because the air is already saturated with moisture.

## Hydration and signs of dehydration

Drinking water is the priority during hot spells. Remember this rule: **if you are thirsty, you are already dehydrated**. Drink plenty of water before, during and after exercise.

Weigh yourself before and after training. Replace the water weight you have lost: the rule is two glasses of water for every half kilo lost.

Avoid drinks such as tea, cola, beer and alcohol. Alcohol and caffeine are **diuretics**, so they dehydrate the body even further.

### Signs of dehydration

1. Thirst
2. Tiredness
3. Headache
4. Confusion
5. Dark urine
6. Dry mouth
7. Loss of appetite

If any of these signs appear, stop and drink. Heat and humidity also make the lungs work harder, and paying attention to your breathing during the session helps keep the effort from spiralling.

## Light meals for training in the heat

During summer, instead of eating large meals, try to eat something light six times a day. Avoid excess protein and spices, because these increase body temperature during digestion.

## Clothing, sun protection and safety

This is not the season for tight shirts or stretch-fibre trousers. Be sensible and stylish at the same time: wear loose, light clothing in light colours. Take a cap to protect your head from the sun and the heat. After training, take off your sweaty clothes straight away and put on clean, dry ones.

Rubber and vinyl garments that are supposed to make you sweat more and lose weight are a bad idea. They could cost you your life.

Never leave the house without **sun protection**. As well as preventing skin cancer, it deflects heat and can prevent burns, which inhibit the body's natural cooling process.

Learn to recognise the first symptoms that your body is not coping with the heat. Looking good does not mean risking your health. Who knows, after reading this guide you might even save the life of someone you love.

When the heat is on, stay cool. And if the heat forces you to change times and sessions, the guide to [common training mistakes](/en/common-training-mistakes/) helps you avoid adding unnecessary fatigue.
