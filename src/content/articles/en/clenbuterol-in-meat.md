The question is not whether meat can contain residues, but how much and how often. Farm animals have received anabolic substances illegally for decades, and testing catches only part of what reaches the market. For a competitive athlete, that reality turns a meal into a risk they do not control.

## What residues appear in meat

Clenbuterol is a beta-2 agonist that has been used in livestock to increase an animal's weight and reduce fat. In humans it has marked cardiovascular effects and it is banned in sport at all times. Anabolic steroids such as nandrolone or clostebol have also been given to animals, and some tissues retain them for weeks.

Liver and offal concentrate more residues than muscle, because they are organs of metabolism and clearance. There the dose per serving can be several times that of a steak. Cooking does not guarantee destruction of the compound: studies that measured metabolites in people who ate treated meat found traces in urine even after the piece was cooked.

## The documented cases

At the Beijing Olympics, an American athlete bought an unusually large chicken breast in a local supermarket. Analysis of that meat showed enough anabolic steroids to produce a positive doping test.

In Spain, more than a hundred people went to hospital with heart problems after eating meat contaminated with clenbuterol. The episode was not sporting: they were ordinary consumers, and the picture they presented, with palpitations and tachycardia, describes what the substance does.

A 1994 study documented metabolites of an anabolic steroid in four of the eight people who ate meat from a treated chicken, 24 hours after the meal. Another case, from 1992, found a positive for clostebol after eating raw minced meat. Cooking lowers the concentration but does not remove it completely.

## Why there is no safe level

Clenbuterol effects appear at very low doses: fine tremor, palpitations, anxiety, a rise in blood pressure and a fall in blood potassium. In people with existing heart problems, a small dose can destabilise the picture. There is no threshold below which consumption is harmless for the whole population, which is why most countries ban its use in animals raised for food.

That absence of a threshold is also the laboratories' argument. Current methods detect concentrations far below those that produce an effect, and a meat residue can stay above the reporting limit. The practical consequence is uncomfortable: an athlete can test positive on an amount that does not improve performance.

## The ban and the margin in the rules

The World Anti-Doping Agency lists clenbuterol among substances prohibited at all times, in and out of competition. There is no permitted therapeutic use for a healthy athlete, and no therapeutic use exemption covers it in the sporting context.

The rules acknowledge the contamination problem with a minimum reporting level: below certain concentrations, the laboratory does not report the finding as a positive. That margin is not a permission. A value above the threshold opens a case, and a contaminated-meat defence requires evidence that is almost never kept.

## Contaminated supplements

Supplements are the other known route. Some factories share mixers between products, and a batch of creatine can come out with traces of a hormonal compound. Other times the addition is deliberate, especially in products bought online or made in countries with weak controls.

The result is the same: a positive for a substance the athlete did not know they were taking. ## Most positives are voluntary

It is worth saying plainly. Contamination exists and is documented, but it explains a minority of cases. In most files, the athlete took the substance on purpose, and the meat or supplement defence appears after the result. Accepting that reality avoids two errors: believing every positive is a conspiracy and believing no meal can ruin a career.

## What the athlete can control

Varying meat sources reduces exposure to one particular batch. Keeping the receipt and packaging of a supplement allows the batch to be traced if a positive appears. Declaring everything taken on the control form, including products without a sports label, leaves a useful record. And asking a doctor about palpitations or tremor after a meal is not excessive: it is the way to document a picture.

None of this makes meat a dangerous food. Most batches are clean and normal consumption poses no cardiovascular risk for a healthy person. The problem is specific: residues in one batch, in an athlete gambling a licence on a meal.
