A lunge is a leg movement with one leg in front and one behind. Both versions start from a similar position and lower the body with the same control. The difference appears on the way up, and that is where the work gets shared differently.

## What both versions train

The work falls on the hip extensors, which are the glutes and hamstrings, and the knee extensors, which are the four muscles of the quadriceps. The hip adductors and abductors also join in, small and barely visible, adding stability and mass to the thigh.

These are the same groups a squat uses. The difference is that the lunge separates the legs: the weight falls on one of them while the other stays in support.

Eccentric is the descent, concentric the ascent and support the work of holding and balancing. The maximum rating appears only on the front leg of the walking version.

## Static lunge: focus and space

On the descent, the front leg carries almost all the weight. The glute, quadriceps and hamstring of that leg stretch under load while the back leg barely joins in.

On the ascent, the front leg pushes and extends the hip and the knee. The back leg adds something too: the table gives it moderate work in that phase. That is the main difference from the walking version, where the back leg stays in support for the whole movement.

With no travel, the static lunge fits in a hallway or a small room. It also lets you pause between reps and correct your position without rushing.

## Walking lunge: travel and work per leg

The descent is identical to the static version: the weight drops onto the front leg and the back leg holds. The ascent changes. The front leg does maximum work and the back leg only follows, so each rep truly loads one leg.

Travel adds a demand the static version does not have: staying balanced as you move forward. It also adds steps, and with them a little cardiovascular work. In exchange, you need space, and technique breaks down more easily when you are tired.

## Which one to choose

Choose the walking version when the goal is conditioning and unilateral work over a distance. Alternating legs spreads the effort and the session keeps moving.

Choose the static version when you want focus on one leg, little space or fine control of position. Also when you want to stop and adjust without losing the thread.

Both fit in the same week. One session with walking lunges and another with static ones covers the pattern without repeating the same stimulus.

## Three mistakes that change the split

**Taking too short a step.** The foot comes close to the body and the lunge turns into a narrow squat. Find a distance where the front knee stays over the foot and the back leg extends without discomfort.

**Letting the knee fall inwards.** The knee loses alignment with the foot and the work is shared badly. Push in the direction of the second toe and keep the arch of the foot.

**Leaning the torso forward.** The descent comes from the leg, not the back. Keep the chest up and the ribs down as you lower.
