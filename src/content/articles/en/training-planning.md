**Training planning** exists whether you name it or not. Every routine sits on a programme of some kind, and writing it down makes your goals realistic and shows you why you get results.

You do not need to be a professional. Someone who rides a bike, hikes in the mountains, plays football or lifts weights can use the same tool: a programme adapted to their traits and to what they want to achieve.

## Where planning comes from

The earliest known references date back to ancient Greece, and the goal was to arrive at competitions in the best possible condition. It was the Soviets in the 1960s who, building on studies of stress and adaptation to effort, supplied the ideas, terminology and structure used today in sports training planning.

## The four training cycles

Whatever the goals and the time available, there are different cycles for organising the work.

### The session

It is the most basic unit. The plan is executed in it and, from the athlete's point of view, it is the training itself.

It has three phases: the warm-up, the main part, where the session's goals are developed, and the cool-down, with stretching and relaxation. Every session has goals to work on and track, so they can be adjusted to the programme as a whole.

### The microcycle

It brings together a set number of sessions, usually over 5 to 10 days, with a common purpose: introducing or loading the base, working at high intensity or volume and, finally, recovering.

Microcycles follow and complement each other. For a competitive athlete, the goal is to reach tournament day well trained, activated and recovered from the effort, ready to compete at full physical and technical capacity.

### The mesocycle

It covers two or more microcycles and usually lasts between 2 and 6 weeks of training. The most widely accepted structure is ATR, for accumulation, transformation and realisation, from Issurin and Kaverin (1985).

The **accumulation mesocycle** prioritises physical conditioning. The **transformation** mesocycle focuses on the activity you are preparing for. In judo, for example, with its heavy strength component and need for explosive actions, you would work maximum strength and explosive strength. The **realisation** mesocycle aims for maximum similarity to the sport itself, with real competition situations, and you recover in order to peak on tournament day.

### The macrocycle

It covers the whole process of reaching maximum form. A season can include one, two or several macrocycles, depending on how many times you need to peak.

In bodybuilding, since most people who practise it do not compete, the macrocycle is usually **open**, with no fixed end. Another common, basic bodybuilding macrocycle splits the year into three periods: strength, size and definition.

## Control and evaluation

Any programme needs control and evaluation to adjust to reality while it is being applied, and to check at the end whether the goals were met.

**Control** is data collection: body measurements, physical tests, clinical analyses. It gives objective information on the success or failure of your actions, your current level, possible signs of overtraining or the stages you have reached.

**Evaluation** shows how far the initial goals are being met and to what degree they are achieved at the end. From there comes the correction of the process, if one is needed.

Two athletes at the same level can respond differently to the same goals: for one they may be out of reach for now, for the other achievable in the short term. Adjust the goals to your real, current level and you satisfy the basic principle of training theory: progression.

## Why plan even if you do not compete

Programming means distributing the different training methods over time according to your goals. Whatever your level and your target, plan your training: you will enjoy the activity more.

A theoretical plan like this one helps you organise yourself when you set practical goals. You will find examples and more detail in the guide to [training periodization](/en/training-periodization/).
