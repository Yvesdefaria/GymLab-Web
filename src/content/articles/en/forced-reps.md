It is the most widely used high-intensity technique in the gym, and you have probably used it without knowing its name. The difference between a forced set and a normal set is what happens after the last rep you can complete alone.

## What they are

Forced reps are a technique for training past [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/). Instead of lowering the weight, as in drop sets, or resting, as in rest-pause, you keep doing reps with the same weight and a partner's help. Unlike [supersets](/en/supersets/), which chain two exercises, here you do not change movement.

The goal is to perform significantly more reps than you could complete in a strength set with that weight. A normal set ends when the muscle fails; a forced set starts right there.

## How to do them with a partner

The classic example is the bench press. Complete the set to failure and, at that moment, your partner helps just enough for 2 or 3 more reps.

The partner's job is the critical part. Too much help and you are not working hard enough to get the technique's benefit. Too little and the reps do not happen. The help has to be just enough to get past the sticking point. The partner follows the bar and applies only the pressure needed to keep the upward phase moving; the effort stays yours.

[Training with a partner](/en/training-partner-benefits/) has its best reason in safety, and forced reps are the clearest case. Someone who knows how to measure their help is worth more than someone who just pushes.

## Without a partner

Without a partner, forced reps are limited to unilateral exercises and some machines. In a unilateral exercise you can use the free arm to help the working arm. On some chest press machines, the foot platform lets you push with your legs and move the handles.

Outside those cases, a partner is not optional. On the bench press, shoulder press or squat, reaching failure without help is a risk that does not pay off.

## When to use them and when to avoid them

Forced reps are one of the most advanced and effective techniques for increasing strength and muscle volume. They are also demanding, and their place in the plan is small.

As a rule, do not use them in every workout. When you do, limit them to the last set or the last two sets of an exercise. You can apply them to any exercise, just like drop sets and rest-pause. It is the same rule the [Weider principles](/en/weider-principles/) set out.

Save them for when you want to give an extra push to a specific body part. [Muscle pump](/en/muscle-pump/) is the sign that you have sent blood to the muscle, not a measure of growth; the set counts for the work and the progression.

## Risks

The price of forced reps is high. They put a great deal of stress on the muscle and the joints, and with excessive frequency they make overtraining inevitable.

Going past failure is less dangerous with machines or dumbbells than with a free barbell. On barbell exercises, an experienced partner lowers the risk.

If you feel a sharp pain in a tendon or a joint, stop. The deep burn in the muscle is the signal of effort; sharp pain is a danger sign.

## How they differ from drop sets and rest-pause

All three techniques share the same premise. They all keep training after muscle failure, and the method changes.

In drop sets you lower the weight, often by 25 to 40%, and keep doing reps to failure with the lighter load. In rest-pause you keep the weight, rest a few seconds and get more reps. In forced reps you keep the weight and receive help on the upward phase.

That is why forced reps depend most on another person. They also demand the most from the partner. If the help is not well measured, the set stops being a forced set.

Forced reps are not for every workout or every exercise. Use them with a partner who knows how to measure their help, on the last set or the last two, and leave them out when your body asks for recovery. [High-intensity training](/en/high-intensity-training/) places this technique alongside the others and explains when to rotate them.
