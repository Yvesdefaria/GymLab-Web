The manoeuvre has one goal: reaching the stage with full muscles and thin skin. It has been practised for decades and passed from locker room to locker room with more confidence than evidence. This page explains what it involves and why it should not be copied.

## What competitors attempt

In the final week, some competitors empty glycogen with fewer carbohydrates and more training volume, then reverse the plan: they eat a lot of carbohydrate and rest. The idea is that an empty muscle will store more glycogen than usual.

Others add sodium and water manipulation: they raise salt and drink more for days and then cut both suddenly. They want less water under the skin and a harder look. What shows on stage lasts a few hours.

## Glycogen carries water

Each gram of glycogen is stored with about 2.7 grams of water. A large load swells the muscle and pushes the scale up several kilos. That water is part of the visual effect, not new muscle.

A trained muscle stores more glycogen than a sedentary one, and supercompensation is real. The actual gain is modest and is used up in the next session; the rest is water and glycogen returning to where it belongs.

## A load is about 6 grams per kilo

When loading is discussed, the figures in circulation sit around 6 grams of carbohydrate per kilo of body weight. For a 90 kg person that is 540 grams a day, almost two kilos of cooked rice. It is an amount that sits badly outside a prepared context and adds nothing to anyone who does not compete.

## Where the risk sits

Water and sodium concentrate the risk. Restricting fluids and cutting salt in the previous days can cause dehydration, cramps, dizziness and drops in blood pressure. In extreme cases hyponatraemia appears, a fall in blood sodium that can cause seizures and arrhythmias. Symptoms can start quietly and escalate within hours.

The kidney suffers with dehydration and with diuretics, which are also banned in competition. The combination of little fluid, little sodium and a diuretic is the one that has sent competitors to emergency rooms.

## Why it is not copied

The peak lasts hours and depends on a preparation that does not repeat in normal life. Copying it without supervision leaves a rebound of water and fat and a lost training week. For fat loss it offers nothing: the deficit remains the only lever.

Emptying glycogen lowers the scale because water is lost, not fat, and that water returns as soon as carbohydrates and salt come back. Mistaking that drop for success is the most expensive error of the final week.

## What to do instead

If you compete, the plan is practised in the off-season, with a coach and with blood tests. The final week is refined by reducing training volume, not by gambling with fluids. A repeatable, measured plan is worth more than a one-day peak.

If you do not compete, there is no reason to empty glycogen. A diet with enough carbohydrates, protein covered and a sensible training load looks better and performs better.
