A serving is the standard amount recommended for a moderately active man eating 2,000 to 2,500 calories a day. A portion is what actually reaches your plate at one meal. Confusing the two terms is why so many arguments about food end without anyone discussing the same thing.

## The portion grew by a third in seven years

An analysis of food portions in the United States compared 1989-1991 with 1994-1996 and found the average size had grown by about a third across every food studied. Seven years were enough to change the scale of the standard plate.

Meat is the clearest case. The recommended serving is 85 g, a piece the size of a deck of cards. Most people eat considerably more. A normal restaurant cut weighs 220 to 350 g, so one piece serves as two to four portions. And the amount recommended at a meal is two servings, not four.

## The muffin and the bagel: a teaching example

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) uses the muffin and the bagel as an example in its education material. A muffin went from about 45 g and 210 calories to 115 g and more than 500. A bagel rose from 140 to 350 calories. These are education figures that show the scale of the change, not the result of a clinical trial.

Even dishes sold as healthy have grown. A restaurant chicken salad may now carry twice the food it did twenty years ago, with twice the dressing.

## Why the big plate wins

There is a commercial reason behind the growth. The food industry sells more quantity for the same money and the customer leaves with the feeling of a bargain. The extra calories in those portions arrive loaded with fat, saturated fat and sodium.

Eating them daily has two measurable consequences: weight goes up and dietary sodium goes up too. That is the part the evidence supports clearly, without turning it into a diagnosis.

The count is not only about calories. The big plate normalises a portion you do not need, and what looks normal today looks small tomorrow.

## Do not eat with your eyes

Your eyes are poor judges of a portion. Who you eat with matters too: if other people are eating nearby, it is easy to keep going past the point of need, because the conversation stops you registering the amount. And the clean plate of childhood still weighs, because many people eat until the plate is empty out of habit rather than hunger.

Serving from large dishes left on the table has the same effect: you go back more often than if the food stayed in the kitchen.

## How to apply it at the table

Swap half the steak for the same weight of steamed vegetables. Serve on small plates. Before eating out, have a snack a couple of hours earlier so you do not arrive hungry. If the portion is huge, set half aside before you start and ask for it to be wrapped.

A mix works for cereal: 80 % whole grain and 20 % of the one you already like. The same total amount tastes sweeter and there is no need to double the bowl.

Order helps too. Start with the vegetables and the protein, and leave the carbohydrate for last. By the time you reach it, hunger has dropped and the portion adjusts itself.

## What a portion does not fix

Portion size moves calories, and calories move weight. The quality of the rest of the plate counts too: an adjusted portion of [shellfish](/en/shellfish-nutrition/) brings protein and iodine, while an adjusted portion of pastry is still pastry.

If you want to keep track without obsessing, the guide to [tracking what you eat](/en/how-to-track-what-you-eat/) and the one on [maintenance calories](/en/maintenance-calories/) set the frame. Eating fast matters as well: the guide to [eating fast and weight](/en/eating-fast-and-weight/) explains why fullness arrives late. And if a diet promises to halve your portion in a week, check the [red flags of a fad diet](/en/fad-diets/) first.

Changing the size of the plate does not replace a plan; it makes the plan easier to follow.
