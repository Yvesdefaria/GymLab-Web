After training, a fixed recipe keeps circulating: 40 grams of protein and 60 to 100 grams of carbohydrate, in a ratio that gets repeated without saying where it came from. The number is copied so often it looks like a physiological requirement. The figures the evidence supports are different and leave more room.

## Two transporters in the gut

Glucose and fructose are absorbed by different proteins. Glucose enters through SGLT1 and fructose through GLUT5, so a meal that only supplies glucose saturates one route and leaves the other idle. Adding fructose opens that second channel and moves more carbohydrate per hour. That is the basis of the mix and also its limit.

The limit is the amount. The second route only adds when the load exceeds what one transporter can handle, and that happens at high intakes: a long race, two sessions in one day or a very large carbohydrate meal. In a portion of 40 to 70 grams, glucose is absorbed without help and the mix adds little.

## The number that decides is the daily total

For someone training with weights, the carbohydrate reference sits around 3 to 5 grams per kilo of body weight per day, spread across meals. That figure covers the session and replaces the glycogen it used. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) explains how it is split and which types exist.

A single meal can carry 40 grams or 70 without changing the outcome if the day adds up. Consistency is what counts: arrive at the session with glycogen and replace it afterwards.

## Protein: 20 to 40 grams

Protein in the meal after training sits in the same range as the rest of the day, between 20 and 40 grams. That amount stimulates muscle protein synthesis and fits the reference of 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo per day.

A shake is not compulsory. Chicken, fish, eggs, legumes or dairy cover the portion with normal food. The shake helps when there is no time or the stomach refuses a plate.

## The window lasts hours

The idea of eating within thirty minutes carries less weight than it is given. Muscle sensitivity stays elevated for hours and the day as a whole decides recovery. Anyone training twice a day does need to shorten the gap; everyone else can eat when it fits. The guide to the [post-workout meal](/en/post-workout-meal/) develops that point with numbers.

Those pieces build portions of 40 to 70 grams of carbohydrate. Two slices of bread with a boiled potato land near 64 grams; a banana with the juice sits around 55. Protein goes on top, as the shake or as a portion of chicken.

Adding sugar to juice does not improve the mix. Sucrose is already glucose and fructose joined together, and it is in fruit and in table sugar. The combination comes from normal food, without dedicated powders.

## When mixing makes sense

The mix has one clear case: long or repeated sessions on the same day, where absorbing plenty of carbohydrate per hour without gut trouble matters. Also for anyone who tolerates a large volume of food poorly and needs to concentrate energy.

Outside that setting, the ratio is irrelevant. Choosing bread, rice, potato or fruit by appetite and by how the stomach feels beats calculating fractions.

## Mistakes that repeat

The first is copying a 2:1:1 ratio without knowing what it represents: the sources do not say which part belongs to each sugar, and the result changes with the food. The second is turning the meal after training into a shake with sugar and nothing else. The third is trusting products that promise instant recovery; the guide to [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) covers those shortcuts.

Fat in the meal after training does not block protein or carbohydrate absorption. It slows stomach emptying, and that shows up in digestion. The guide to [dietary fats](/en/dietary-fats/) and the one on [trans fats](/en/trans-fats/) place that nutrient in context.

If the goal is fat loss, the meal after training counts toward the daily total and needs no special treatment. To gain weight, raise the carbohydrate portion and keep protein steady.
