The chest answers well to work, which is why almost everyone trains it. The problem is rarely a lack of effort. It is a set of habits inherited without review, and they explain why some people spend years at the same weight and with the same chest.

## Lifting too much weight

The first fix is the usual one: pushing the maximum on the basics. A press is not measured by the plates on the bar, it is measured by the stimulation that reaches the pectoral.

As soon as the load goes past what you control, the helpers step in. The front deltoid and the triceps push, the pectoral comes along and the weight rises. The set looks good and the stimulus has been split three ways.

The fix is to drop the weight to a number you can move with the same technique on the first and the last rep. That number, not your maximum, is the one that builds muscle. The guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/) explains how many sets the group needs at that load.

## Pushing with the chest, not the shoulder

Some people move big numbers on the bench press and have a flat chest. The shoulders and triceps move the weight, and the pectoral just passes through.

The right position starts before the first rep. The shoulder blades come together and down, the sternum lifts and the upper back stays on the bench. From that base, the arm pushes from the pectoral.

The arch has a clear limit. A slight arch with the glutes on the bench is a normal pressing position. Lifting the hips off the bench turns the press into a different exercise, with more risk and less range.

The elbows follow, somewhat tucked and never flared to a right angle. If you feel the front deltoid doing the work, check the shoulder blades before adding sets.

## The mind-muscle connection

Feeling the muscle while it works is directed attention, and it changes which part of the body does the work when the load is not maximal.

There is a simple test that needs no equipment. Put one hand on the opposite side of your torso and make the pressing motion with the other arm, with no weight. At the end of each rep the pectoral should feel tight under your hand. Repeat the motion as if it were a flye and you will feel the pectoral harden near the sternum.

With that reference, go back to the bench. It takes weeks to show up, but when it does, the same load lands in the right place.

## Flyes build mass too

The press moves the load away from the body on the horizontal plane. The flye performs the pectoral's other action: bringing the arm towards the centre of the torso, as in a hug. That angle builds too.

The usual mistake is treating the flye as a finishing detail, with little weight and twenty reps. A flye takes load and takes work close to failure, in a range of six to twelve reps.

In the technique, the elbows stay slightly bent and the range is controlled. In a flye the hands may finish close, with soft elbows. In a press, bringing the dumbbells together at the top adds adduction and breaks tension, so it is not the goal.

## Stretching between sets

Stretching the chest has two clear benefits: it allows a longer range in the flye and it reduces the stiffness that builds up over weeks of pressing. Many people also notice it helps them feel the contraction.

The third reason sometimes quoted, that stretching tears the fascia so the muscle can grow, is unconfirmed. You can stretch for the first two and leave the third aside.

The practical version: at the end of a press set, take two light dumbbells and set up in the starting position of a flye. Lower one centimetre every two seconds as far as you can go without pain. Let go and go back to the press.

## Pyramid up or pyramid down

Adding weight and dropping reps, or starting heavy and lightening, both work. The difference is how they spend your energy.

Starting light and going up is safer, but it spends part of the set on weights that do not stimulate. Starting heavy allows more reps at the top load, at the cost of a very thorough warm-up and more risk if technique fails.

The recommendation is to alternate the two schemes from time to time, so the body does not get used to one. If you pick the descending one, warm up thoroughly before the first heavy set.

## Do not repeat the same exercise

One hard-to-see cause of a plateau is doing three exercises that work the same thing. Barbell incline press, dumbbell incline press and machine incline press share an angle; adding them in the same session adds fatigue without new stimulus.

A complete session covers three groups: a press for the middle or lower chest, a flye and a press for the upper chest. Picking one from each gives real variety and avoids repeating the same pattern. With that rule, rotating exercises can last for months without running out of catalogue.

## Frequency: 48 hours between sessions

The chest needs time to repair the tissue you have worked. Given the size of the group and the weights involved, about 48 hours between sessions for the same muscle is a reasonable reference.

Training it twice a week, one hard session and one lighter, fits that margin and works well in most plans. What does not fit is repeating it every day and trusting the body to adapt.

The guide to [training frequency](/en/training-frequency/) explains how to spread the muscle groups across the week. The key is not the number of days, but that each session starts with the muscle recovered.

## Sets and reps

In an intense session, the useful range runs from four to six reps at the low end up to ten or twelve. Below four, the work looks more like strength than hypertrophy; above twelve, the load is too light if the goal is mass.

For sets, three or four exercises with three or four sets each is a manageable plan for almost everyone. Twenty-set sessions exist, and so do the people who cannot recover from them.

Testing a one-rep max in the middle of a session adds nothing to growth and adds risk. If you want to know where you stand, the guide to the [one-rep max](/en/calculate-one-rep-max/) has the formulas to estimate it without attempting it.

## Alternating basics and isolation

There is a practical problem that appears as soon as the weight goes up: the first press tires the triceps and the front deltoid, and the second press runs out of strength before the pectoral gets its share.

The solution is to slot the flye between the two presses. The pectoral keeps working, the triceps rests for a few minutes and arrives at the second press in shape. It is a simple way to keep the order without piling fatigue onto the same helpers.
