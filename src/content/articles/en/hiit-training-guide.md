The basis is simple and demanding at once: alternate short bouts of maximal or near-maximal effort with active or full rest. Heart rate climbs to the limit in the work intervals and drops just enough to repeat with quality.

Here is what HIIT is, what it is not, how to structure a session and six routines ordered by level. No need to end up on the floor every day: the point is to apply the right stimulus with some sense.

## What HIIT training is and what it is not

HIIT stands for High Intensity Interval Training. The goal is to push heart rate to the limit during work and allow a partial recovery before the next bout.

Forty minutes of burpees, squats and abs with barely any rest is not HIIT. That is a metabolic circuit, and you are probably doing it at moderate intensity, because maximal intensity cannot be held for that long.

Real HIIT is short. If you can do HIIT for an hour, all you are doing is getting very tired.

## Real benefits of HIIT

HIIT does not make up for a bad diet and it does not erase the weekend pizza. That said, the general evidence gives it clear benefits for health and performance.

The main practical benefit is efficiency. You can get cardiovascular adaptations similar to traditional low intensity steady state cardio (LISS) in less than half the time. That helps if you have under 30 minutes a day.

VO2 max is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in, transport and use per unit of time, and one of the best predictors of health and longevity. High intensity work is the most direct way to raise it, and it improves insulin sensitivity along the way.

HIIT produces more EPOC (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption) than traditional cardio. The effect is real and it lifts your metabolic rate after training, but the extra burn rarely exceeds 50 to 100 kcal. Its real value is in mitochondrial adaptation, not in burning calories on the sofa.

Where fat is concerned, high intensity training preserves muscle mass better than endless cardio, which helps genuine body recomposition. Without a calorie deficit in your nutrition you will not lose weight, no matter how many sprints you run.

## The key variables: work-to-rest ratio and intensity

For a HIIT session to work you have to set work time and rest time. If rest is too short, you cannot hold intensity. If it is too long, you lose the stimulus.

The most common ratios by level and goal:

- **1:3 or 1:4 ratio**, for beginners or maximal sprints: 15 seconds of hard work and 45 to 60 seconds of rest.
- **1:2 ratio**, for intermediates: 20 seconds of work and 40 of rest.
- **1:1 ratio**, for advanced lifters: 30 seconds of work and 30 of rest.

The acid test is the talk test. During the work interval you should be unable to string together more than two or three words without gasping. If you can hold a conversation about last night's episode, raise the resistance or the speed.

Working near the limit is not the same as reaching failure on every set. The guide to [muscle failure training](/en/muscle-failure-training/) helps you tell a one-off tool from a bad habit.

## Example HIIT routines, from beginner to elite

Always warm up for 5 to 10 minutes at an easy pace before any of these routines. Pick the one that fits your level and the equipment you have.

### Routine 1: stationary bike or elliptical

The low impact option for teaching the heart the stimulus.

- **Effort:** 20 seconds of fast pedalling at moderate resistance.
- **Rest:** 40 seconds of very easy pedalling.
- **Rounds:** 6 to 8.
- **Total:** about 8 to 10 minutes of effective work.

### Routine 2: bodyweight at home

No machines and no jumps, to protect your knees and keep the neighbours happy.

- **Effort:** 30 seconds of mountain climbers at top speed.
- **Rest:** 30 seconds of marching on the spot.
- **Rounds:** 8 to 10.
- **Total:** 10 minutes of effective work.

### Routine 3: treadmill

For anyone who already handles steady cardio and wants a step up in intensity.

- **Effort:** 30 seconds running at a fast pace, not an all-out sprint but demanding.
- **Rest:** 60 seconds walking at an easy pace or standing on the side rails.
- **Rounds:** 8 to 10.

### Routine 4: kettlebells

Excellent for the whole posterior chain: glutes, hamstrings and lower back.

- **Effort:** 20 seconds of explosive kettlebell swings.
- **Rest:** 40 seconds of full rest.
- **Rounds:** 10 to 12.

The swing is a ballistic movement, and the guide to [ballistic training](/en/ballistic-training/) explains why power is trained with acceleration rather than slow weight.

### Routine 5: outdoor sprints

Only suitable if you have good running technique and no joint issues.

- **Effort:** 15 seconds of sprinting at 90 to 95% of your capacity.
- **Rest:** 45 to 60 seconds walking very slowly until your breath returns.
- **Rounds:** 8 to 10.

### Routine 6: rowing machine or Air Bike

These machines involve the whole body and send heart rate through the roof in seconds.

- **Effort:** 30 seconds rowing or pedalling at full intensity.
- **Rest:** 30 seconds of very easy movement.
- **Rounds:** 10 to 15.

## The Tabata method and why almost nobody does it right

The original Tabata became famous for lasting only 4 minutes: 8 rounds of 20 seconds of work and 10 seconds of rest.

What usually goes unsaid is that the original study used Olympic athletes pedalling at a brutal intensity. The protocol demands work at 170% of VO2 max, in other words supra-maximal power.

In practice, most people doing a Tabata at home with squats are doing a moderate intensity interval circuit. Reaching that oxygen demand with bodyweight exercises is biomechanically impossible, so the name does not turn the session into Tabata.

## Typical mistakes that ruin a high intensity session

- **Skipping the warm-up.** Going from zero to a hundred without preparing muscles and joints is the fast track to a strain. Always warm up dynamically.
- **Turning it into a 45 minute session.** If the session stretches out, intensity collapses. A good HIIT leaves you finished in 15 or 20 minutes including the warm-up.
- **Doing it every day.** The central nervous system needs recovery. Five HIIT days a week only lead to overtraining and stalled progress.

If fatigue piles up, a [deload week](/en/deload-week/) brings the body back without losing what you gained. If you want low-impact cardio to alternate, the [swimming routine](/en/swimming-workout-routine/) fits between sessions; if you prefer running, the guide to [enjoying running](/en/enjoy-running-popular-races/) orders paces and weeks.

## Frequently asked questions about HIIT

### How many times a week should I do HIIT?

One to three sessions a week is the sweet spot for most people. Alternating it with strength training or low intensity cardio such as walking is the best strategy.

### Is fasted HIIT better?

There is no strong evidence that fasted HIIT burns more fat in the long run. Train when you have the most energy and feel best. If training without breakfast makes you dizzy and hurts performance, eat something first.

### Can I do HIIT if I am overweight?

Yes, but avoid high impact exercises such as jumps and sprints to protect your joints. Choose the stationary bike, the elliptical, the rowing machine or swimming.

### Does HIIT make you lose muscle?

The opposite. Because sessions are short, the hormonal environment they create favours muscle retention far more than endless moderate cardio.

### How long should a HIIT session last?

Not counting the warm-up and cool-down, the pure intervals should last between 4 and 20 minutes at most, depending on the protocol you choose.

HIIT means alternating near-maximal intensities with well calculated rest, respecting that rest and not repeating it every day. Adaptation happens while you rest.
