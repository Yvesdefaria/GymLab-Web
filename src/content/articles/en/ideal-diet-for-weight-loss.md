There is no single ideal diet. There is a structure that adapts to each person's kitchen, budget and schedule. Diets that work share three traits: a deficit you can sustain, enough protein to preserve muscle and a volume of food that fills you up.

## The four parts of the plate

The most useful template is a proportion. A protein source takes a quarter of the plate, vegetables take half, carbohydrate takes another quarter and fat is added in a small amount. Ingredients change, the structure does not.

Protein is the anchor. Without it, the plate fills with carbohydrate and fat and hunger returns sooner. Vegetables add volume and fibre for very few calories, and carbohydrate supports training. Fat is measured because it is the densest nutrient.

## The numbers that matter

A deficit of 300 to 500 kcal a day produces a loss of 0.5 to 1 % of body weight per week, a pace you can maintain. Protein stays at 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo and fibre at 25 to 35 grams a day. Those three numbers explain more than any single food.

For an 80 kilo person, daily protein is 128 to 176 grams. Fibre is covered with vegetables at two meals, whole fruit, legumes two or three times a week and whole grains. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) helps split the rest around training.

## A plate with numbers

One example main meal for that 80 kilo person: 180 grams of chicken breast, 200 grams of vegetables, 60 grams of dry rice and a spoonful of olive oil. It adds up to about 550 kcal and about 45 grams of protein.

That plate fits into a day of 2,000 to 2,200 kcal with room for breakfast and dinner. There is no need to weigh vegetables or count every gram of oil. With protein weighed and carbohydrate measured dry, the rest is adjusted by eye. The exact weights are a starting point: adjust portions every two weeks with the weight average and with how training feels.

## The plate changes with the day

On a hard training day, the carbohydrate quarter grows and fat comes down a little. On a rest day, carbohydrate shrinks and vegetables take up more space. Protein does not change: it is the part that protects muscle when the deficit tightens. The same structure works with fish, eggs, legumes or tofu in place of chicken.

Legumes solve a full meal for little money. A hundred grams dry provides about 20 grams of protein and plenty of fibre. The guide to [legumes in the diet](/en/legumes-in-your-diet/) explains how to cook them and how to pair them with a cereal.

## Adherence: the variable that decides

A perfect diet abandoned in three weeks loses to a normal diet kept for a year. Adherence is built with recipes you know how to cook, a repeatable weekly shop and room to eat out. It is also built with controlled hunger: protein, fibre and volume do more than willpower.

Cooking juicy chicken in bulk on Sunday saves decisions during the week. The guide to [juicy chicken](/en/juicy-chicken/) gives the exact temperature and safe storage. The one on [high protein lunches](/en/high-protein-lunches/) solves the meal at work.

## What you do not need

No single food guarantees the result. A burger and a beer fits as one planned meal; turning it into a weekly free day is what breaks the account.

Exercise preserves muscle and widens expenditure. The guide to a [fitness lifestyle](/en/fitness-lifestyle/) turns it into repeatable logistics, and the one on [weight rebound](/en/weight-rebound-after-dieting/) explains what happens when the plan is dropped.

The deficit remains the mechanism. The guide to the [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/) shows how to calculate it. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) remind you that no tub replaces a well built plate.
