The idea of raising loads progressively has been known and applied since antiquity. Greek mythology credits the first example to Milo of Croton, an Olympic wrestling champion and pupil of the mathematician Pythagoras (580-500 BC).

As a teenager, Milo decided to become the strongest man in the world and began lifting and carrying a calf every day. As the animal grew and gained weight, Milo grew stronger. When the calf became a bull, long-term progression let him lift it. That earned him the title of strongest man on earth.

## What the overload principle says

The increase in muscle size, tone and definition is a direct result of how much and how well you train over time. From beginner to Mr. or Ms. Olympia, the workload must rise gradually, in line with each person's physiological and psychological capacity.

The most effective technique for choosing the load is the **principle of staged variation**. It accounts for a basic condition: an increase in load must be followed by a period of reduction.

That **reduction phase** works as recovery. The body adapts to the heavier load and prepares to receive another increase. That is the adaptation and overload principle in action.

Each person responds differently to stress, so training must be planned around the goal and the individual adaptation level. If the load rises abruptly, it can exceed adaptation capacity and break the balance of the overload and adaptation cycle. When that happens, adaptation stops being optimal and injuries can appear, slowing or delaying the programme.

## Staged variation: raise and lower the load

The staged variation approach repeats a microcycle or training week in which resistance rises progressively. Then comes a **reduced-load** stage that secures recovery.

Each stage is more than one session. Overload is not increased every workout.

A single session provides too little stimulus to produce marked adaptations. Adaptation appears after repeated exposure to the same load.

Each stage is one week, each load change opens a new block and each block is the time in which the body uses and adapts to that load. The percentages above each stage are the suggested share of maximum load. Progress shows in the first three weeks, which is why the load is reduced in week four.

## One week of overload, day by day

This is how the body might respond to progressive increases.

On **Monday** a new microcycle starts with a higher overload. After the session, the body enters a **state of fatigue**: a physiological crisis, because it is not used to that stress level.

If the stimulus continues, by **Wednesday** the body begins to get used to the load and adapts over the next two days.

On **Friday** you will feel good and able to lift more. After the fatigue crisis comes an adaptation phase with some physiological improvement. In the second week the body feels comfortable physically and mentally, which signals that it is time to raise the overload and the adaptation level again.

Each phase of the microcycle adds improvements until the reduction arrives. That phase gives the body the time it needs to refill energy stores, restore psychological balance and clear the fatigue accumulated over the previous three weeks. The fourth stage moves from lower loads into a new increase.

## Why the same percentage gets heavier over time

Even if the load increase looks small, remember that strength also rises, so maximum loads go up and the percentage of maximum moves with them.

The first time you reach **80% of your maximum**, that 80% might be 60 kg. Three weeks later, through adaptation and strength gains, the 80% may have become 65 kg. By using progressively heavier loads over the long term, the percentage stays the same.

## How to apply it to your plan

Progression means raising the load for several weeks, accepting a deload week and starting again from a higher point, rather than adding plates every session.
