The sentence comes up in every diet conversation: if you eat more carbohydrates than you burn, the surplus turns into fat. The first part is true and the second needs nuance, because the body has several routes before it builds new fat.

## What happens to a plate of rice

Starch is broken into glucose in the intestine, passes into the blood and enters cells with the help of insulin. Muscle and liver store it as glycogen, and the rest is oxidised for energy. When glycogen is full and glucose keeps arriving, the liver can convert part of it into fat. That process is called de novo lipogenesis.

## Direct conversion is small

In a mixed diet, de novo lipogenesis supplies a small fraction of the fat that gets stored. Turning carbohydrate into fat costs energy, so the body prefers to oxidise carbohydrates and keep the fat that already comes with the food. Conversion rises when the surplus is large and lasts for days, especially with liquid sugar, and even then total energy balance remains the main factor. The route matters less than the weekly total, and the weekly total is easier to control when fibre and protein sit in every meal.

## Surplus and appetite do the work

When total calories exceed expenditure for weeks, the surplus is stored as fat whatever its source. Carbohydrates provide 4 kcal per gram and are easy to overeat in liquid form: soft drinks, juice, beer. They also push you to eat more when they arrive in products with fat and sugar, because they satisfy little per calorie.

Dietary fat is denser, at 9 kcal per gram, and is stored at a lower cost. That is why a surplus rich in fat adds weight sooner than the same surplus from carbohydrates. Protein and fibre satisfy more per calorie and make the deficit easier to hold.

## Insulin is not a switch

Insulin lowers fat release from adipose tissue after a meal and favours storage. That action is short and part of the normal cycle: it also curbs appetite and moves glucose into muscle. Saying that an insulin spike equals new fat ignores energy balance.

In a deficit, high insulin does not prevent fat loss; in a surplus, low insulin does not prevent fat gain. The guide to [insulin and bodybuilding](/en/insulin-and-bodybuilding/) develops that idea in more detail.

The response varies between people: anyone training strength has more glucose transporters in muscle and tolerates a carbohydrate meal better. Insulin sensitivity improves with training and worsens with inactivity and excess fat, and that matters more than the glycaemic index of one food.

## Glycaemic index as a tool

A low-glycaemic food raises glucose slowly and usually satisfies more. A high one rises fast and is useful before or during training, when available energy matters. Neither is good or bad on its own.

The index does not state the amount: a serving of watermelon has a high index and little glycaemic load. Fibre, protein and fat in the plate change the response. The guide to [carbs at night](/en/carbs-at-night-and-fat-gain/) dismantles the fear of the clock.

## What to watch in practice

Add up the daily total, cover protein, raise fibre to 25-35 grams and limit liquid calories. If the scale climbs, review the surplus before blaming the rice. None of this makes carbohydrates a free food: a surplus of any macronutrient adds fat, and carbohydrates are easy to overeat when they arrive with fat, sugar and no fibre. [Carb cycling](/en/carb-cycling/) and [loading and depletion](/en/carb-loading-and-depletion/) are ways of spreading those carbohydrates, not of skipping the sum.

The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) covers types and amounts, and the one on [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) avoids products that promise to burn fat without changing the diet.
