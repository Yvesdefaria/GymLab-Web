Before the first quadriceps set, the knee needs two things: temperature and range. The rest of the protection comes afterwards, with the load and with the way you lower. This page puts the first part in order and states plainly what a warm-up can and cannot do.

## What a warm-up can and cannot do

A warm-up raises muscle temperature, takes the joint through its range and gives you the first read on how the leg is responding that day. That improves the first set and stops you starting cold.

What it cannot do is armour the knee. Trouble shows up through poorly dosed load, through technique breaking down under fatigue and through pain ignored for weeks. The warm-up prepares; the rest is settled in the session.

That is why this routine swaps static stretching beforehand for dynamic reps. Stretching cold before lifting is a contested habit: there is no consensus that it improves performance, and it is certainly not what protects the joint. The guide on [warm-up and stretching](/en/gym-warm-up/) covers that general part.

## The circuit: calves, quadriceps, hamstrings

The order is short and specific. Twenty to thirty light reps of each exercise, with no rest between one and the next:

1. Standing calf raise with body weight or minimal load.
2. Squat with body weight or an empty bar.
3. Stiff-leg deadlift with an empty bar or two light dumbbells.
4. Squat again.

One round is usually enough. If you come in from the cold in winter or have spent an hour sitting, do a second. The stopping rule is simple: the leg feels active and your breathing is a little quicker.

## Why that order

The calves go first for an anatomical reason. The gastrocnemius arises on the femur, just above the knee, so ankle work also reaches the neighbourhood of the joint. The soleus stays in the lower leg and crosses only the ankle. That attachment to the femur is the reason to start with the calf.

The squat follows because it moves knee and hip together. The hamstrings close the circuit because they cross the knee from behind, and the second squat brings the focus back to the quadriceps with the leg already warm.

## The set you do not skip

The circuit does not replace the approach to the first exercise. Before the first working set, do at least one light set of the first quadriceps exercise of the day, with little weight and short of failure.

That set confirms the knee is responding and gives you the load reference for the first real set. If you have just trained hamstrings or glutes, you can skip the circuit: the legs are warm and repeating it only adds fatigue. The specific set still happens. The guide to the [glute warm-up](/en/glute-workout-warm-up/) applies the same logic: a short circuit and one approach set before loading.

## The four rules that matter most

Four things carry more weight than any warm-up.

**Well-dosed load.** The jump in weight between weeks matters more than the order of the exercises. Raise volume suddenly and the knee notices before the muscle does.

**Knee over foot.** On the way down, the knee follows the line of the foot. Let it drift inward and the load spreads badly, and the quadriceps loses part of the work.

**Controlled descent.** Lowering more slowly than you rise lets the muscle do the work and stops the joint taking the weight in one hit.

**Sharp pain.** If a stabbing pain appears as you bend, the set ends there. Training through it lengthens the problem instead of solving it.

## How to fit it into your week

The circuit goes before any quadriceps session and takes two or three minutes. If you train legs twice a week, you can repeat it both times: the cost is low and the first set improves. And if the session combines squat and leg press, the guide on [squat versus leg press](/en/squat-vs-leg-press/) sets the order.

If your knee has already given you trouble, a warm-up does not replace an assessment. The guide on [whether the squat is dangerous](/en/squat-safety/) reviews what is known about the joint under load, and the one on [quadriceps myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) makes clear what foot position can and cannot emphasise.
