The idea that the body accumulates toxins and needs a periodic clean sounds good and sells better. The problem is that it describes no known process. The body has an elimination system that works without holidays, and the cures that promise to switch it on have not shown that they add anything.

## A detox: what the liver and kidneys do

The liver turns substances arriving in the blood into more water-soluble molecules, and the kidneys remove them in urine. It is a system with several phases, enzymes and transporters, and it runs without a special diet. Skin, lungs and gut handle a small share of the exit.

When people talk about detoxing, they are talking about that system. There is no new organ that a green drink switches on, and no pathway that opens with a fast. What does exist is a liver overloaded by alcohol, high body fat or some drugs, and a kidney struggling with dehydration or high blood pressure. There the intervention makes sense, and it is not made of juice.

## Why a juice adds no step

No food introduces a cleaning route the body does not already have. Products sold as detox aids have no regulated definition, and when their claims have been assessed, the evidence did not support the effect. Some work as mild laxatives, which shows up in the toilet, not in the blood.

A long fast or a juice cure can lower weight through water and glycogen, and regain it afterwards. It can also cut the energy available for training and for meeting the day's protein. The result looks more like a temporary restriction than a clean-up.

## What does move the needle

Fibre is the most concrete lever. Around 30 grams a day, from vegetables, fruit, legumes and wholegrains, improves transit and feeds the gut bacteria. Less alcohol is the second: the liver processes it before almost everything else, and a daily glass is not a neutral habit. Enough protein covers repair and the immune system. Water keeps the kidney filtering, and the guide to [hydration for training](/en/hydration-for-training/) adjusts the amount to sweat.

Sleep counts too. A lack of sleep changes appetite, glucose and recovery, and no herbal tea compensates. With seven or eight hours, the system works better than with any supplement.

## The immune system after a hard session

Hard training leaves a window of several hours in which some defences drop. The evidence is not as clear-cut as it was repeated for years, and sleep, food and rest offset the effect. A weekend cure is not needed: what is needed is not chaining weeks of maximum volume without a deload. The guide to [vitamins for the immune system](/en/immune-system-vitamins/) reviews the nutrients with a real role.

## Fasting, the gut and cleaning

Intermittent fasting has effects on weight and glucose that are under study, but it is not a cleansing tool. Going sixteen hours without food does not clean the blood; it changes the energy substrate and the rhythm of meals. For people with demanding training it also makes the day's protein harder to hit. The guide to [intermittent fasting and weights](/en/intermittent-fasting-and-weights/) explains when it makes sense and when it does not.

## Why the symptoms blamed on toxins appear

Fatigue, heavy digestion, headaches or poor sleep have common, checkable causes: too little rest, stress, alcohol, a disordered diet or an illness that needs a diagnosis. Blaming a toxin without examining them delays the consultation. No single symptom diagnoses intoxication.

## When to see a doctor

Jaundice, very dark urine, swelling in the legs or abdomen, pain in the right side and tiredness that does not pass deserve a medical visit. So does high body fat with abnormal blood tests: the link between excess fat and metabolic disease is explained in the guide to [diseases from excess fat](/en/diseases-from-excess-fat/). A blood test says more than any over-the-counter toxin test.

## A pattern that works better than a cure

A plate of vegetables, a legume, fish or chicken, olive oil and fruit for dessert does more for the liver than any green juice. Sleeping well and moving daily complete what no label promises. If the goal is weight loss, the framework is the calorie deficit, not a weekend cure.

Products sold as light do not change that framework either. The label is read in the guide to [light products](/en/light-products-guide/), and the decision stays the same: what the package adds to the day.

## What you do not need to buy

A supplement does not replace the pattern. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) apply to detox products too: spending money on an extract while neglecting fibre, alcohol and sleep. The liver works better when daily life makes its job easy.
