Creatine has an odd relationship with blood tests. It is the supplement with the best safety record in healthy adults and, at the same time, the one that causes the most alarm on a routine panel. Both claims are true because they measure different aspects.

## What creatinine measures

Creatinine is a waste product made when muscle uses phosphocreatine for energy. It is produced at a fairly steady rate, passes into the blood, and the kidney filters it out into urine.

Labs use blood creatinine to estimate glomerular filtration rate, a number that approximates how much blood the kidneys filter per minute. It is an estimate, not a direct measurement, and it depends on muscle mass, age, sex and hydration.

## Why it rises with creatine

If you take creatine, there is more creatine in muscle and more creatinine in blood. The kidney filters the same, but the starting value is higher and the formula reads it as a slower kidney.

Creatine raises blood creatinine without changing kidney function. It is a lab number, not a sign of damage: the body converts creatine into creatinine, and the value rises because there is more raw material. If you have a blood test, mention that you take it; the doctor can use cystatin C or ask for a week off before repeating the test.

## Exercise moves the number too

Creatine is not the only factor that muddies the reading. In one study, aerobic exercise on the day of the test raised creatinine by around 20% and lowered estimated filtration by 10%, with no kidney problem behind it.

That is why it is worth not training hard in the 24 hours before a panel and arriving well hydrated. If the result looks odd and you had trained that day, repeating it after rest usually clears the picture.

## How it is checked properly

Cystatin C is a protein the kidney filters that does not depend on muscle mass. It is used more and more to estimate filtration when creatinine is unreliable, especially in very muscular or very thin people, in older adults, and in anyone on supplements that shift the number.

The other option is simple. Stop creatine for seven days and repeat the test. Creatinine returns to its starting value and estimated filtration recovers its usual figure. Seven days without the supplement do not change muscle or performance in any noticeable way.

What does not help is arguing with the doctor or insisting the supplement is harmless. The professional needs to know what you take in order to read the result, and that information is yours to give.

## Who should not take it without asking

In healthy adults, trials at the usual doses have found no kidney damage, and the longest follow-up covers several years. Some groups should ask before starting, such as people with diagnosed kidney disease, a transplant or a single kidney, pregnant or breastfeeding women, minors, and anyone taking medication that affects the kidney.

Dehydration matters too. Creatine does not dehydrate on its own, but a long hot session with little water can affect the panel. The guide to [creatine and cramps](/en/creatine-and-cramps/) covers water and sodium.

## The dose and the check

Three to five grams a day, every day, with no required loading. That dose keeps the tank full and does not demand more water than the body asks for.

If you take creatine continuously, one blood test a year with creatinine and cystatin C is a reasonable way to keep an eye on it. There is no need to measure anything else or to stop the supplement as a precaution.

## The context that does matter

Creatine does not damage a healthy adult's kidney, and it does not protect it either. Guidance that accuses it usually confuses creatinine with kidney function, and guidance that defends it unconditionally makes the opposite mistake.
