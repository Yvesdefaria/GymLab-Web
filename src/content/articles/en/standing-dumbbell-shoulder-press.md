## The trunk sets the limit

The anterior deltoid and the lateral deltoid move the arm above the head. The triceps extends the elbow through the second half of the range and the clavicular head of the chest assists the press. That is the visible part of the exercise.

The part that decides the set is different. With the dumbbells above the shoulder, the weight moves away from the centre of gravity and the spine has to stop the torso from drifting back. The abs, the glutes and the feet pushing the floor form a block that resists that extension. The longer the lever, the more the block costs.

That is why the standing press is an anti-extension lift. The deltoid is rarely the first thing to fail. What fails first is the ability to keep the ribs down while the weight rises.

## The starting position

The feet go at hip width, with the weight spread between the middle of the foot and the heel. The knees stay unlocked and the glutes are tight. Before the first rep, lower the ribs and brace the abdomen.

The dumbbells start at ear height, just outside the shoulders, with the palms facing forward and the thumbs closed around the handle. The elbows sit under the hands, pointing at the floor, at 90° or slightly less in the bottom position. If the elbow rises above that line, the forearm tilts and the dumbbell drifts off the path.

The grip stays the same for the whole set. Turning the palms toward the body halfway up looks for a more comfortable angle, and it also shares the work differently.

## The ribs mark the end

A clean rep ends with the arms extended, the dumbbells over the shoulder and the ribs where they started. If the ribs open and the lower back arches at lockout, the press has become a standing chest push. The weight no longer travels over the foot; it travels over the lower back.

That is the signal to end the set. There is no need to reach deltoid failure if extension shows up first. Lower the weight or cut the set, and note the rep where the change appeared. Over the weeks, that number becomes the real measure of progress in this lift.

## The head does not arch the lower back

A common correction says that tilting the head back on the way up endangers the lower back. That is not the cause. The head can follow the dumbbells without the spine changing shape. What arches the lower back is losing the abdomen and letting the ribs open.

The gaze stays forward, on a fixed point. As the dumbbells pass forehead height, the head moves just enough to let them through and returns to place. The neck does not need to extend for the weight to rise.

The confusion comes from joining two things that happen together. The lifter looking at the ceiling is usually the same one losing the brace on the last rep. The head follows the movement and the trunk decides whether the lower back arches.

## Standing or seated

Seated with a backrest, the bench carries part of the work and the shoulder gets more useful sets. Standing, the whole body takes part and the absolute load drops. Both versions train the same push with a different bill.

## Dose

Three sets of 8 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The load goes up when the last set ends with the ribs down and the same path as the first.

If the weight stalls and the brace holds, review the volume. If the brace breaks on the third rep, the weight is too much for the trunk you have today.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **Arching the lower back on the way up.** The chest gains height and the lower back pays the difference.
- **Flaring the elbows.** The shoulder loses a stable position and the dumbbells leave the drive line.
- **Bouncing at the bottom.** The dumbbell drops too far and the shoulder loses tension before the next rep.
- **Looking at the ceiling.** The neck extends and the head stops following the dumbbells.
- **Moving the feet.** A step mid-set unloads the trunk and turns the press into a push with momentum.
