To know your strength level, the ideal is to measure maximum dynamic strength. For that you calculate the one-rep max, or 1RM, the heaviest resistance you can overcome for one complete rep.

On the bench press, your 1RM is the weight that lets you complete one rep and not a second. That mark changes with the exercise and with the moment.

## What the 1RM is and what it is for

The 1RM does two jobs. In competition it sets your strength limit, and in training it lets you calculate working loads for different rep ranges.

Testing the 1RM directly demands a considerable effort and carries injury risk. That is why formulas estimate it from a set with more reps. The [1RM calculator](/en/one-rep-max-calculator/) applies one of them and avoids the maximal attempt.

If you want the details of the calculation, the guide to the [one-rep max](/en/calculate-one-rep-max/) explains the formula and the procedure step by step. The one-rep max for hypertrophy work is usually estimated with a formula and used as a reference, not as a fixed prescription.

## Why a percentage of 1RM fails in volume work

Many routines say something like "do 8 reps at 70% of your 1RM". The problem appears when you take it to the gym floor.

First, you would need the 1RM for every exercise. The 100% mark on a bench press does not match the one on a cable crossover. Even within the bench press, changing to an incline alters the number.

Second, the 1RM value is only valid for the first set. From the second set on, muscle fatigue changes the percentages.

Third, strength depends on factors that change daily: nervous system stimulation, recovery level, nutrition or the time of day. The plan may call for 90% of your 1RM today. If a crying baby kept you awake all night, that 90% no longer means the same thing.

That is why a fixed percentage is not very useful in a volume routine. It is useful in maximum-strength plans or for assessing strength every so often.

## How to choose the load without a 1RM

The percentage range is the same for everyone. In volume routines it usually sits between 70 and 80% of the 1RM. The specific load is not the same, because each person has a different strength level.

The practical answer is to go by feel. Hypertrophy work uses submaximal loads. Pick a weight that lets you do about 8 or 10 reps and reach muscle fatigue without touching failure on every set.

When you can do more reps with that weight, add load. That is the signal the stimulus has become too small.

Working in that margin has an advantage. It does not matter if you do one or two reps more or less on a given day. Little by little you learn what load each exercise needs.

## When the 1RM is worth it

The 1RM makes sense in routines built around maximum strength gains, where the working weight is calculated as a percentage of the max. It also works for assessing strength periodically.

A one-rep max for hypertrophy rarely justifies direct testing. A reference set near the limit and an estimate are enough.

Progressive overload remains the criterion for adding weight. The 1RM only tells you where you were when you measured it.

## How to progress by feel

Start with a weight that leaves you 8 or 10 reps and finish the set at the point of muscle fatigue, without reaching failure on every set. If you hit failure on the first set, you started too heavy.

When the same load lets you do more reps with the same technique, add weight in the next session. If technique breaks before you reach the planned reps, reduce it.

The [rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/) and the guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) help you calibrate that margin. With experience, choosing a load becomes faster and more precise.

A one-rep max for hypertrophy plan does not require a direct test. It needs a record of what you lift and the willingness to adjust when the weight is too light or too heavy.
