For decades the sardine was the cheap fish nobody bragged about eating. The low price had nothing to do with its value: it has to do with abundance, short fishing trips and a tin that keeps. Weighed as nutrients per euro, very little matches it.

## The tin, number by number

The figures are for sardines in oil, drained and with bones, and come from USDA FoodData Central. Draining matters: the oil in the tin is not part of the fish and leaves with two presses of a fork.

## Fresh or canned

A fresh sardine is another thing. According to BEDCA, 100 g of raw sardine carries 131 kcal, 20.4 g of protein and 4.8 g of fat. Fewer calories, less fat and no added salt. The tin, by contrast, concentrates the fish, adds oil or brine and keeps for months in the cupboard.

The fat difference between the two is not only the oil in the tin: sardines gain and lose fat with the season and the fishing ground, so two tins of the same size can differ. For an ordinary day the tin wins on convenience; for a meal with time, the fresh fish wins on flavour and on control of the salt.

Canning exists because sardines spoil within hours. The name is linked to Sardinia, where large colonies of the fish lived, and the canning industry popularised it in Europe and the United States at the start of the last century.

## The bones and the calcium

The calcium in the tin does not come from the fish: it comes from the bones, which soften in the heat treatment and are eaten without noticing. That is why a drained tin with bones carries 382 mg per 100 g and a clean fillet does not.

That detail makes it one of the cheapest calcium sources that does not depend on dairy. For anyone who avoids milk, or simply wants variety, one tin covers more than a third of the daily reference in a single meal.

Phosphorus and vitamin D travel with the calcium, and the guide to [foods with vitamin D](/en/vitamin-d-foods/) explains why that trio matters together. [Shellfish](/en/shellfish-nutrition/) follows the same mineral logic with less fat.

## Omega-3: one tin covers four days

EPA and DHA are the omega-3 fats the body actually uses, and the European reference for adults is 250 mg a day, about 1.75 g a week. A drained 100 g tin carries around one gram, so one or two tins a week meet the target without supplements.

That oil has a calorie cost. A hundred grams of drained tin is 208 kcal, and most of it comes from the fish fat and from what remains of the oil. If the goal is fat loss the tin is still a good option, but it counts as a fat, not as a lean protein.

## Salt: the only catch

A drained 100 g tin carries about 505 mg of sodium, around 1.3 g of salt. That is between a fifth and a quarter of the 5 to 6 g daily limit, and in a diet already tight on salt it weighs.

There are two ways out. The first is to drain and rinse the fish, which takes part of the brine with it. The second is to choose tins in water or with less salt when they exist, and balance the rest of the day. The guide to [limiting sodium](/en/how-to-limit-sodium/) has the rest of the hidden sources.

## Mercury: why sardines escape it

Large fish accumulate mercury because they eat other fish and live for years. The sardine sits at the base of the chain, feeds on plankton and lives briefly, so it accumulates far less. That is why it is recommended without the precautions that apply to bluefin tuna or swordfish.

The full rule, with the species worth limiting and a sensible frequency, is in the [fish consumption guide](/en/fish-consumption-guide/). Here the conclusion is enough: two or three tins a week are a safe option for almost everyone.

## How to prepare them

Fresh, a sardine needs little heat and little else. Grilled with coarse salt, two or three minutes a side, and a squeeze of lemon. Baked on a bed of potato. In escabeche, the traditional way of keeping them at home. Butterflied and grilled, it eats without bones.

From a tin, drained with a little tomato, onion and olive oil, it solves dinner in five minutes. On wholemeal toast it is a complete sandwich. With [eggs](/en/eggs/) and a salad, a whole meal.

What does not work is burying it under heavy sauces: the strong flavour is part of the point, and covering it with mayonnaise only adds calories.

## For training

Sardine protein is complete, with the nine essential amino acids, and its share per 100 g comes close to chicken. It is not a superior protein: it is a convenient, cheap one, with something chicken does not have, which is the omega-3 and the calcium.

In a training week, two tins cover the oily fish quota and leave the rest of the meals free. It pairs well with a carbohydrate that brings little fibre before training, such as [bread](/en/bread-and-muscle/), and with soluble fibre at breakfast, such as [oat bran](/en/oat-bran-for-weight-loss/), to close the day.
