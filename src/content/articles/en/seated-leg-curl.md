The seated machine has a reputation as the comfortable option. It lets you load knee flexion with the pelvis held and the torso stable, which makes it the best choice for training the hamstrings with the hip flexed. The rest is setup, range and tempo.

## How to do the seated leg curl

Set the back pad first. The knee joint should line up with the machine axis. If the knee sits ahead of the axis, the roller pulls on a diagonal and the range gets shorter; if it sits behind, the thigh pad loses contact.

Lower the thigh pad until it is snug, not tight. Its job is to hold the pelvis, not to pin the thigh. Place the roller just above the ankle, on the lower calf, never on the instep.

With your back against the pad and your hands on the handles, breathe in and bend the knees. The heels travel towards the glutes as far as the machine allows. Hold for one or two seconds at the top; the lowering phase takes two or three seconds.

## Why the flexed hip shifts the emphasis

The hamstrings are three muscles. The semimembranosus and the semitendinosus cross the hip and the knee; so does the long head of the biceps femoris. The short head of the biceps femoris crosses the knee only, and that difference is what gives the seated position its point.

With the hip flexed, the two muscles that pass over the hip lose some length and the short head is in a better position to produce force. The result is a bias towards the short head, not isolation: all three still flex the knee.

The gastrocnemius also crosses the knee and always assists knee flexion. Dorsiflexing the ankle increases its share, but does not create it. That is why the curl never isolates the hamstrings completely, and why the roller should be lowered with the foot relaxed. The full anatomy is in [hamstring anatomy](/en/hamstring-anatomy/).

## Full range ends at 130-140 degrees

Ninety degrees, with the shin vertical, is half the range. At that point the foot points at the floor and the muscle still has about thirty degrees to go. Full range is roughly 130 to 140 degrees, depending on calf size and machine design.

The last degrees are the hardest to fill and the first to be cut. If the machine loses resistance at the end, finish the rep with a pause before the roller unloads. Calling a rep that stops at vertical a full rep leaves out the part of the range where the short head has the biggest advantage.

## Tempo, feet and breathing

The two-to-three-second lowering phase is where most of the stimulus sits. At the top, the brief pause keeps the contraction and prevents a bounce.

The toes may point slightly in or out. That is an observation about position: if one side always turns the same way, check the machine setup and ankle mobility before drawing conclusions. The foot stays relaxed, without gripping the roller with the toes.

Breathing follows the movement: air before bending, release at the end of the range.

## Sets, reps and rest

This page's recommendation: three sets of 10 to 15 reps, with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. That is an isolation dose, not a maximum-strength one: the weight must allow the pause and the full range on every rep.

Start with the weaker leg and match the reps on the other. Progression is the usual one: add weight when the last set is clean, with the pause and no pelvic movement. If the range shortens when you add load, the load is still too early. The general criteria are in the [hamstring training guide](/en/hamstring-training-guide/) and in [eccentric hamstring training](/en/eccentric-hamstring-training/).

## Common mistakes

Too much weight is the first. With a heavy load the pelvis lifts, the back leaves the pad and the roller moves on momentum.

The second is stopping at 90 degrees and calling it full range. The third is placing the roller on the foot: the ankle bends, the calf takes part of the work and the hamstring loses tension.

The fourth is bouncing at the bottom. The controlled lowering phase is not cosmetic: it is where the muscle holds the load at its longest.

## Where it fits in the routine

It goes at the end of a leg or hamstring day, or as a second exercise after a deadlift. With the torso stable and the pelvis held, it lets you add volume without adding lower-back fatigue.

The lying curl, with the hip extended, changes the angle and the stretch; it is in the [lying leg curl](/en/lying-leg-curl/). The standing version, with the torso leaned forward, is in the [standing leg curl](/en/standing-leg-curl/).

The dumbbell version, for when there is no machine, is in the [dumbbell lying leg curl](/en/dumbbell-lying-leg-curl/). And if you want the other hamstring route, the hip one, the [Romanian deadlift](/en/romanian-deadlift/) is the reference. The weekly split is in [leg routines](/en/leg-routines/).
