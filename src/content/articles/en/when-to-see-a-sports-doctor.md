When a strange pain appears, almost everyone does the same: search online, try ice and rest and, if anything, take a pill. Sometimes it works. Other times you only buy time and problems.

In my case it started with low back pain. I thought it was the chair, I thought it was training, I thought it would pass. By leaving it and leaving it, it turned out to be a CAM-type hip impingement, a form of femoroacetabular conflict in which a bony bump rubs the labrum when the hip flexes. It ended in arthroscopy. I tell it because it sums up the idea: self-diagnosis is cheap on day one and very expensive at the end.

> **Notice:** This page is educational information and does not replace an assessment by a healthcare professional. It tells a personal experience, not a diagnosis. If the pain is intense, appears suddenly, wakes you at night or comes with loss of strength, get advice before you keep training.

## Why I tell this: back pain that came from the hip

Not all low back pain starts in the spine. The hip can send pain to the lower back when there is a femoroacetabular impingement. Stiffness when you put your socks on, discomfort after a long time sitting or pain when you rotate the hip are clues pointing that way.

That dull pain in the lumbar area was, for me, the first warning from the hip. I was slow to hear it because it fitted the easy explanation: too many hours sitting, too much load in [lower back work](/en/strong-lower-back/).

Strengthening glutes and core gave me my life back. I wish I had paid attention to the symptoms earlier, because a sports doctor would have found it in time.

## Signs that justify an appointment

You do not have to wait until you cannot move. These situations are already enough:

- Pain that does not improve in one or two weeks.
- Pain that limits walking or sleeping.
- Episodes that keep coming back, even when each one seems mild.
- Deep clicking, a feeling of blocking or loss of mobility when you squat or turn.
- Pain when changing direction if you play sports with pace changes and twists, such as padel, football or cross training.

Some signs admit no waiting: night pain that wakes you, limping, loss of strength or numbness. With any of them, the appointment is a priority.

## What self-diagnosing costs

Long absolute rest weakens you and delays recovery. Self-medication covers the pain, not the cause. And forums confuse: two people with identical symptoms can have opposite injuries.

I fell into the full combo: rest, anti-inflammatories and stretching by eye. The result was arriving late and needing hip arthroscopy. With an assessment at the start, the road would have been shorter in time, money and frustration.

The underlying problem is that an untreated femoroacetabular conflict can irritate the labrum and the cartilage. Other poorly managed injuries end in early osteoarthritis, chronic pain and repeated sick leave. The difference between treating on time and treating late shows up in the prognosis.

## What a sports doctor adds that a search does not

The specialist starts from a detailed clinical history and a directed examination. Depending on what they suspect, they will request specific X-rays, such as a Dunn view to see a CAM, an MRI for soft tissue or an ultrasound to guide an injection. It is not about running a thousand tests, but the right ones.

Their other job is to rule out the real source of the pain. A hip that hurts like a back, a knee that actually comes from the hip or an irritated nerve look very similar in the consultation and very different in treatment. In my case, that crossing between back and hip was the key: once it was explained well, everything fitted.

## From conservative treatment to arthroscopy

The first step is conservative: load education, physiotherapy, a strength programme and, where appropriate, ultrasound-guided injections. Anti-inflammatories adjusted to the case relieve pain, and physiotherapy addresses mobility, motor control and pain. The main character today is therapeutic exercise. When pain persists, the guide to [physiotherapy for chronic pain](/en/chronic-pain-physiotherapy/) explains how it is approached.

When there is mechanical damage that does not respond to that work, minimally invasive surgery is considered. Arthroscopy allows the CAM bump to be shaved, the labrum to be repaired and the joint space to improve. It is the right option in the right cases, never a shortcut.

In my case, after the operation, a solid strength plan made the difference between being operated on and living without fear again. That plan is the same one behind any well-built [return to training](/en/muscle-recovery/).

## A glute and core routine to protect the hip

This routine is an example. What counts is what your sports doctor or physiotherapist prescribes.

Do it two or three days a week, with a warm-up, a strength block and a cool-down.

- **Warm-up:** hip mobility in flexion and rotation, plus lateral band walks.
- **Strength:** glute bridge (3×10-12), light Romanian deadlift (3×8-10), step-ups (3×8-10) and plank with taps (3×20 seconds).
- **Cool-down:** breathing and gentle hip flexor stretches.

Strengthening the gluteus medius and maximus, the adductors and the core stabilises the pelvis and spreads the loads. When I dropped my guard with this routine, the discomfort came back. Here, [consistency](/en/how-to-stay-consistent-working-out/) weighs more than intensity.

## When to train again and what to avoid

You go back when you walk without pain, sleep well and tolerate the tasks of the day. Those three signs are worth more than any date on the calendar.

At the start, avoid deep hip flexion and rotation positions, repeated impact and sudden jumps in load. Begin with controlled strength and progress from there.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if my low back pain comes from the hip?** If it hurts when you flex or rotate the hip, or putting your socks on is hard, there is reason to suspect it. A sports doctor confirms it with an examination and specific X-rays.

**Should I see a physiotherapist or a sports doctor first?** With persistent pain, blocking, limping or recurring episodes, start with the sports doctor. The diagnosis and the plan come from there; physiotherapy and exercise follow as partners in the treatment.

**Is arthroscopy inevitable with FAI?** Not always. Many cases improve with strength and control. Surgery is considered when symptoms limit you and imaging confirms a mechanical conflict.

**Which exercises should I avoid at first with hip pain?** Deep flexion and rotation, such as a very low squat, and aggressive pivots. Impact too. Start with controlled, progressive strength.

## Conclusion

Do not diagnose yourself. Listen to the symptoms and leave the diagnosis to someone who does it every day. I learned the hard way: that low back pain was the hip and I arrived late by waiting.

If you are in doubt, book the appointment. For the base work you can do in the meantime, the guide to [back pain exercises](/en/back-pain-exercises/) orders the safest movements and their sets.
