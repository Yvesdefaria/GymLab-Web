It is a crunch with your back on a large ball, also called a fitball or Swiss ball. The support point changes, and so does the range: on the floor the movement starts with a flat back, and on the ball you can go lower and stretch the abs at the end of the descent.

That is why it shows up so often in group classes and training rooms: the equipment is cheap, takes up little space and needs nothing else.

## Setup

Sit on the top of the ball and roll forward just enough for your glutes to clear the edge. Feet stay flat on the floor, about shoulder-width apart, for stability.

Lie back until your hips are well supported on the ball. Your hands hold your head but do not pull on it or jam your chin into your chest.

## Execution

Without pulling on your head, contract the abs to lift the torso and take your shoulder blades off the ball as far as you can. Press your lower back into the ball and hold the peak contraction for one second.

On the way down, exaggerate the range at the bottom to stretch the abs. Return slowly, without bouncing: the ball's momentum is the fastest way to turn the exercise into a swing.

Breathe out as you rise and let air in as you lower. Holding your breath at the contraction feels like more tension, but it turns a set of twenty reps into an involuntary static effort. If you need air, take it at the top.

## Oblique variation

Alternate straight reps with reps to each side. As you rise, rotate the trunk and drive one shoulder towards the opposite knee. The rotation comes from the trunk; twisting the elbow does not change the work the oblique gets.

## What the ball really changes

An unstable surface does not make the exercise harder by itself. The ball forces you to stabilise the contact, and that demand spreads work across more muscles. In exchange, it lets you apply less force in the main movement: there is no fixed base to push against and part of the effort goes into not falling off.

The result is a tool for range and lower-back comfort. If you want overload, the floor or a decline bench with a plate gives you more room. If you want amplitude and a comfortable position for the back, the ball delivers.

## Sets and reps

The original article sets no sets or reps, and the exercise takes both: short slow sets with a pause at the contraction, or long sets chasing endurance. With no reference, start with three sets of fifteen to twenty reps and adjust as your technique holds up.

To decide how many sets to give the abs each week, the guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/) gives ranges by muscle group.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling on your head with your hands.
- Rising with momentum from the arms or legs.
- Lifting the lower back off the ball as you rise.
- Arching the back to gain range instead of stretching the abs.
- Rocking the ball to build momentum.
- Rushing the descent so the abs never reach the stretch.

## Where it fits in a routine

Swiss ball crunches are trunk-flexion work, like crunches on the floor. They can come first or last in the session; what changes is what arrives fresh. If it is your only exercise, three sets are enough. If you combine it, use two or three sets inside a block of three or four movements.

The ball gives more range than the floor; a plate on your chest raises the load. You cannot maximise both at once, so pick according to the goal of the session.
