The other fats come with nuance. This one does not. Partial hydrogenation created a fat that was useful for industry and hard to defend for the body, and research accumulated since the 1990s shows a consistent effect on cardiovascular risk. The good news is that spotting it needs no laboratory: reading is enough.

## What hydrogenation does

To turn a liquid oil into a semi-solid fat, industry adds hydrogen at high temperature and pressure with a metal catalyst. The process stops before it finishes, and at that point some fatty acids change shape: double bonds shift from cis to trans. The result is a more stable, firmer fat, convenient for pastry and frying.

Partial hydrogenation is what generates industrial trans fats. Full hydrogenation leaves a nearly saturated product with very few trans fats, which is why ingredient lists distinguish partially hydrogenated oil from hydrogenated oil.

## The harm depends on the dose

Cohort studies show a consistent link between trans fat intake and cardiovascular risk. The effect on lipids is twofold: LDL rises and HDL falls, a combination other fats do not produce with the same intensity. Trans fats are also associated with inflammation and endothelial dysfunction.

The relationship is dose-dependent. Replacing 2 percent of energy from trans fat with unsaturated fat is associated with lower coronary risk, and the benefit appears with small changes. At intakes close to zero, the room for improvement runs out.

What the evidence does not support is blaming trans fats for the cardiovascular epidemic of the twentieth century. The story is wider: smoking, blood pressure, sugar, sedentary living and changes across the whole food supply.

## Where they still appear

The classic sources are industrial pastry, biscuits, snacks, some spreads and fast-food frying. Regulation has cut them heavily in many countries, and in the European Union the limit has been 2 grams per 100 grams of fat since 2021.

That limit does not erase them. A product can comply and still supply trans fat in a small portion. The label remains the practical tool.

## The label that says zero

In several countries, a label may declare zero grams when the content per serving falls below half a gram. That is not a universal law, and it does not mean the product is free of trans fat: it means the figure is rounded.

The ingredient list is more reliable than the table. If partially hydrogenated oil appears, the product contains industrial trans fats, even when the table says zero. Reading that line takes ten seconds and does not depend on the country's rules.

## What not to fear

Refined oils are not toxic. Refining removes impurities and flavours, and the fatty acid profile stays that of the original oil. Nor is swapping margarine for butter a rule: most modern margarines contain no partially hydrogenated oil, and butter supplies saturated fat.

Natural trans fats in meat and dairy from ruminants are eaten in small amounts and do not show the same effect. The guide to [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) puts that intake in context.

The saturated limit is calculated on total calories, not on grams of fat. In a 2,500-calorie diet, 10 percent is 250 calories, about 28 grams of saturated fat. The guide to [dietary fats](/en/dietary-fats/) works through that sum with the other types.

## A rule you can follow

Buy food with a short ingredient list and cook more often. When you buy processed food, read that list before the table. The [anti-inflammatory diet](/en/anti-inflammatory-diet/) proposes a pattern that reduces ultra-processed food without turning it into a ban.

An occasional sweet does no damage. The aim is for industrial trans fats to be the exception rather than a daily part of the diet. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) remind you that no product makes up for a label left unread.
