The number of reps in a given set depends on your training goals. The weight you select also depends on those reps, so reps and resistance are two variables that go hand in hand.

As a general rule, pick a weight that helps you reach muscle failure on the last set of the exercise. From there, the range changes with what you are after.

## Optimal rep range: what it depends on

The relationship between reps and load is direct: more reps mean less weight, and the other way round. Fix the goal first, then adjust the load to that range.

The four fundamental goals are strength, size, a mix of both and muscular endurance. Each one has its own number of reps per set.

## Reps for strength: 3 to 6 per set

When the main goal is **strength**, the optimal range per set is **three to six** reps.

The aim is to overload the muscles with as much weight as you can handle for a very short period, which you cannot do with many reps.

Strength is defined as the maximum amount of weight you can lift once, and it is usually measured with maximum lifts at low reps, such as one, three or five-rep maxes. Conditioning the muscles to handle maximum loads is the key to developing strength.

## Reps for size: 8 to 12 per set

Muscles grow when they are trained with heavy loads that generate force on each rep, not with light reps on every set. The optimal range for maximum **size** is **8 to 12** reps.

This range combines a relatively heavy weight with enough reps to increase **time under tension**, meaning the time of contraction in each rep, and that makes the muscle fibre grow.

## Reps for strength and size: 6 to 8 per set

If you want a **mix of strength and size**, reps drop to the end of the strength range and the end of the hypertrophy range, around **6 to 8 reps**.

Dedicated lifters very often do sets of 8 reps to get the benefits of both strength and hypertrophy training.

## Reps for muscular endurance: 15 or more

Increasing muscular **endurance** is often confused with getting more **definition**.

You may have heard that a high number of reps, between 15 and 20 or more, helps you look more defined. The reality is less simple. **Getting more defined requires losing body fat**, and that comes from healthy eating habits and exercise.

Training in the 8 to 12 range is effective for developing definition, as much as or more than sets of 15, 20 or more.

Sets of 15 or more reps are ideal for increasing muscular endurance, which helps above all if you compete in endurance sports such as cross-country running, cycling or swimming.

## What changes in the muscle fibre with each range

With **few reps** you get a large myofibrillar increase (type II fibres) in the cells and a small increase in muscle fibres. With **more reps** you gain endurance (type I fibres).

Low reps (1 to 5) do not have to make you bigger: they bring a strength increase, which comes from adaptations in the muscle fibres. That explains why some weightlifters look thin and yet have a lot of strength.

With **medium reps** (6 to 12) the adaptations happen mostly in the muscle fibres. That is why this kind of rep is more advisable for bodybuilding and hypertrophy: you gain size and strength, although the strength increase is not maximal.

With **higher reps** (12 to 20) the changes are basically metabolic, because they favour aerobic work: they improve muscular endurance at the expense of strength.

One example: with 6 to 8 reps you get a myofibrillar increase and gain much more strength and hypertrophy. With 8 to 12 a fibrillar increase still exists, but it is smaller than with 6 to 12 and much smaller than with 1 to 5.

The advantage of 8 to 12 is that this regime gives you maximum hypertrophy, the best way to gain size if strength is not the main goal. You also gain strength, but not like with 1 to 5.

As the number of reps goes up, the load goes down, and the other way round.

## Why variety in rep ranges matters

You do not need to train every muscle group with the same frequency or follow the same rep ranges across your whole body.

Some groups respond better to high reps than others. The back responds better to more reps than the chest, which is why these two groups are trained differently. Trial and error is the best way to find what works for you.

Whatever the goal, it is essential to **change your rep ranges** from time to time. If you train your arms with 10 sets of 10 reps a week, they will have adapted to that range within the week and will stall.

Work at different times with fewer reps (6 per set) and with more reps (12 to 15 per set). Even though those ranges lean towards strength and endurance, the best way to make continued gains is to combine them with a range that promotes hypertrophy. The same applies when you train for strength.

There are many ways to change or cycle different rep ranges: it is what we know as **periodisation**.

## In short: which range to pick

3 to 6 reps for strength, 6 to 8 for strength and size, 8 to 12 for maximum size and 15 or more for endurance. Change ranges from time to time and adjust the load so you reach failure on the last set.
