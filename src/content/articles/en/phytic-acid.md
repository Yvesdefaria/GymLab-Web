Phytate, the salt form of phytic acid, sits in the seed to feed the embryo when it germinates. The human body does not make it and does not keep a reserve that runs down over days without it. What is eaten is partly absorbed, fermented in the colon or excreted, and that double life explains why it was treated as a problem for years and is studied as a substrate today.

## What it is and where it sits

It is an inositol molecule with six phosphate groups, able to trap positive charges. Whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds are the usual sources; sesame and wheat bran concentrate a fair amount. The content falls with processing, but it does not disappear in a boil.

## The side that subtracts: minerals

The six phosphates bind strongly to iron, zinc, calcium and magnesium. That bond forms complexes the small intestine cannot absorb, which is why a diet heavy in whole grains and light on animal protein can worsen iron and zinc status. The effect depends on the amount, the rest of the plate and the person.

A plate of lentils with pepper and lemon makes better use of iron than the same plate without vitamin C. An omelette with cheese adds calcium and protein that offset part of the loss. In a varied diet phytate rarely causes a deficiency on its own; in vegetarian diets with many legumes and few fortified foods it is worth checking ferritin and zinc now and then.

## The side that adds: colon bacteria

In the colon, phytate that was not absorbed feeds the microbiota. Bacteria ferment it and produce short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the intestinal lining. The effect is real and modest, and it does not turn phytate into a treatment.

Studies linking phytate to cancer, diabetes or heart disease are mostly observational or done in the lab. They do not show that eating more phytate prevents anything. The ability to bind heavy metals described in a test tube is not chelation therapy either.

## The three levers

Soaking is the simplest. Leaving legumes and grains in water for eight to twelve hours and throwing that water away removes part of the phytate, especially in split grains and small legumes. Changing the water halfway helps a little more, and rinsing before cooking removes what the soak pulled out.

Sprouting activates the grain's own phytases and removes more phytate than soaking alone. Two or three days are enough to notice the change in texture, and sprouted grain cooks like dry grain.

Fermentation is the strongest lever for bread. Sourdough and fermented legumes use the phytase of yeast and lactic acid bacteria, which breaks phytate down before baking. A wholemeal sourdough keeps less phytate than the same bread without fermentation.

Heat alone does not remove phytate. Boiling rice or pasta destroys some phytase, not all the phytate, and cooked grain keeps much of its mineral-binding capacity. Baking bread without fermentation barely changes the phytate level. The idea that cooking destroys almost all phytate is false.

## Where it fits in the diet

There is no need to avoid legumes, oats or sesame. The sensible strategy is to soak, sprout or ferment what you can, spread mineral sources across the day and avoid taking an iron or zinc supplement next to a plate loaded with phytate.
