The wrist is the last link between the forearm and the bar. Its position does not change the path of the elbow, but it does share the work between the biceps and the forearm muscles, and it decides how much strain the joint takes on every rep.

## What the wrist does in a curl

The biceps does not attach to the wrist, so the angle of the hand does not alter elbow flexion. What changes is the length of the forearm muscles and the distance between the bar and the elbow axis.

A wrist bent forward shortens the lever and brings the weight closer to the elbow. A wrist bent backward lengthens that lever and leaves the weight further away. The difference shows in the load you can move and in the discomfort that appears at the end of the set.

## The three positions

**Neutral.** The hand continues the line of the forearm, with the joint straight. It is the position in which the wrist transmits force most efficiently.

**Flexed.** The hand bends toward the palm during the lift. It is what many people do when chasing one more rep.

**Extended.** The hand bends backward, with the fingers pointing at the floor at the bottom. Some coaches recommend it to take the forearm out of the equation.

## Neutral is the recommended position

Locking the wrist in a neutral position strengthens it mechanically and shifts the emphasis to the biceps. The result is that you lift more weight, work the muscle harder and punish the joint less.

That is why this page's recommendation is the same for any barbell curl: a neutral wrist through the whole range, without bending it on the way up or down. And the heavier the load, the more reason to lock it: heavy sets are where the wrist bends when strength runs out.

## A flexed wrist does not change the biceps and tires the forearm

Mechanically, flexing the wrist does not affect the biceps contraction. It can make the exercise slightly easier because the weight sits a little closer to the elbow, which is where the feeling that it helps on the last rep comes from.

The forearm pays for it. The wrist flexors are small and give out before the biceps, so the set ends when the hand fails and not when the muscle you wanted to train fails. The joint gains nothing either: the forced position adds tension on every rep.

## An extended wrist buys isolation with risk

Extending the wrist backward reduces forearm involvement and can isolate the biceps more. That is why some coaches propose it.

The problem is twofold. The amount of weight you can use drops sharply, because the lever gets worse, and the wrist joint takes a strain it is not built to handle under load. A light curl tolerates it; a heavy one does not.

## How to choose in practice

Use the neutral position as the rule and lock the wrist as soon as the load gets demanding. You can try the other two deliberately, on a light set with few reps, to feel the difference. What you should not do is discover at the end of a heavy set that the wrist bent on its own.

If you can only lift the bar with a flexed wrist, the weight is too heavy for that exercise. Lower the load and recover the neutral position before adding kilos again.
