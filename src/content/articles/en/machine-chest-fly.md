## The difference from the pec deck

On a lever machine, each arm pushes a grip attached to a lever. The elbow does not rest on anything and the hand can pull on the grip, so the arm moves like a seated crossover. On the [pec deck](/en/pec-deck-fly/), the forearm and elbow rest on a pad and the movement comes from the shoulder alone.

The difference matters because it changes the lever. With the elbow on a pad, the arm stays fixed and the shoulder does all the work. With the grip in the hand, the elbow can bend a little more when the weight bites and the range shortens without telling you. On both machines the chest makes the same gesture; what changes is how much room the arm has to adapt.

## Three fly stations

All three options bring the hands together in front of the chest. The equipment decides where tension lives and how much control you have over the path.

The cable station gives the most room to adjust the angle, which is why it has its own pages in the [cable crossover](/en/high-cable-crossover/) and the [flat-bench cable fly](/en/flat-bench-cable-fly/).

## Seat height and handle height

Set the seat so the handles sit at chest level. If they sit higher, the shoulder starts the range above its line and the deltoid takes the first push. If they sit lower, the arm starts below and the chest loses the stretch at the start.

Seat height does not change which fibres work. The chest has two heads, clavicular and sternocostal, joined to a single tendon, and both contract on every rep. What changes with height is the position of the shoulder relative to the resistance, and that moves the point where the exercise costs more.

That is the useful detail: the machine does not separate one region of the muscle, but the seat decides where it bites. Try two heights with a light weight and keep the one that lets you bring your hands together without the shoulders rising.

## The path and the hold

Start with the arms open and the elbows slightly bent, well short of 90 degrees. Keep that angle through the whole range: if the elbow closes as you push, the exercise turns into a press and the chest loses the fly gesture.

Bring the grips together in front of the chest until they almost touch and hold the position for one second. Without rolling the shoulders forward and without lifting the back off the pad. Then return slowly, without letting the weight stack rest between reps.

The hold is the part most often skipped. Without it the weight bounces and the point of peak contraction passes without work.

## The dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 or 90 seconds of rest. The load is chosen so the hold completes on every rep and the back stays against the pad.

Because it is an isolation movement with a fixed path, you can take it close to failure without a partner: the lever stays where you leave it and there is no bar to rescue. That is the machine's advantage and also its trap, because the weight can be dropped without technique noticing. The guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains when going that far pays and when it does not.

Add load when the 15 reps come out with the full hold and the elbows at the same angle from the first rep to the last.

## Common mistakes

- **Seat too low.** The grips sit above the shoulder and the range shortens.
- **Closing the elbows as you push.** The fly gesture turns into a press.
- **Rolling the shoulders forward.** The chest loses position and the work shifts to the deltoid.
- **Letting the stack rest.** Tension disappears exactly at the most stretched point.
- **Half reps.** Opening only a third of the range leaves out the part that stretches.

## Where it goes in the session

It is an isolation exercise and goes after the presses, when the chest already carries load and the shoulder welcomes a fixed path. It also works as the first station in a machine session or when the shoulder asks for a guided range.
