Among people who train with weights there is a belief that overtraining is common and that anyone who trains has lived through it. Reality is more modest: it is not as frequent as many think. What is true is that training too much, or with too much intensity, leads there.

Two concepts that get confused deserve separating. Functional overreaching leaves fatigue that clears in days with rest. Overtraining persists for weeks and asks for changes to the plan, not a nap.

## What overtraining is

Overtraining is a state of exhaustion that slows progress in the gym or, at worst, reverses it. To gain strength and muscle you have to train hard and stay consistent, and you also have to raise the load progressively. The problem appears when intensity climbs too high, rest falls too short or both happen at once.

[Adaptation to weight training](/en/how-the-body-adapts-to-weight-training/) needs a dose and a recovery. Without both, the stimulus turns into wear.

## The effects of overtraining by system

### Nervous system

Overtraining affects the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. The usual signs are:

- Higher resting heart rate.
- Lack of appetite.
- High blood pressure.
- Weight loss.
- Trouble sleeping.
- Irritability.
- Earlier fatigue.

If you have more than one of these symptoms, review the routine as soon as you can.

### Hormone levels

The literature describes overtraining as lowering testosterone and raising cortisol. Hormones play a central role in building muscle, so that imbalance slows progress. High cortisol together with low testosterone forms a catabolic combination that favours muscle loss.

### Immune system

Overtraining can reduce antibody and lymphocyte levels, which makes you more vulnerable to illness. If you get sick, you miss sessions and progress stalls exactly when you were chasing it hardest.

### Metabolism

- Microdamage in the muscle.
- Chronically low glycogen levels.
- Slow, weak muscle contractions.
- Excessive lactic acid accumulation.
- Extreme [soreness](/en/delayed-onset-muscle-soreness/).
- Damage to tendons and connective tissue.

## Weights or cardio: where it shows up first

Any form of overtraining is bad. The one caused by weight training tends to run deeper and last much longer than the one that comes from cardiovascular exercise.

There are three reasons. To grow, muscles need to recover from the last session; work them before that happens and you tear tissue that has not rebuilt yet. Weight training taxes the nervous and hormonal systems harder. And it can send a beginner down the wrong road, spending on supplements they do not need or considering worse shortcuts.

Only competitive athletes, such as swimmers, runners and cyclists training two or more hours a day, run a serious risk of cardiovascular overtraining. For the average person it is far easier to overtrain with weights, and the effects can be more serious.

## How to tell if you are overtraining

If you listen to your body, you often see the signs before they get serious. Losing interest in training, sleeping badly, feeling weak or irritable are reasons to ease off. A [deload week](/en/deload-week/) or a few days of rest usually settles the warning.

The second tool is your training log. If progress stalls for one session, you had a bad day. If the next session is even worse, something is off.

A concrete example. In one session you do 5 reps of the deadlift at 100 kilos. Next time you are due to progress and you get the same 5: fine, it happens. But if the third session only gives you 3 or 4, it is time to ask what is going on. Spotting that requires a training log you actually keep.

## An eight-question self-check

This questionnaire is a self-check, not a diagnosis. Answer honestly:

- Are you always tired?
- Are you losing muscle size?
- Have you lost strength?
- Are your muscles tense?
- Has your appetite dropped?
- Do you struggle to fall asleep?
- Do your sessions run out of energy?
- Have you lost motivation?

If you answer yes to more than three questions, you may be overtraining. If you answer yes to more than five, it is very likely. In both cases the practical answer is the same: cut the load, sleep and review the plan.

## Four blocks to prevent it

### The right training volume

Working out the right volume is easy at the start. You learn it with common sense and with your own recovery capacity.

For a beginner, the goal is to improve every session. If that is not happening, volume or intensity is too high. For an advanced lifter, improving every session is not possible, so you alternate loading periods with [deload weeks](/en/deload-week/) that take a step back and then climb again from a higher peak.

[Training volume](/en/training-volume/) and how close you go to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) are the two dials that matter most in that sum.

### The right nutrition

- Do not skip breakfast if you are trying to gain size.
- Never go hungry. Muscle is an expensive tissue and you have to feed it often with quality food.
- Eat your biggest meal of the day within the hour after training.
- Keep glycogen tanks full. Lowering carbs can make sense for definition, although training without that extra energy brings overtraining closer.

### Rest

Rest is essential to avoid overtraining. Avoid late nights and sleep about seven hours a night, always at the same time. The guide on [how much sleep you need to progress](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) goes into the detail.

As a general rule, leave at least one day between two consecutive sessions. If you do not, do not train the same muscle group on back-to-back days.

### Periodisation

[Periodisation](/en/training-periodization/) gives you flexibility to adjust the training regime. You can work in cycles shorter than a month or longer.

In the short term, regulate intensity in cycles. After four or six weeks of high intensity, ease off and reduce the load for a couple of weeks. Those lighter periods bring two benefits: the body rests and moves away from overtraining and injury, and the mind breathes too, which keeps motivation alive.

Intense sessions are hugely demanding, and the lighter period stops you from burning out. In the long term, change the focus of the programme by season: size in winter, definition in spring, maintenance in summer and a low-intensity block when rest is due.

How you split the cycles is your call. What matters is that variation sits in the plan from the start.

## The signal that counts

Overtraining shows up in the trend across several weeks. A log, a couple of honest questions and a deload week on time settle almost everything.

When the fatigue stays for months, it is time to reduce, sleep and build again.
