## What myostatin is

Myostatin is a protein that restrains muscle growth. It is produced in skeletal muscle and acts as a limit: when the signal is high, tissue grows more slowly; when it falls, muscle has more room. It is part of the system that keeps muscle size within a range. It belongs to a family of signalling proteins that regulate tissue growth, and muscle is where it acts.

The name suggests a door that opens. In muscle, myostatin works as a brake that is released. The difference matters when someone sells a supplement that claims to do exactly that.

## The mice without the gene

In 1997, a research team deleted the myostatin gene in mice. The animals grew with twice the muscle mass and larger fibres. The finding mixed hypertrophy, the growth of each fibre, with hyperplasia, an increase in fibre number.

That result is the basis of almost all the interest. The animals also carried less fat on the same food, a detail that opened the door to studying obesity. It is also the basis of almost all the exaggeration: a mouse without the gene is not a human taking a capsule.

## The drug pipeline

Serious attempts to block myostatin are drugs, not supplements. Antibodies against the protein have reached clinical trials in people with muscle diseases, with results that have not produced an approved medicine. Follistatin and the myostatin propeptide are proteins that bind it and neutralise it; they are also being studied as therapy.

None of those routes is oral or intended for a healthy person who trains. They are large molecules, given by injection, and their target is a disease, not a bench press record. The clinical interest is in muscular dystrophy and in muscle loss from illness.

## Why no consumer blocker exists

A supplement would have to reach muscle, bind myostatin and reduce its signal in a measurable way. No over-the-counter product has shown that in humans. The proposals in circulation, such as glucosamine or algae extract, have no published trials showing the effect.

There is no dose to copy either. The dosing table that circulates online does not come from a trial. It is an extrapolation. That is why this page does not reproduce it.

## What you can do

Strength training is the most direct stimulus you have at hand. Studies in people associate resistance exercise with a lower myostatin signal, and regular activity keeps that brake below inactivity. The signal is transient and returns to baseline after recovery. Prolonged rest does the opposite: it raises myostatin and speeds up muscle loss.

The training dose in the [volume](/en/training-volume/) guide is 9 to 20 sets per session for the large groups and 6 to 16 for the small ones, spread across the week. [Progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) supplies the other half: without more weight or more reps over time, the stimulus stops being new.

The other lever is not overdoing it. Excessive training without rest raises cortisol and can increase myostatin instead of lowering it. Sleep and a sensible volume count as much as the session. A session is a signal; the weeks of sessions are what change the tissue.
