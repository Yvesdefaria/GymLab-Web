Sticking to a diet for months does not depend on willpower. It depends on what is in the fridge at nine on a Tuesday night, on whether a written list exists and on whether anyone checks the result once a week. Diet discipline is logistics, and logistics can be built.

## Discipline is a full fridge

Willpower runs out through the day and arrives at the end of it with little left. The fridge does not argue. When protein is cooked, rice sits in a container and fruit is in sight, the easy decision matches the right one.

That does not turn eating into a punishment. It means the important decisions are made once, in the shop and in the kitchen, instead of ten times a day in front of a shelf.

## The shopping list

The first step is picking three or four meals that can be repeated and writing down their ingredients. With that list you shop once a week and buy what is needed to cover the protein, fat and carbohydrate the plan asks for.

The list prevents two problems at once: forgetting the protein and buying too much out of hunger. If something is not on the list, it stays in the shop, because this week it is not needed. The guide to [bodybuilding nutrition](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-basics/) gives the figures the list has to cover.

## Preparing ahead

Cooking double twice a week handles most of the work. A tray of chicken or fish, a pot of rice or potatoes and a dish of roasted vegetables cover several meals without repeating the same recipe every day.

Containers go at eye level in the fridge, and portions that will not be eaten within three days go to the freezer. Cooked protein keeps three or four days in the fridge, so the freezer covers the rest of the week. Snacks are prepared too: washed fruit, yogurt, boiled eggs and nuts in small portions. The basic [nutrition guide](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-guide/) organises the rest of the food groups.

## The environment decides

What is in sight gets eaten more. A bowl of fruit on the counter and vegetables at eye level change decisions without effort; what helps goes within reach and the rest can stay where it is.

Shopping hungry is another way to sabotage the list. A meal before walking into the shop and a route through the real-food aisles are enough to leave with what was planned.

## The weekly review

Fifteen minutes a week is enough to review what happened. Weekly weight is compared with the previous week, not with yesterday; the plan is compared with what was actually eaten. It also helps to note which meals failed and why.

That review produces one change for the following week: prepare earlier, buy more protein, leave the snack ready. One change a week holds; five at once do not. Write the review down, even in three lines, because a vague intention gets forgotten and a written task gets done. The guide to [staying consistent with training](/en/how-to-stay-consistent-working-out/) applies the same logic to sessions.

## The 80/20 without moralising

Eighty per cent of meals are planned and twenty per cent are left free. That proportion allows a dinner with friends or a meal out without breaking the plan, as long as the weekly total holds.

One meal does not ruin six days of work, and a weekend is not paid back with fasting or extra cardio. The guide to [cheat meals](/en/cheat-meals/) explains how to fit that meal into the weekly budget without turning it into a binge.

## When a day goes wrong

A bad day is fixed at the next meal, not next Monday. Returning to the plan at the following intake is more useful than making up for it with a fast or an extra session that leaves the rest of the week exhausted.

It is also worth checking whether the slip was logistics. When nothing was ready, the fix belongs in the fridge, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) recall that no jar rescues a badly planned shop.
