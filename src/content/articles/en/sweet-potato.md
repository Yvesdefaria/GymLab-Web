Sweet potato, boniato and camote are names for the same tuber, Ipomoea batatas. The flesh can be orange, white or purple, and the skin runs from white to dark purple. The orange variety carries the most beta-carotene, the pigment the body turns into vitamin A.

In the kitchen it behaves like a sweet potato. Roasted or boiled it works as a side, in salads, in purées and in doughs. Its advantage over potato is not weight loss: it brings more fibre, more potassium and an amount of beta-carotene that potato does not have.

Raw, the same tuber brings 86 kcal and 337 mg of potassium per 100 g, because it keeps the water that cooking evaporates. That difference explains why 100 g of baked sweet potato has a higher nutrient concentration and also more calories per bite.

## Glycemic index depends on the method

The version in circulation said sweet potato has a low glycemic index and compared its 70 with the 82 of potato. The second part is right and the first is not: 70 is a high index. Cooking changes the number. Boiled, sweet potato stays at 44 or 50, a low value. Baked it climbs to 70 because starch gelatinises and water evaporates.

That nuance matters if you watch the glucose response. A baked sweet potato eaten alone raises blood glucose more than the same tuber boiled, and considerably more if it is very ripe. Pairing it with protein, fat or vegetables slows the rise. The [carbohydrate](/en/carbohydrates/) guide explains how to place these servings around training, and the [pre-workout meal](/en/pre-workout-meal/) page sets the timing.

## Potassium and beta-carotene

A medium sweet potato of 150 g brings between 500 and 700 mg of potassium depending on whether it is boiled or baked. Potassium helps keep blood pressure in check when the diet carries too much sodium; the page on [how to limit sodium](/en/how-to-limit-sodium/) develops that relationship.

Beta-carotene is provitamin A and the table reflects it. A 200 g serving covers an adult's daily vitamin A need comfortably. No tuber prevents cancer on its own, and diets rich in beta-carotene are associated with better health because they tend to include more vegetables and fruit, not because one food performs magic.

## Glycogen and water, a 1 to 3 ratio

Carbohydrate is stored in muscle as glycogen, and each gram drags about 3 g of water with it. That is why a diet with enough carbohydrate keeps the muscle full and recovers better between sessions. Sweet potato fits that role with 20 g of carbohydrate per 100 g, a moderate figure that leaves room for rice or bread in the same meal.

## How to cook it

In the oven, whole and with the skin, at 200 °C for 45 to 60 minutes if large. In the microwave, pierced with a fork, 8 to 10 minutes; it is the quick route when time is short. Boiled, in chunks, 20 minutes. On the griddle, in one-centimetre slices, it works as a side.

The skin stays. It brings fibre, potassium and quercetin, and protects the flesh from drying out. Wash it well and cut it only if it is hard or damaged. To lift the sweetness, a little cinnamon or curry is enough; sugar is not needed.

## Buying and storing

Choose firm pieces without wrinkles, bruises or soft spots. They keep in a cool, dark place for three to five weeks. The fridge does them no favours: below 12 °C the flesh is damaged and a hard patch appears that will not soften when cooked.

## When it fits in your diet

Before training, 150 g of sweet potato with a protein source two or three hours before the session is a complete meal. Afterwards, the same serving with egg, fish or legumes restores glycogen and adds fibre. [Peanut butter](/en/peanut-butter/) brings the fat and protein missing from a quick breakfast, and [avocado](/en/avocado-in-your-diet/) covers the fat when you do not want bread.
