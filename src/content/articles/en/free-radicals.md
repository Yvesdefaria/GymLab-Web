Oxygen is essential and, at the same time, capable of generating reactive molecules. That tension explains why the body does not eliminate oxidants but uses them and keeps them under control. Health depends on balance, not absence.

## What a free radical is

A free radical is a molecule with an unpaired electron. That condition makes it unstable and reactive: it takes an electron from another molecule and leaves it altered. Oxygen forms several of these species, known together as reactive oxygen species.

Superoxide and the hydroxyl radical are radicals. Hydrogen peroxide is not, although it is reactive and belongs to the same group. The distinction matters because not every oxidant is a villain and not every stable molecule is harmless.

Their life is short and their action is local. A radical generated in a mitochondrion does not travel the body causing havoc; it reacts near where it is born.

## The body makes them on purpose

White blood cells produce oxidants to destroy bacteria. Nitric oxide, also a radical, regulates blood vessel tone. Insulin signalling and muscle contraction use reactive molecules as messengers.

Exercise generates free radicals and, with them, part of the signal that makes muscle adapt. Studies with high doses of vitamins C and E have shown that blocking that signal can reduce some training adaptations, such as the improvement in aerobic capacity.

That is the central argument: the body does not want zero oxidants. It wants to produce the necessary ones and repair the damage from the rest.

## When excess takes its toll

The problem appears when production exceeds repair capacity for years. Tobacco smoke, air pollution, alcohol, sustained high glucose and chronic inflammation are important sources.

Oxidative damage affects membrane lipids, proteins and DNA. LDL oxidation participates in atherosclerosis, and DNA damage is one of the mechanisms linked to cancer. These are well-described associations, not a chain of cause and effect that a juice resolves.

The repair system works all the time. Enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase convert one species into another and neutralise them. Food supplies the minerals those enzymes need, not an external shield.

## Antioxidant supplement trials failed

The theory was simple: if oxidants cause damage, taking antioxidants should protect. Large trials did not confirm it.

Beta-carotene supplements increased lung cancer incidence in smokers in two large trials, which is why they stopped early. Vitamin E did not reduce cardiovascular events or cancer in large trials, and selenium did not show the benefit attributed to it. Vitamin C does not lower cold incidence in the general population.

A meta-analysis of trials with high doses of beta-carotene, vitamin A and vitamin E found a small but consistent increase in mortality. Isolating a vitamin at a high dose changes its behaviour; food does not pose that problem.

## Food covers what a pill cannot

Fruit and vegetables supply vitamin C, beta-carotene, vitamin E, polyphenols and fibre in the same matrix. That combination is what is associated with lower risk in population studies, and it is not reproduced by a capsule.

The practical recommendation stays the same: at least 400 grams a day from fruit and vegetables, with a range of colours and textures. Whole grains, legumes, fish and olive oil complete the pattern.

## What does move the needle

Not smoking is the measure with the largest impact on oxidative stress. Moderating alcohol, sleeping, keeping weight stable and controlling glucose in those who have high levels complete the list.

Exercise belongs in the same category even though it generates free radicals. The body responds by adapting: it raises its defences and improves repair. A sedentary life protects against nothing.

Sun protection prevents damage to the skin, the most exposed organ. No diet replaces sunscreen.

## How much is enough

There is no need to count antioxidants or chase doses. A pepper or two kiwis cover an adult's vitamin C, and an orange supplies most of it. A handful of nuts and olive oil cover vitamin E. Beta-carotene arrives with orange and green vegetables.

Supplements have a place in diagnosed deficiencies and very restricted diets, with professional judgement. Outside those cases, money earns more at the greengrocer.
