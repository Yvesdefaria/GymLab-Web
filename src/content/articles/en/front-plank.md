The position looks simple because nothing moves. The work is in what does not happen: the hip does not drop, the lower back does not sag and the shoulders do not climb towards the ears. When one of the three goes, the set is over even if there are seconds left on the clock.

## What the front plank trains

The rectus abdominis brakes extension of the spine, the obliques control lateral tilt and the transversus keeps the pressure inside the abdomen. The glutes and the pelvic floor close the chain from below, and the shoulders carry the weight of the upper body.

It is an anti-extension exercise: the spine stays stable while gravity pulls the centre of the body towards the floor. That pattern shows up in the squat, the deadlift and any task where someone pushes or carries a load in front of the torso.

Its limit is clear too. A plank builds stability, not range or hypertrophy. For the rectus abdominis in its flexion role, crunches and their variations remain the main work, and the plank supports them.

## Starting position

- Lie face down with the forearms on the floor and the elbows under the shoulders.
- Rest on the toes and spread the feet slightly for a wider base.
- Push the floor with the forearms and lift the hips until the body forms one line from head to heels.
- Keep the neck in line with the spine and the gaze on the floor, a few centimetres in front of the hands.
- Squeeze the glutes and draw the navel in before you start counting.

A mat under the forearms saves you the discomfort of bare contact with the floor. If the palms or wrists complain, make fists and rest on the little fingers, with the hand in a neutral position.

## Execution and breathing

Hold the position without moving the pelvis. The abs contract as if you wanted to press the navel against the lower back, and that tension stays while the air goes in and out.

Do not hold your breath. Holding it makes the position easier and raises blood pressure more than necessary; if breathing is hard, try two or three short exhales during the hold. The set is held with the diaphragm working, not with the air locked.

The end of the set is set by the position. When the hip drops, the lower back arches or the shoulders climb, it is over: adding seconds only piles up time in bad posture.

## Common mistakes

- **Hips too high.** The position turns into an inverted V and the abs stop holding the line. Lower the pelvis until it lines up with the shoulders and heels.
- **Arched lower back.** The load moves to the lower back. With weight on top, that arch is a direct route to a strain; without it, it already means the set is too long.
- **Shoulders at the ears.** The work shifts to the traps. Push the floor and move the shoulders away from the head.
- **Head hanging.** The neck bends and the line breaks. Gaze down, neck neutral.
- **Holding your breath.** The set gets shorter and pressure climbs for nothing.
- **Chasing the minute at any cost.** The clock is a reference, not the goal.

## How long and how many sets

Fifteen seconds is the minimum for the position to make sense. From there, the useful range runs up to a minute, and the reference for most people is sixty seconds with clean technique.

Three or four sets cover the dose. Two to four sessions a week are enough: the core recovers too, and adding days does not speed anything up. Leave at least a day between sessions.

When the minute comes easy, the sensible progression is to add load rather than time. A plate or a backpack on the back raises the demand inside a short, measurable window, and technique gets checked on every set.

## Variations

The [weighted plank](/en/weighted-plank/) is the loaded version of the same exercise: it covers where to place the plate, how to progress from the clock and what to do when you train alone.

The [side plank](/en/side-plank/) works the flank of the trunk with the body on its side and the support on one forearm. It is another pattern, with other muscles leading, and it deserves its own progression.

The knee version works as an entry point or as an adjustment on an off day. It shortens the lever and lets you keep the line without fighting the weight of the legs.

## Where it fits

It goes after the dynamic movements and before the accessories, or at the end of an abs session that already includes flexion and rotation. As a warm-up, two short sets of fifteen or twenty seconds work well.
