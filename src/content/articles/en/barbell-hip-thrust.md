The hip thrust has earned a place in almost every glute routine, and the argument is sound: it loads hip extension with little compression on the spine. The barbell version has more detail than it looks, and the [machine version](/en/hip-thrust-machine/) solves some of it without solving all of it. This page covers the bar: setup, range, mistakes and dose. The benefits and the short-position argument live in the [hip thrust benefits guide](/en/hip-thrust-benefits/).

## The bench, the bar and the pad

The setup has three parts and none is optional. The bench edge goes at the height of the shoulder blades, in the mid back. If it sits too low, the lower back arches to compensate; if it sits too high, the range shortens and the chin drifts to the chest.

The bar rests on the hip crease, with a pad. That crease is where the pelvis takes the load without compressing the abdomen. A bare bar on bone hurts, and a bar high on the belly pushes into the spine instead of into the hip.

The feet go at shoulder width, toes slightly out. The reference is the shin: at the top it ends up almost vertical. If the knees stay deeply bent at the top, the feet are too close; if the shin leans forward, they are too far away.

## The range

With the setup done, the range is short and direct. The chin tucks slightly, the ribs stay down and the abdomen braces before the bar moves.

Push through the heels and lift the pelvis until the torso and the thighs form a line. Squeeze the glute there for a second. The bar stays over the hip crease for the whole rep; it does not drift toward the belly or the thighs. The way down is the other half of the exercise: lower under control to the floor or to the starting point, without dropping the bar.

The neck stays neutral, eyes forward. Looking at the ceiling or jamming the chin into the chest changes the head position without improving extension. The hip ends extended, not hyperextended: if the lower back arches at the top, the set is already over.

## What it trains

The driver is the gluteus maximus. The hamstrings help because they cross the hip, the adductors stabilise the pelvis and the quadriceps keep the knee over the foot. The gluteus medius and minimus are abductors, not extensors: they stabilise the pelvis but do not push the hip up. [Hip anatomy](/en/hip-anatomy/) sets out that division in more detail.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing height with the back.** If the lower back arches, the hip is already extended and the spine is doing the lifting.
- **Moving the feet too far away.** The further they go, the more hamstring and the less glute. Adjust until the glute does the work.
- **Bouncing off the floor.** The controlled descent is half the stimulus.
- **Letting the knees cave.** The knees follow the line of the foot through the whole range.
- **Starting with plates.** Learn the movement with an empty bar and a pad before adding weight.

## Sets, reps and rest

This page recommends three or four sets of eight to twelve reps, with ninety to one hundred and twenty seconds of rest. That is a dose for size and control. For strength, four sets of six to eight with two minutes, as long as the position holds.

Progression is the usual one: when the last set closes with the same control as the first, add two and a half or five kilos. Keep the same pad and the same bench height between sessions, so the load is the only variable that changes. [Progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) explains how to do it without jumps.

## It complements the hinge and the squat without replacing them

The hip thrust has one concrete virtue: it loads the glute in its short position, with the hip extended and little axial compression. It also has an equally concrete limit: it does not take the hip through deep flexion and it does not load the body like a squat.

The deadlift and the Romanian deadlift cover the long range of the hip, with the glute and hamstrings stretched and a high axial load. The [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/) covers knee flexion with an upright torso and the full pattern. The hip thrust sits between the two and covers what neither does as well.

That is why it works as one piece and not as the centre of the plan. A programme with only hip thrusts for the glute leaves out the range of the hinge and the work of the squat. Three or four sets a week are enough; the rest of the volume belongs to the other two patterns.

## Where it fits in the week

It goes after the main lift, when the hip is warm and the back does not have to carry the bar. It also works on a glute day next to [glute bridges](/en/glute-bridge/) as a learning version and [hip extension](/en/hip-extension-glutes/) as accessory work.

The [leg routines](/en/leg-routines/) show where to place it by frequency, and the machine version allows the same dose when the bar setup is not comfortable.
