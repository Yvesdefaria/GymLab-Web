The word inflammation carries a huge promise and a loose definition. It helps to separate what a lab measures, what large population studies observe and what a shop wants to sell.

## What an anti-inflammatory diet is and what it measures

Acute inflammation is a normal response: the body mobilises immune cells against an infection or an injury and then returns to rest. Low-grade chronic inflammation is different. It does not hurt or show, it is detected through markers such as C-reactive protein or interleukin 6, and it appears alongside obesity, a sedentary life, poor sleep and diets low in fibre.

A diet can move those markers a little. The studies that manage it work with whole patterns over months, not with a single food or a shake. That scale is what gives the label its meaning.

## The pattern that repeats in the studies

When a trial lowers inflammatory markers, it almost always compares similar patterns: more vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish; fewer soft drinks, pastries, ready meals and processed meat. That set resembles the classic Mediterranean diet and also other traditional plant-based diets.

Observational studies find that people who follow that pattern have lower C-reactive protein values and fewer cardiometabolic risk factors. These are associations, with all the caution the term demands: people who eat this way also tend to sleep better, move more and smoke less. Even after discounting those factors, the pattern keeps a favourable relationship.

## Fibre, vegetables and fruit

Fibre is the nutrient that appears most consistently in studies on inflammation. Between 25 and 35 grams a day, spread across vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains and nuts. The gut microbiota ferments part of that fibre and produces short-chain fatty acids that take part in immune regulation.

No single vegetable lowers inflammation on its own. What adds up is the daily dose and the variety of colours, because each group brings different polyphenols. The guide to [vegetables at dinner](/en/vegetables-or-fruit-for-dinner/) suggests practical ways to raise the serving without repeating the same dish.

## Oily fish and omega-3

Oily fish provides EPA and DHA, two fats the body uses in the inflammatory response. Two servings a week of sardines, mackerel, herring or salmon cover the usual recommendation and also bring complete protein and vitamin D.

Fish oil supplements do not reproduce that effect in healthy adults. Trials with high doses show small, inconsistent changes in markers, and going past the dose carries risks. Eating fish is cheaper and harder to overdo.

## Ultra-processed food and the rest of the context

Ultra-processed products are associated with more inflammation and a lower-quality diet. The mechanism is a mix: many calories per bite, little fibre, plenty of sugar and salt, and a format that invites fast eating. There is no need to demonise every packet, but cutting frequency is worth it.

Context weighs as much as the plate. Sleeping little raises inflammatory markers, so does a sedentary life, and alcohol adds to it. The guide to [hydration for training](/en/hydration-for-training/) reminds you that drinking enough belongs to the same set of habits that support the immune response.

## What the label does not support

There is no universal list of anti-inflammatory foods. The lists in circulation mix reasonable data with exaggeration: turmeric, ginger, green tea or coconut oil appear as remedies with effects that trials in people do not confirm at usual doses.

Nor is there a blood test that decides your menu. C-reactive protein helps assess an infectious or inflammatory process, not choose between rice and quinoa. And cutting entire food groups rarely improves anything if total calories and fibre are not sorted out first.

Vitamins and minerals matter when one is missing, and the guide to [vitamins for the immune system](/en/immune-system-vitamins/) covers that in more detail. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) explain why a jar of turmeric does not replace a serving of vegetables.

## How to build the plate

The practical version asks for no gram counting and no special products. It changes the proportion of what you already eat and keeps it over time.

Protein has to arrive, especially if you train strength. The guide to [what to eat to build muscle](/en/what-to-eat-to-build-muscle/) sorts sources by serving and explains how much you need a day. If meat is part of your diet, the page on [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) compares cuts and makes clear what each one brings.

The result shows up over months of consistency, with a follow-up blood test when a doctor asks for one and with the sense that food supports training instead of getting in its way.
