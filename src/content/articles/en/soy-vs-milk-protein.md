The studies that popularised the superiority of milk compared different amounts of protein, or 11 gram bars with less leucine on the soy side. At those doses, leucine was different too. When trials match protein and leucine, the gain in muscle mass and strength from soy and milk looks much the same.

## What changes when the dose is matched

That does not make soy identical in every respect. It digests faster than casein and slower than whey, and its leucine density is lower. The useful question is not which one is better, but how much is needed to match the response.

## Soy is a complete protein

Soy supplies the nine essential amino acids, just like milk. The difference is in the amounts: leucine is lower per gram of protein, and some amino acids appear in tighter proportions. That is corrected with dose, not with a mandatory combination at every meal.

With isolated soy protein, 25 to 30 % more powder matches the leucine in a whey serving. With soy concentrate, the adjustment is slightly larger because the powder carries less protein per gram. A soy and pea blend also closes the gap without raising the dose.

## What the 92 % and 78 % figures measure

Kinetics studies describe how much of the ingested protein is used after a meal: around 92 % for milk and 78 % for soy. It is a measure of postprandial utilisation, not a verdict on muscle. Part of the soy protein is oxidised before it reaches the tissues.

That figure matters for calculating how much protein is needed, not for dismissing soy. If utilisation is lower, more grams compensate. At matched protein and leucine, the hypertrophy difference is small or absent in training trials.

## Leucine: the variable you can control

Leucine is the amino acid that acts as the signal to start protein synthesis. Soy has less per gram than whey, so a 25 gram serving of soy delivers less signal than 25 grams of whey. Raising it to 30 or 35 grams, or blending with pea, matches the signal.

There is no need to obsess over an exact leucine number at every meal. With 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo per day and servings of 25 to 40 grams, most trainees cover the requirement. Soy fits that split without trouble.

## Speed, satiety and daily use

Soy digests faster than casein and slower than whey. That middle speed makes it useful at any hour, though it is not the most filling choice at night. For breakfast or after training, it works like any other protein.

Taste and texture are different, and some people notice more gas at first. That is a matter of tolerance, not quality. The page on [vegan protein](/en/vegan-protein/) covers plant sources and their blends.

## How to use soy without losing anything

Use 25 to 40 grams per serving, set the total at 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo, and raise the dose by 25 or 30 % if soy is the only source. If the budget allows, a soy and pea or soy and rice blend spreads the limiting amino acids better.

Soy is not a second-class protein. It is a complete protein with less leucine per gram, and the difference is paid in grams. ## Who it makes sense for

For anyone who does not eat dairy, for anyone looking for a complete plant protein, and for anyone who wants to vary the source. Soy allergy exists and is more common in children; anyone who has it needs another option. Lactose intolerance is not a reason to give up milk, but soy solves that case.
