You may have seen digits such as 4-0-2 or 2-0-X in a training programme and never tried them because you did not know what they meant. They refer to the execution times of the exercises and, more precisely, to the speed of the reps within each set.

## What training tempo is

Tempo refers to training speed, one of the components of weight work that gets the least attention.

Instructors teach gym members that movements should be performed slowly and under control, to reduce the risk of the weight getting away from them. Up to a point slow training is good, but not always.

No way of training is the best for ever. The human body is a highly adaptive organism and responds differently to training. Varying the variables forces it to adapt, and one of those variables is the speed at which you perform your reps. Execution speed has been studied by European and American exercise scientists in many athletes.

## Lifting speed

The first thing to know is that lifting speed matters, a great deal.

Picture two people doing ten reps of a row. One raises the weight counting to three and lowers it counting to six; the other swings it up and down like a pendulum. Both do the same number of reps with the same weight, yet the effect is not the same, because speed is moving other variables.

For a bodybuilder, one of those variables is **time under tension**, the total time the muscles are contracting to complete a set.

To build muscle mass to the maximum, time under tension in a set should sit between 20 and 70 seconds. That range allows plenty of variation: from a single rep that lasts 70 seconds to a set of 15 reps that also lasts 70.

That does not mean a bodybuilder cannot do longer or shorter sets, only that most of them should fall inside that range.

## How to read tempo notation

The three numbers of a tempo describe the three phases of a rep, in this order:

With a 3-2-1 tempo, for example, you take three seconds to lower the weight, pause for two seconds at the bottom before lifting it and take one second to return to the starting position.

Sometimes you will see an X instead of a number. It is used to mean lift the weight as fast as possible. Rest between sets, which is also part of a routine, is written separately, in seconds.

## What slow training is for

To develop optimal strength, slow training with heavy weights has a clear advantage over high-speed training. Lowering the weight slowly lengthens the duration of the stimulus and raises the levels of tension on the muscle, two factors that favour strength and muscle growth.

High intensity and a slow rhythm produce a series of effects on muscle building: they stimulate type II muscle fibres and increase muscle glycogen, phosphocreatine, adenosine triphosphate, adenosine diphosphate, creatine, phosphorylase and Krebs cycle enzyme activity. Taken together, more strength and more muscle mass.

## How to vary speed to build strength

Two recent studies showed the value of varying training speed compared with keeping a constant contraction speed throughout a programme.

The first concluded that, to keep adding kilos, you should train at both slow and fast speeds. The second found that slow training produces more significant strength gains than fast training, but also that fast training alone does not produce changes as large as a combination of both.

## The final piece of advice

This kind of training should be applied once you already have a solid base of maximum strength. In time, many athletes get great results from high-speed exercises, but first they need to build that base with slow movements and keep including them throughout their athletic career to secure continuous progress.

## In short

Tempo orders the three phases of every rep and tells you how long the muscle spends under tension. For hypertrophy, aim for sets of 20 to 70 seconds; for strength, lower the weight slowly and do not sacrifice technique. Alternate speeds instead of sticking to the same one.
