If you repeat the cardiovascular stimulus continuously and systematically, in the medium and long term your body builds deep adaptations and delivers a more efficient cardiovascular response.

With those adaptations, from a training standpoint, your body produces more energy from other substrates such as stored fat, tolerates higher and longer loads and resists fatigue better.

## Aerobic adaptations: the momentary ones

These adaptations come from stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system. Several pathways cause a redistribution of blood flow through vasoconstriction and vasodilation, sending blood to the most active areas. The heart increases its rate and the force of contraction, producing an increase in cardiac output of up to five times and up to double the systolic blood pressure.

### Stroke volume

During exercise the heart has to pump more blood. The left ventricle increases its filling volume and reaches maximum values at around 50% of exercise intensity, when filling and pressure are complete, in trained and sedentary people alike, men and women.

Above that intensity a stabilisation phase begins and lasts until very high workloads. At that point, stroke volume falls because the progressive rise in heart rate does not allow diastolic filling and, as a result, reduces the volume of the left ventricle, the chamber responsible for ejecting blood to the whole body.

### Heart rate

Besides raising stroke volume, the heart also beats more often. At submaximal intensities, that rise in heart rate is linear with exercise intensity.

As intensity climbs, stroke volume and heart rate rise together. At high intensities, stroke volume drops because the heart does not have time to fill completely between contractions.

That is why the heart reaches its full capacity and its greatest adaptations at submaximal intensities, and you do not need high-intensity work to achieve the biggest cardiac adaptations.

### Blood flow redistribution

During cardiovascular exercise, blood flow is redistributed to send more blood to the working muscles. In the most active areas, the diameter of the capillaries increases. The sympathetic nervous system reduces the blood supply to the stomach, intestines, kidneys and skin, while vasodilation occurs in the active areas.

Blood reaches the working muscles, delivers oxygen and energy substrates and carries away carbon dioxide and waste metabolites.

### Ventilation

The lungs expand to their maximum and at a higher rate, so ventilation increases. It can go from 5 l/min at rest to 200 l/min at maximal exercise, a 35-fold increase over its resting value.

## Deep adaptations: what stays after training

Momentary adaptations return to their resting state when the exercise ends. If you keep cardiovascular training sessions going over time, with some frequency and regularity, deep adaptations appear, and many of them last for the rest of your life.

The body adapts with anatomical and physiological changes that raise its functional level. Those adaptations have a significant effect on heart rate, both at rest and during exercise. The heart works more efficiently, ejects more blood and the cardiac muscle performs better, so it does not need to contract more often. Heart rate falls at rest and during submaximal exercise.

## Why maximum heart rate barely changes

Training usually brings no change in maximum heart rate. It stays practically identical in a trained person and in a sedentary one. The difference lies in the intensity needed to reach it: the better your fitness, the more intensity you need. A sedentary person reaches their maximum much sooner.

## The three systems that adapt

The deep adaptations to aerobic exercise are spread across three interrelated systems: circulatory, respiratory and metabolic.

### Circulatory system

**Greater cardiac mass and enlarged chambers.** The chambers and walls of the heart grow, its filling capacity improves and cardiac volume increases. The heart, which works as a pump, can fill a little more and push blood out with more force.

**Capillarisation.** Higher capillary density lets blood reach the fibres of the heart and skeletal muscles more easily, delivering the oxygen and nutrients that muscle contraction needs.

### Respiratory system

The lungs work more efficiently: the respiratory surface increases and alveolar-capillary diffusion improves. As a result, vital capacity can increase by up to 2 litres and respiratory rate falls.

### Metabolic system

**Higher myoglobin content.** The number of red blood cells and haemoglobin, the oxygen carriers in the blood, rises significantly. Transport capacity is greater and oxygen availability improves.

**More mitochondria.** Trained muscle uses the oxygen delivered by the capillaries more effectively thanks to an increase in the number and size of mitochondria, the power plant that produces energy. In trained people, the ability to obtain energy from glucose and fatty acids also rises.

**More oxidative enzymes.** The activity of the enzymes that oxidise glucose and fat increases. The body breaks down glucose and, above all, fatty acids more efficiently, and glycogen recovery improves: the stores refill in less time.

**Greater fat oxidation.** The use of fat as an energy substrate increases, and its mobilisation and transport improve. That spares muscle glycogen, which is useful for fast energy but scarcer than the body's fat stores. Over the long term, body fat percentage falls.
