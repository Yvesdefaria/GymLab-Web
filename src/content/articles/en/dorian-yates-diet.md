Yates starts from an idea that still holds: bulking is not eating everything that moves. His original text says so plainly and puts the weight on three things: training, eating and resting. The diet is the part that can be written down.

## Seven meals, every three hours

The day starts at seven and ends at ten at night, with one meal every three hours:

- 7:00. Breakfast: 100 g oats, six egg whites, two yolks, two slices of wholemeal bread and a banana.
- 10:00. Mid-morning: 40 g protein powder with water and 300 g boiled potato.
- 13:00. Lunch: 200 g chicken breast, 100 g rice and 100 g vegetables.
- 16:00. Before training: 40 g protein powder with water and a banana.
- 18:00. After training: 70 g carbohydrate powder and 30 g protein.
- 19:00. Dinner: 200 g lean beef, 300 g boiled potato and 200 g broccoli.
- 22:00. Before bed: 40 g protein powder and 50 g oats.

The order assumes training at five in the afternoon. If you train at another time, move the two meals around the session and leave the rest alone.

## 3,500 calories with a clear split

The plan declares 3,500 calories: 1,750 to 1,925 calories from carbohydrate, about 1,050 from protein and 525 to 700 from fat. In grams that is about 260 of protein, 440 to 480 of carbohydrate and 58 to 78 of fat. It is a high-carb, moderate-fat split, with protein at 30 percent of calories.

## Protein: 2.5 g/kg is the ceiling

The text asks for 2.5 g per kilo and, a few lines later, admits that 1.8 or 2 is enough. Both numbers cannot be the target at once. The 260 g in the plan equals 2.5 g/kg for someone at 105 kilos, a large bodybuilder in the off-season. At 80 kilos, the same split leaves 144 to 176 g. Above 2.2 g/kg no extra muscle appears, and the money goes up while room for fibre goes down.

## What the carbohydrate drink adds

The post-workout meal carries 70 g of carbohydrate powder and 30 g of protein. Those 70 g are about 280 calories of carbohydrate; the protein adds 120. Together they cover the gap between the session and dinner. A banana, 60 g of rice and a yoghurt do a similar job with food.

## How much weight to gain

No law fixes a kilo of muscle a month. What shows up is that a fast gain carries more fat than muscle, and that fat then takes months to remove. Half a kilo to a kilo a month is a reasonable cap for someone with years of training behind them; a beginner can gain a little faster without collecting as much fat.

To track it, weigh three or four times a week and watch the average. If a month adds more than a kilo and a half, there are calories to spare.

## The advice that holds

Of the ten tips Yates published in his book, four are still useful:

- Record your food for a couple of weeks to see what changes.
- Spread protein across six or seven meals.
- Adjust the percentages to the person: anyone who gains fat easily drops carbohydrate and raises protein.
- Stay as consistent with the diet as with the training.

## Where it fits in your plan

Before copying the menu, work out maintenance calories and add 300 to 500. The guide to [calories for muscle gain](/en/calories-for-muscle-gain/) runs the numbers with your weight and activity. The seven-meal plan comes after that, once you know how much to eat.

His training is documented in the [Dorian Yates series](/en/dorian-yates-series/) and in the [abdominal work](/en/dorian-yates-abs/), which completes the same high-intensity system. If you want fewer meals and more fish, the [Herculean diet](/en/herculean-diet/) spreads 3,100 calories across six. And if reaching the protein target is the problem, the [high-protein diet](/en/high-protein-diet-for-muscle/) splits it into six moments at 2,900 calories.
