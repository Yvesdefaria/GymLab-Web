A meal works when it can be repeated on an ordinary Tuesday. The plates below are built to repeat and they cover protein, carbohydrate, fibre and micronutrients with portions you weigh once and remember for months.

## What makes a plate work

Protein drives the building. Between 25 and 40 grams per meal spreads the daily 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo well. Carbohydrate fuels training and sits near the session. Vegetables add volume, fibre and micronutrients without many calories.

The insulin rise after eating carbohydrate is a normal body response, not a goal to chase with an exact mix. The day's total decides the result, not the order in which you eat.

## How portions scale

Order matters when calories have to move. First adjust the carbohydrate, with more rice, more oats or more sweet potato. Then the fat, with oil, avocado or nuts. Protein stays almost fixed, because it is the part that is hard to cover and the part that protects muscle when the total falls.

For bulking, adding 100 to 150 kilocalories per plate is enough to add 300 to 500 a day. For cutting, taking 100 off each plate leaves a moderate deficit without touching protein. Swings of 500 calories in a single meal usually last a week.

One example: dinner adds 30 grams of olive oil and reaches about 825 kilocalories. The same dinner with 150 grams of sweet potato instead of 250 drops to about 470. The plate changes gear without touching the chicken or the vegetables.

## The daily total

The six plates together come to around 2,800 kilocalories and some 250 grams of protein. That is more than most strength trainees need, so the list works as a pantry of ideas, not a mandatory menu.

For an 80 kilo adult, three main plates and two of the smaller ones come to around 2,300 kilocalories, a reasonable base before adjusting. The plate before and the one after training are chosen around the session time, and whichever is left becomes the free meal of the week.

## Chicken, beef or fish

Protein swaps almost by block: 180 grams of chicken, turkey, white fish or lean beef covers one meal. The difference is the fat that comes with it.

Choosing a fatty cut does not spare glycogen or protein. It adds extra calories, and those calories count the same as the ones in oil or bread. If the cut is fattier, reduce the added fat in the plate and the total does not move. The guide to [cooking juicy chicken](/en/juicy-chicken/) solves the boring part: keeping the breast from drying out.

## When each plate is eaten

The plate before training works between one and three hours ahead. The idea that cottage cheese has to reach the blood at the exact hour is more marketing than physiology. Digestion does not obey such a fine clock, and the day as a whole is what counts.

After training, the next meal replaces glycogen and supplies protein. There is no need to run to the fridge. If dinner comes two hours later, that dinner does the work. ## What the plate does not need

A plate with chicken, rice and vegetables does not ask for supplements. Protein powder only compensates when food falls short. Creatine makes sense as a separate extra, not as an ingredient. And vegetables are not replaced by a multivitamin to save time: fibre and volume count too.
