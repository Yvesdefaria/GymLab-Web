Abdominal machines vary from one maker to another, and some let the abs contract at both ends of the range. In the most common version only the upper body moves: the pelvis and legs stay fixed.

Resistance comes as a plate stack, and the usual rep band runs from moderate to high. It is a gym exercise on a dedicated machine.

## What the machine adds

The main advantage is that load can go up. On the floor, a crunch gets harder by lengthening the set or adding a plate that never sits right; on the machine you move the pin.

The second advantage is stability. The seat and rollers hold the body, so no effort goes into balancing. A beginner can learn the movement without distraction, and an advanced lifter can finish an ab session with a hard set when the free movements no longer come out clean.

The third is safety near the limit. The path is guided and the weight returns on its own, so there is no unstable position to get stuck in.

## The two versions

The upper-body crunch machine moves the trunk and keeps the pelvis still. That is loaded trunk flexion, the same family as the floor crunch or the decline bench.

The rotary torso machine adds rotation. The abs twist against resistance and the work is shared between the rectus and the obliques. They are two different stimuli and should be treated as different exercises, even when they share a room.

## How to set up the machine

First fit the machine to your body and take a trial run with no weight. If the path does not match your frame, no load adjustment fixes it.

Sit tight into it, with your glutes and back pressed firmly against the pad. Your feet go safely and evenly under the padded rollers, and your hands grip the handles to steady the upper body.

Look straight ahead. Do not press your chin into your chest; when you do, the neck starts riding along with the movement.

## Execution

Pull the torso down without jerks, contracting the abs as far as you can. The lower back rounds as the abs shorten; that rounding is part of the movement.

Hold the contracted position for a second and exhale there. Then let the weight take you back up slowly instead of releasing the tension all at once.

## Common mistakes

- Pressing the chin into the chest and pulling with the neck.
- Pulling with the arms instead of the abs.
- Using half the range because the weight is too heavy.
- Letting the stack drop on the way back so the plates slam.
- Switching machines every week and having nothing to compare.

## How to load it

When twelve reps with good form come easily, stop adding reps and add a plate on the next set. The original article cites a band of eight to twelve reps, which works as a reference for deciding the jump.

The last set takes a [drop set](/en/drop-sets/): at failure, move the pin to fewer plates and go again to failure. It is a way to accumulate work once the technique is learned.

## When the set ends

The set ends when the torso stops coming down or when the movement shifts to the arms and the neck. If the stack moves but the abs do not shorten, the set is over even if reps remain.

The original text does not fix how many sets to do. Weekly volume lives in the [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/) and in the [hypertrophy routine](/en/abs-hypertrophy-routine/), which uses this exercise with heavy loads.

## Where it fits

As a loading block it works at the start of an ab session or at the end, after two or three free movements. At the start it arrives fresh and moves more plates; at the end it serves to finish with a drop set.

The [cable crunch](/en/kneeling-cable-crunches/) offers a similar load with the body kneeling and more freedom of range. The practical difference is the path: the machine guides it and the pulley makes you control the trunk. The rest of the family is in the [ab exercises](/en/ab-exercises/) hub.
