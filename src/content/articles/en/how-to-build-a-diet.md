Most plans fail on the order. You start with percentages, divide the grams and find that the calories no longer match the goal. The correct sequence runs from the total to the parts: calories first, then the protein and fat floors, and finally the carbohydrates that fill what is left.

## Step 1: set the calories

The first number is maintenance, estimated with a formula and corrected on the scale. The guide to [maintenance calories](/en/maintenance-calories/) explains the full method. From there, subtract 300-500 kcal to lose fat or add 200-300 kcal to gain mass. Without that number, any split is a guess.

## Step 2: protein as a floor

The reference for strength training is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day. At 80 kilos, that is 128 to 176 grams. A comfortable value sits at 1.8-2 grams per kilo, especially in a deficit.

Protein protects muscle when calories fall and supports the gain when they rise. Above 2.2 grams per kilo there is no proven advantage for hypertrophy in healthy people, and every extra gram displaces food that brings other things. The guide to [protein for athletes](/en/do-athletes-need-more-protein/) details how to spread it across meals.

## Step 3: fat also has a floor

Fat covers 0.8 to 1 gram per kilo. At 80 kilos, 64-80 grams. Below 0.8 it becomes harder to cover fat-soluble vitamins and keep a stable hormonal profile. Olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish and egg yolks cover the quota without effort.

## Step 4: carbohydrates fill the rest

They are calculated by difference. Subtract the calories from protein and fat from the total, then divide what remains by four. For lifters, the practical reference is 3 to 5 grams per kilo. If the result falls below 3, review total calories or fat before cutting further.

## Fibre and meal structure

Fibre stays between 25 and 35 grams a day, spread across vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains. Raising it suddenly causes gas, so it is worth taking two or three weeks.

The number of meals depends on appetite and schedule. Three to five works for almost everyone. Each meal with protein carries 25 to 40 grams, an amount that muscle and satiety use well. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) explains how to place them around training, and the one on [protein and carbs together](/en/protein-and-carbs-together/) orders the plate.

## The weekly check

Weight is measured daily while fasted and compared week to week using averages. If the goal is fat loss and weight has not moved in two weeks, cut 100-200 kcal, starting with carbohydrates. If weight is falling by more than 1 % a week, add 100-200 to protect muscle.

Waist and strength complete the picture. Stable weight with more reps on the same exercises is a good sign in a cutting diet.

## Why fixed percentages fail

A 40/50/10 split sounds tidy until it is applied to a high total. At 3,600 kcal, 50 % protein is 1,800 kcal, which means 450 grams a day. That figure has no support and leaves fat at 40 grams, below most people's floor.

Percentages are useful for checking a plan that has already been built. As a starting point they ignore two figures that matter: the useful protein ceiling and the fat floor. That is why the plan is assembled in grams per kilo and reviewed in percentages.

With protein between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo, the plan holds when calories rise or fall. The guide to [lean muscle gain](/en/lean-muscle-gain/) explains how to raise calories without breaking the plan, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) avoid purchases that change none of these numbers.
