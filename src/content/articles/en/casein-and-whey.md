Casein and whey do not compete to be the better protein. They are two speeds of the same food, and the body uses both. The practical choice depends on the gap you want to cover during the day, not on a hierarchy between them.

## What milk contains: 80 % casein

Of every 100 grams of milk protein, about 80 are casein and about 20 are whey. Casein is the fraction that curdles in the stomach and forms a kind of gel; whey is the liquid left when the curd separates. A glass of milk carries both, and so does a yoghurt.

## The whey peak is short

Whey digests quickly and raises amino acids in blood to a peak that arrives around 90 minutes. That rise stimulates muscle protein synthesis, mainly through leucine, and then falls. Casein does the opposite: it thickens in the stomach and releases amino acids for about seven hours, with a lower peak and a longer tail.

The difference has been measured many times and does not translate into a large advantage in results. When total protein and leucine are matched, hypertrophy with one source or the other comes out similar. What changes is convenience and timing.

## Speed matters at the edges of the day

There are two moments when speed adds something. After sleep, the body has gone hours without amino acids and whey arrives sooner. Before sleep, casein keeps the supply going through the night and shortens the long fast. During the rest of the day, a whole meal does the same job.

The idea that casein builds muscle while you sleep is closer to advertising than to physiology. Night-time protein synthesis responds to the daily total and to the last meal; a casein shake helps if you are short of grams, not because the night has special properties. The guide to [the best night-time protein](/en/casein-protein/) works through that calculation calmly.

## The total decides

Daily protein sits between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight, with a practical ceiling around 2.4. For a 75 kg person, that is between 120 and 165 grams a day. Splitting it into 25 to 40 gram servings covers the day without needing shakes.

Whey and casein are convenient ways to reach that figure, not shortcuts. A whey shake with milk already carries both fractions; so does a Greek yoghurt or a cottage cheese. The guide to [protein](/en/protein-guide/) sorts the sources and the one on [too much protein](/en/too-much-protein/) explains what happens when the figure goes too far.

## When to choose each one

Whey works when you want fast protein and little lactose: isolate or hydrolysate. Casein works when you want satiety and a slow release: before sleep or in a meal that has to last. Whole milk serves both purposes and adds fat and lactose.

Whey concentrate has more lactose than the isolate and costs less. If milk does not sit well with you, the problem is usually lactose rather than protein; the guide to [lactose intolerance](/en/lactose-intolerance-and-training/) separates that discomfort from allergy. The whey process is covered in [whey protein production](/en/whey-protein-production/).

## What milk does not do

Milk has no special hormonal properties beyond its protein and micronutrients. It is a complete and cheap food, and that is already enough to include it in the diet if you tolerate it. Neither of its two fractions needs a separate supplement to work.

## How to read the label

On a supplement, look at protein per 100 grams, lactose and the order of the ingredients. A product with casein as the first ingredient will be slower than one with whey first. Price per 25 grams of protein compares better than the price of the tub, and the guide to [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) avoids purchases that add nothing.
