The public argument about GMOs looks little like the science that evaluates them. The conversation deals in whole categories of food, companies and countries, while the real assessment happens event by event: one variety, one gene and one trait. That difference explains why the same studies can lead two people to opposite conclusions.

## What a GMO is and what it is not

A GMO is an organism that has received genetic material from another species through genetic engineering. The result carries one or a few new genes, inserted at one point in the genome, and keeps the rest of its information. It is not the same as a hybrid, which comes from crossing varieties of the same species, or as an induced mutation, which alters existing genes without adding foreign DNA.

The technique moves a gene between species that would not cross naturally, and the new gene may be expressed in a way that did not exist in the food before.

## Trait by trait: the Bt plant

Bt maize produces a protein from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*. The protein binds to specific receptors in the gut of lepidopteran larvae, breaks their intestinal cells and stops feeding. Mammals do not have those receptors, and the protein is digested like any other, which explains much of its safety margin.

Specificity has a limit. Bt maize pollen can reach nearby plants and affect non-target caterpillars, an effect studied before a crop is authorised. Resistance is monitored too: pests exposed continuously can select insensitive individuals, which is why refuges of non-Bt plants are required.

## Herbicide tolerance and residues

The other family of traits is tolerance to a herbicide, mostly glyphosate. The modified plant survives the treatment, which allows weeds to be cleared without damaging the crop. The debate then moves to the residues left in the grain.

A residue is not a risk on its own. Authorisation sets a maximum residue limit in food and an acceptable daily intake for the herbicide, calculated with a margin below the dose that shows no effect in studies. European monitoring programmes compare levels found in food with that limit. Most samples fall below it, and the ones that do not are withdrawn from the market.

The agronomic cost is separate: repeated use of the same herbicide selects resistant weeds. It is a documented problem in several regions and depends on field management, not on the consumer.

## What is assessed before approval

Each application includes the characterisation of the gene, the protein it produces and its stability, the composition of the food against the conventional variety, possible toxic and allergic effects, and a monitoring plan. Allergenicity gets special attention because a gene can transfer a protein from an allergenic food into one that was not allergenic.

The assessment tests the reasonable worst case, not average consumption, and requires a label or a restriction if the food is not equivalent to the conventional one in a key nutrient.

## The European label

In the European Union, Regulation 1829/2003 requires a label on any food that contains, consists of or is produced from GMOs, with a 0.9 % threshold for accidental presence. The rule covers oil, lecithin and derived additives even when DNA is no longer detectable in the final product.

One exception surprises many people: meat, milk and eggs from animals fed GM feed carry no label, because the animal is not genetically modified. The label reports the origin of the ingredient, not the whole feed chain.

The phrase "without GMOs" is voluntary and regulated. When it appears, it means the product meets the threshold and that traceability exists. The label is there to choose with, and it does not describe a hazard.

## What assessment bodies say

The European Food Safety Authority, the World Health Organization, the FAO and national science academies have reviewed the body of evidence. Their conclusion agrees: authorised and marketed GMO foods do not show a greater risk than their conventional equivalents.

The statement refers to the products assessed, not to any future GMO: a new trait needs new tests.

## How to read the label without alarm

Look for the ingredient and its origin: soy, maize, rapeseed, cotton or their derivatives. If the package says it contains GMOs, the information is doing its job. If it does not say so and the ingredient is one of those, it may sit below the threshold or come from a conventional variety.

The label does not measure nutritional quality. A GMO product can be an ultra-processed food full of sugar, and a conventional one can be fresh vegetables.
