A trained midsection braces the trunk when the load travels away from the body, and it is also the area people train most for looks and program worst. The reason is usually the same: exercises are chosen for the area they promise to work, not for the movement they produce.

There is no routine here. There is a map of exercises, with what each one asks for and what each one gives. Sets, reps and the weekly order live on the [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/) page.

## The muscles of the midsection and what they do

The plural word abs is misleading. The rectus abdominis is a single muscle running from the pubis to the sternum and ribs, and the bands of connective tissue crossing it draw the visible blocks without splitting it into two halves. What changes between exercises is the pattern, not a portion of the muscle.

Four more structures work around the rectus, and each has its own action:

- **External obliques.** They rotate the trunk and side-bend it. They are the superficial layer of the flank.
- **Internal obliques.** They sit underneath and help with rotation and side bending.
- **Transversus abdominis.** The deepest layer, working like a belt: it compresses the ribs and stabilises the spine.
- **Serratus anterior.** It runs over the upper ribs, on the side of the chest, and draws the shoulder blade forward when you push or throw.

The core also includes the lumbar muscles and the hip. That is why the abs are never trained alone: any exercise that loads the trunk with stability adds work for this area.

## Which family trains each pattern

Four patterns cover almost the whole catalogue. Telling them apart is the most useful decision you can make before choosing an exercise.

**Trunk flexion.** The sternum moves towards the pelvis. Here the rectus abdominis is the main mover. The floor crunch, the decline-bench crunch, the machine crunch, the cable crunch and the ball crunch belong here.

**Hip flexion.** The legs travel towards the chest. The iliopsoas and the rectus femoris do the main work, and the abs stabilise the pelvis and add the posterior tilt at the top. Hanging leg raises, reverse crunches and bench leg raises are in this family.

**Rotation and anti-rotation.** The obliques turn the trunk or resist a turn. The oblique crunch, the bicycle and plate twists are in the first group; the Pallof press and its variations are in the second.

**Isometric.** The muscle contracts without movement. The front plank and the side plank are the classic examples, and the [barbell rollout](/en/barbell-ab-rollout/) is the moving version of the same anti-extension job.

No family is superior. A complete programme touches all four, with more weight on some than others depending on the goal.

## The technique rules that apply to everything

These guidelines do not depend on the exercise. They apply to the simplest crunch and to the most heavily loaded movement.

- **Relaxed head.** The gaze goes forward and up, and the chin stays off the chest. The neck does not pull.
- **Hands behind the head, never behind the neck.** The weight of the head rests on the hands; it is not pushed with them.
- **Elbows open.** Close them on the way up and the range shortens and the abs lose tension.
- **Ribs down.** The abdomen compresses inwards instead of bulging out.
- **Lower back in contact.** At the end of every rep the lower back stays on the floor or the bench. If it arches, the set is over.
- **Exhale on the way up.** Air leaves through the mouth in the contraction and comes in through the nose on the way down.
- **Slower down than up.** The eccentric phase is where the movement is controlled.
- **No momentum.** No swinging, bouncing or inertia. If you cannot control the speed, the exercise has moved to another muscle.

## Strength endurance, strength or power

The goal changes the format, not the exercises.

If you want tone and endurance, work with high reps and little resistance: long sets of crunches, raises and planks. It is the most common route and the one that tolerates frequent work best.

If you want muscle development or transfer to a strength sport, raise the intensity: cables, machines and added weight, with moderate reps. The abs respond to load like any other muscle, and the block's [hypertrophy routine](/en/abs-hypertrophy-routine/) develops that idea.

If you want power, the work becomes explosive and light: medicine ball throws, fast movements with moderate weight. Speed is the variable that matters, and volume has to stay low to keep it.

## The catalogue, family by family

**Trunk flexion.** The floor crunch is the base. From there, the [decline bench](/en/decline-bench-crunches/) adds range and removes the rest at the bottom; the [machine](/en/machine-crunches/) allows load without a balance demand; the [high pulley](/en/kneeling-cable-crunches/) offers continuous tension and is the best option for heavy work. The ball crunch changes the range and the stability, and the right-angle leg crunch makes it easier to keep the lower back down.

**Hip flexion.** The hanging leg raise is the hardest version and the one that asks most of your technique; the guide to [hanging abs](/en/hanging-ab-exercises/) covers the full progression. Reverse crunches lift the pelvis at the end of the range, and raises on a flat or decline bench let you control the range with the back supported.

**Rotation.** The lying side crunch is the simplest and needs no equipment. The bicycle, with the elbow towards the opposite knee, is harder because the legs never touch the floor. Plate twists add load to the turn, and before those it is worth reading the warning on the [plate twists](/en/plate-twists/) page, which explains why loaded rotation is not the best idea for everyone.

**Isometric.** The front plank teaches you to hold the spine still, and the side version adds the flank. The barbell rollout is the advanced version of anti-extension. The page on [isometric abs](/en/isometric-abs/) covers times, mistakes and progression.

## How to choose an exercise

Three questions settle the choice.

**What equipment you have.** With nothing: crunches, reverse crunches, floor side crunches and planks. With a decline bench: extra range for trunk flexion. With a cable station: progressive load and continuous tension. With a pull-up bar: the whole hanging family.

**What level you are at.** If you are starting, begin with trunk flexion and planks, and leave hanging hip flexion until control of the pelvis is clear. The abs do not need tricks, they need execution.

**Which pattern you are missing.** If your whole routine is trunk flexion, you are missing hip, rotation and anti-extension. If it is all isometric, you are missing range. A catalogue is there to fill gaps, not to pile up exercises.

## How much to train and when to stop

The abs are a muscle like any other and they can be overtrained too. Outside specialised programmes, three sessions a week is a reasonable ceiling, and two are enough for most people.

The set ends when technique breaks: the lower back lifts, the neck takes over, the body swings or the range shortens. That rule is worth more than any rep count, and it is the same across all four families.

> **Note:** this page describes exercises, not a medical plan. If low back or abdominal pain appears during execution, stop and get it checked before continuing.
