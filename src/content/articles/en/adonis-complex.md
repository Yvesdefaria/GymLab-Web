The phrase became popular in the late nineties with a book that described men caught between an ideal of a muscular body and the feeling of never arriving. Psychiatry took part of that phenomenon under a name of its own, muscle dysmorphia, and placed it inside a known disorder.

## What muscle dysmorphia is

Someone who develops it sees themselves as small or soft even after years of training and with more muscle than average. The preoccupation does not stay in appearance: it changes behaviour. The person spends hours training, arranges meals around protein and avoids places where the body is visible, such as the beach, the changing room or a date.

Vigorexia is not an independent diagnosis in the manuals. It sits inside body dysmorphic disorder, with the label of muscle dysmorphia, and shares ground with eating disorders. The border with the legitimate wish to improve is interference: when the goal displaces work, rest and relationships, the problem is no longer aesthetic.

## The signs worth noticing

- Training through an injury that asks for rest, or refusing any rest day.
- Checking the scale, the mirror or body fat several times a day.
- Giving up the pool or the beach out of shame about the body.
- Eating the same food every day and feeling anxious about a meal outside the plan.
- Using steroids or other substances to speed up the change.
- Comparing the body with social media accounts for hours.
- Feeling irritable or low when a session is cancelled.

One or two of these behaviours define nothing on their own. The picture appears when they accumulate, last for months and cause distress or loss of function.

## Who it affects

Muscle dysmorphia is described mostly in young men who lift weights, and also in physique sports such as bodybuilding. Puberty and the first years in a changing room are sensitive moments: the body changes, comparison is constant and other people's comments carry weight.

Men also develop anorexia and bulimia, and they seek help less often. Social pressure pushes towards muscularity more than towards thinness, which changes the shape of the disorder without changing its severity. The environment matters: a gym that rewards sacrifice without limits and a coach who celebrates training injured feed the problem.

Age does not protect either. Someone returning to training after years needs health goals, as the guide to [starting to train at 70](/en/never-too-late-to-exercise/) sets out, not a comparison with the body of a twenty-year-old.

## The mirror and social media

Comparison does not start in the changing room. Social media shows selected bodies, with chosen light and posture, and the algorithm learns which images hold the gaze. A teenager who spends two hours a day looking at trained bodies builds a reference that does not exist in real life.

Dose and use weigh more than the platform: comparing your body with strangers, measuring personal worth in interactions and seeking validation in every post. Cutting the time and unfollowing accounts built around physique changes the reference without leaving the platform.

## What helps

Psychotherapy with a cognitive behavioural approach is the treatment with the strongest support for body dysmorphic disorder. It works on body perception, checking behaviours and fear of judgement. A professional decides whether medication is needed and rules out other causes of the distress.

In training, the goal moves from the mirror to performance: marks, sets and weeks of progress. A plan with fixed rest days and no daily weighing reduces the compulsion. The diet loosens in stages, because strict restriction keeps the cycle going.

The environment helps without surveillance: asking about mood before weight, not commenting on other bodies and respecting the limits a person sets. Competitive bodybuilding adds pressure of its own, and the guide to [bodybuilding beyond muscle](/en/bodybuilding-lifestyle/) describes that context.

## When to ask for help

A consultation is justified when the body takes up most of the day, when training replaces obligations or when thoughts of harm appear. Also with the use of [anabolic steroids](/en/steroids/), because the substance worsens the picture and adds heart and liver risk.

The first step does not require a diagnosis or a full confession. It is enough to raise it in a primary care appointment or with a psychologist and let the professional guide the way. Eating disorders have treatment, and the earlier it starts, the better the outlook.

Concern about the chest deserves a separate appointment: [gynecomastia](/en/gynecomastia/) has medical causes and a different approach. The search for shortcuts leads to supplements and substances, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) review what adds nothing to the plan.
