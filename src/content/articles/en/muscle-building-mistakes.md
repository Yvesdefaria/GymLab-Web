Getting big requires eating more and lifting more, and also knowing what the body needs and when. These eight habits show up again and again in the gym and explain why some months of effort leave no trace.

## Eating in excess

Treating a size phase as an excuse to eat whatever you like is a first-class ticket to the obese club. If you want to gain weight you have to eat more, but food choice still matters.

When the goal is muscle rather than fat, watch your macronutrients. A common split is 40% protein, 40% carbohydrates and 20% fat, or 50/40/10, with small adjustments as you progress.

Protein is the key to development. A 90-kilogram person needs 180 to 360 grams a day, spread across several meals. For the rest of your calories, choose vegetables, legumes, fruit, nuts and oats.

## Lifting too heavy

Big muscles come from big weights, but that simple line has led many athletes into joint problems and injuries. Training heavy for 3 to 5 reps builds strength, not necessarily size.

If you lift badly, you do not even get that strength. Strict reps, with enough of them per set, are the road to size. Muscle needs concentrated stimulation, not a violent heave to finish the rep.

Use loads that let you do 6 to 12 controlled reps with good technique. The mirror and the scale will notice soon enough.

## Too many single-joint exercises

A two-hour biceps routine does not build the arms you want. Neither does 15 sets of curls twice a week. The usual estimate is that every 5 kilograms of muscle adds 2 to 2.5 centimetres to your arm, and those centimetres come from global work, not the triceps machine.

Build your programme on multi-joint exercises. The deadlift, squat, leg press, bench press, barbell row and overhead press force the body to develop more muscle.

Start every session, whatever the muscle of the day, with compound movements.

## Too much cardio

You want muscle without fat, so you add five or six hard cardio sessions a week, some of them an hour long. Then you wonder why you are gaining no muscle at all.

The body needs energy to tolerate that volume. When glycogen runs out it may turn to fat, but sometimes it chooses muscle, the last thing you want to lose. Frequent cardio also drains the energy you need to move heavy weights.

Limit cardio to no more than three moderate sessions a week while you are chasing size.

## Adding volume without limit

When progress stalls, the first reaction is usually to do more. Sometimes less means more.

Muscle does not respond to raw amounts of work. Training too often or doing too many sets leads to overtraining and makes it much harder to gain size or lose fat.

Limit sets to 12 to 16 per muscle group. To add stimulus, use drop sets, supersets or forced reps from time to time. They take the muscle past failure without inflating the volume.

## Overusing machines

Machines have advantages, but nothing replaces the effect of barbells and dumbbells on muscle. The fascination with every new apparatus has people queuing for the guided press instead of moving heavy objects against gravity.

The Smith machine and cables work well and fit size and definition goals. The problem is making them the base.

Limit them to one or two exercises per muscle group in each workout. The rest of the plan should rest on free weights.

## Getting dehydrated

Water increases blood volume and helps limit the muscle breakdown caused by free radicals. Even so, many people do not drink until the session is over.

Hard training raises your water needs above those of a sedentary person. Aim for 3 to 5 litres a day, spread across the day.

## Limiting carbohydrates

You cannot live on protein alone. The bad reputation of carbohydrates has pushed many athletes away from the macronutrient that helps them grow most.

Carbohydrates are an ally, especially in a size phase. Take 4 to 5 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, mostly from slow-digesting sources such as wholemeal bread and oats.

The exception is post-workout: that is when 60 to 100 grams of fast-digesting carbs help refill muscle glycogen.
