**Bodybuilders** assume every exercise must travel through its full range of motion (ROM). However, a full range is not always necessary and can even work against certain goals. If you want to succeed in the long run, you need to know when to use it and when to leave it out.

## What full range of motion is

**Full range of motion (ROM)** is the path from the maximum stretch position to the full contraction of an exercise. When you try to develop and shape muscle, working through the full ROM is necessary because it hits every muscle fibre from every possible angle and increases **intensity** by contracting the muscles more often.

That increase in volume helps build bigger, more complete muscles, and it is one reason bodybuilders look different from powerlifters.

## The right range for each exercise

ROM means something different when you talk about multi-joint exercises for size. In a **bench press**, the pectorals are not the only muscles working: the triceps and deltoids take part too.

Using the full range would mean locking the elbows at the end of every rep, which has three consequences:

1. It does not increase intensity on the pectorals.
2. It makes the triceps fatigue earlier, so they cannot contribute to the pectoral effort. Since the goal of the bench press is to work the pectorals, unless you are doing a close-grip press, you are better off stopping before you fully extend the arm.
3. It puts unnecessary pressure on the joints and can increase the risk of long-term injury.

Leave triceps training for isolation movements such as cable pushdowns and dumbbell kickbacks.

## The rules of ROM

These are the general rules for using the most effective range of travel.

### Single-joint isolation exercises

On leg extensions, triceps pushdowns and concentration curls, use a full ROM: stretch at the end position of the movement and squeeze hard at full contraction. To do that, make sure you use a weight that is not excessive.

### Heavy exercises for mass

On heavy movements, work through a good ROM, but without fully stretching the joints or squeezing and holding the contraction. The bench press and the leg press are good examples.

### Partial reps

Do not use them all the time. Limit them to the last or last two sets of an exercise, mainly to finish off a muscle group.

## When to shorten the range and why

Shortening the ROM makes sense when your goal is the weak part of a lift or a specific fibre. The **Curl 21**, also known as 21s, is the classic example: seven reps in the lower half of a biceps curl, seven in the upper half and seven more through the full range to exhaust the arms completely.

During the lower half of the movement, the brachialis and brachioradialis do more of the work, while the biceps handles the upper half. A partial ROM lets you focus on different muscle fibres and promotes complete biceps development. Remember that the last seven terrible reps use the full range of motion.

Partial reps also work as a strength progression. Start with a heavy weight you cannot move through the full range, add load little by little and use that short range until you can complete one full rep with that weight. Then carry that strength into the full movement.

The range is not a fixed rule. On isolation work, travel the whole path; on heavy movements, protect the joints; at weak points, shorten it on purpose. If you feel that the stretch is limiting you, [stretching](/en/stretching/) helps you recover range without losing tension. On guided lifts such as the [Smith machine](/en/smith-machine-sticking-points/) you can set the stops and work the same range every time.

To choose the load you use in each range, check the [optimal rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/). And if your execution falls apart when you shorten the movement, go back to the guide to [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) before adding weight.
