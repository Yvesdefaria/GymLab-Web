Rules about what not to mix have circulated for decades, and almost all share one idea: the digestive tract is an assembly line that takes one nutrient at a time. Physiology says otherwise. The stomach mixes, acidifies and empties at its own pace, and the small intestine digests protein, fat and starch in parallel.

## What is claimed and what can be checked

Food-combining diets start from a premise: put protein and carbohydrate together and the body cannot handle both, so one of them turns into fat. The premise fails at the first step. Digestive enzymes work at the same time and do not compete for a single channel.

Speed does change. A meal heavy in fat or fibre takes longer to leave the stomach. That slowness explains the heaviness after a large plate; it does not mean the nutrients are lost on the way.

## Rule 1: acid with starch

Starch starts breaking down in the mouth with salivary amylase, which works poorly in an acid medium. That is where the advice against mixing lemon, vinegar or acid fruit with potato, bread or rice comes from.

The missing detail is that salivary amylase does only part of the job. Pancreatic and intestinal enzymes finish digestion in a different medium. Stool analyses of people who ate these combinations found no sign of poor starch digestion. Dressing potatoes with lemon is not a problem.

## Rule 2: sugar with starch or protein

The popular version says sugar waits in the stomach, ferments and produces gas. The stomach does not ferment sugars to any meaningful extent: its contents are acid, and they pass into the intestine at a rate set by the volume and composition of the meal.

A dessert after a large plate takes longer to reach the intestine. That does not turn it into gas or a toxin. If a sweet food makes you feel bad, the cause is usually the size of the meal or a specific sugar.

## Rule 3: fat with protein

This rule has a real mechanism. Fat delays gastric emptying, and a very fatty meal digests more slowly. A steak with butter sits heavier than the same steak off the grill, and the difference is felt in the stomach.

The consequence is digestive. Spreading fat across the day instead of concentrating it in one meal avoids the slump afterwards. There is no need to keep olive oil away from meat.

## Rule 4: don't mix proteins

The idea that two protein sources on one plate get in each other's way has no support. The gut secretes proteases for all the protein that arrives, and amino acid absorption depends on the daily total. Egg and tuna in the same salad digest without conflict.

## Rule 5: milk and meat

The story of a film that milk forms around meat and that blocks gastric juices does not hold: milk mixes with the stomach contents like any other food. What does exist is lactose intolerance, which causes gas and discomfort from a lack of lactase.

If milk makes you feel bad, the problem is lactose. The guide to [fixing lactose intolerance](/en/lactose-intolerance-and-training/) explains the options.

## Vitamin C and plant iron

Here is a mechanism worth using. Iron from plants is absorbed less efficiently than iron from meat, and vitamin C improves that absorption when taken in the same meal. A squeeze of lemon on lentils, pepper in a bean stew or an orange for dessert help the iron in the plate go further.

The effect is modest and does not replace treatment for anaemia. If iron is low, blood work and a doctor set the path.

## How to build the plate

The useful rule talks about foods, not combinations: a protein source, a serving of carbohydrate, vegetables and some fat. That structure covers what the body needs without banning mixtures or forcing each nutrient to be eaten alone.

To spread it across the day, the guide to [protein and carbs together](/en/protein-and-carbs-together/) orders meals without magic formulas. If the doubt is a shake that sits badly, the guide to [shakes versus whole foods](/en/shakes-vs-whole-foods/) compares both options. And [beans](/en/beans-for-muscle/) are a good example of a plate that brings plant protein, fibre and starch together without any conflict.
