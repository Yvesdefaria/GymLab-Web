Alcohol has a social advantage that no other molecule shares and a metabolic bill that comes due in the order it is drunk. For someone who trains, the useful question is not whether one drink ruins the week, but how much alcohol is in that drink, what happens that night and what happens in the muscle once the session is over.

## How much alcohol is in a glass

A standard drink in Spain equals 10 g of pure alcohol, about 70 kcal. A 330 ml beer at 5 % carries about 13 g; a 150 ml glass of wine at 13 %, about 15 g; a 40 ml spirit at 40 %, about 13 g. The formula is millilitres times strength times 0.8, and it works for any bottle.

The calories in alcohol add to those of the drink rather than replacing them. Beer also carries carbohydrate, and a cocktail can hold as much sugar as a snack. Counting units rather than glasses avoids comparing containers that are not the same size.

## What happens in the body

Alcohol is absorbed quickly in the stomach and small intestine. The liver handles around 95 %, at a limited rate of roughly 0.1 g per kilogram of body weight per hour. Eating first and drinking slowly lower the peak; the total and the time set the rest.

Nothing speeds that rate up. Coffee clears the feeling of sleepiness and does not lower blood alcohol. Water hydrates and does not remove alcohol. A cold shower restores alertness for a few minutes and leaves the same amount of alcohol in the blood. The only way to lower the number is to wait.

## Fragmented sleep

Alcohol shortens the time it takes to fall asleep and makes the night worse. It reduces REM sleep, increases awakenings in the second half and worsens snoring and apnoea. A drink before bed acts as a sedative and changes the architecture of sleep: the night ends up lighter.

For someone who trains, the effect adds up. Deep sleep is the window where the hormonal response is regulated and physical recovery is completed. The guide to [how much sleep you need to progress](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) describes that link without turning it into a promise.

## After training: protein synthesis

A trial in young men gave a high dose of alcohol, 1.5 g per kilogram, after a strength session. Muscle protein synthesis fell by about 25 % in the following hours, along with anabolic signalling. The dose was high and the study small, and it still points in a clear direction: alcohol does not help build muscle.

Protein in the meal afterwards matters more than any drink. If dinner includes 30 or 40 g of protein, the daily balance improves, but alcohol still takes its cut in the immediate window. The guide to [muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) sorts out the other factors, and the one on [frozen foods](/en/frozen-foods/) solves dinner without improvising.

## Performance and hangover

One or two units the night before do not change strength in a measurable way for most people. The problem appears with more: coordination, reaction time and hydration get worse, and the next day's hangover reduces the load that can be moved and the quality of technique.

Alcohol also changes body temperature and perceived effort, two factors that matter in long sessions. In endurance sport, prior dehydration adds to the dehydration of training.

## What does not work

Coffee, cold showers, salt water and miracle supplements do not speed up alcohol metabolism. Advice that promises to prevent a hangover usually confuses relieving symptoms with removing the cause.

Only time cures a hangover, plus some water and food if the stomach tolerates it. The guide to [wine and health](/en/is-wine-healthy/) reviews resveratrol without turning it into an excuse, and the one on [alcohol and creatine](/en/creatine-and-alcohol/) settles whether drinking cancels the supplement.

## How much is little

Public health guidance places low-risk drinking at around one unit a day for adults, and some bodies recommend less. Zero risk exists only at zero alcohol, and that sentence matters most for cancer and the liver. No amount improves athletic performance.

For competitors, the simplest rule is to keep units away from key sessions and from the nights before competition. The guide to [sex and performance](/en/sex-and-athletic-performance/) covers the other superstition of the night before.

## When to seek help

If drinking costs more than it gives, if the amount creeps up or if anxiety appears on days without alcohol, talk to a professional. The [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) page does not replace that conversation, and no supplement makes up for a night that keeps repeating.
