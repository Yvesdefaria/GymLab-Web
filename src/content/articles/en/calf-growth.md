The calves are a small group that walks with you all day. They tolerate high frequency and, for most lifters, recover quickly; even so, they are among the last to show change. The explanation lies in the anatomy and in how they are trained.

## Why calves are stubborn

The soleus has a high proportion of slow fibres, resistant to fatigue, while the gastrocnemius mixes fast and slow fibres. That difference explains two things: the soleus tolerates long sets and frequent work, and the gastrocnemius responds better to heavy loads with moderate reps.

Neither of them is doomed to stay the same. They are postural muscles, used to working for hours without rest, so they need a stimulus that goes beyond what walking already gives them. With conventional sets and light weight, the margin stays small.

## The knee rule

The gastrocnemius crosses the knee and the ankle. The soleus crosses only the ankle. That anatomical difference decides the exercise.

With the leg straight, the gastrocnemius leads. With the knee bent, the gastrocnemius loses tension and the work passes to the soleus. That is why standing raises are gastrocnemius work and seated raises are soleus work. Neither one trains both muscles at once.

The soleus does not cross the knee, so it does not stabilise it: its job is the ankle. It is also the larger of the two by mass, even though it sits hidden under the gastrocnemius. The [calf and soleus anatomy](/en/calf-anatomy/) covers the attachments and the reason for that difference.

## The best mass movements

The exercises fall into two types: those done with straight legs and those done seated. With straight legs the gastrocnemius works; seated, the soleus takes over.

In a session with both, do the standing work first and the seated work after. The gastrocnemius is what gives the visible shape of the calf, and it deserves to be trained while the leg is fresh.

Toes pointing forward is the strongest position and the one that lets you move the most weight. Turning them in or out changes the emphasis very little.

## Execution cues

- **Knees unlocked.** In standing movements, keep a slight bend so the joint is not loaded in full extension.
- **Full range.** Rise until you are on your toes and lower until the ankle is completely stretched. The stretch at the bottom is part of the stimulus.
- **Slow tempo.** Control the lowering and feel the work. When fatigue arrives, speed the lift up a little to get past the sticking point.
- **Constant tension.** Do not bounce or rest between reps: the heel touches the bottom and comes straight back up.

## The mass routine

Three exercises, two standing and one seated. It is the original routine, with the same numbers.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Donkey calf raise | 5 * | 15, 12, 12, 20, 20 |
| Standing calf raise machine | 5 * | 15, 12, 12, 20, 20 |
| Seated calf raise | 4 | 15, 15, 20, 20 |

> **\*** The five sets include a 15-rep warm-up set. Do not take it to failure.

Pick a weight that reaches failure at the target reps of each set. The last two sets of each exercise are congestion sets: lower the weight a little and chase more reps. [Muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains how to recognise it and when to stop.

## The beginner session (0-3 months)

For the first months, two exercises are enough: one standing and one seated. Do the standing one first.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standing calf raise | 3 * | 15, 12, 12 |
| Seated calf raise | 3 * | 15, 12, 12 |

> **\*** The three sets include a 15-rep warm-up set. Never take it to failure.

At this stage you do not need to reach failure on the working sets. Choose a weight that lets you complete the reps with good technique and leaves one or two in reserve.

## The home routine

A little equipment covers both positions. The single-leg raise replaces the standing machine and the seated dumbbell version replaces the seated machine.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single-leg standing calf raise | 4 | 15, 12, 12, 20 |
| Seated calf raise with a dumbbell | 4 | 15, 12, 12, 20 |

For the seated version, sit on the edge of a flat bench with the balls of your feet on a block 8 to 10 centimetres high. Place a dumbbell or a few plates on your knees to create the resistance. If you do not have a bench, any stable surface at knee height will do. Hold the weight with one hand so it does not shift. Switch legs between sets so both do the same work.

The last set of each exercise is a congestion set: lower the weight and add reps.

## How to progress

Calves tolerate frequency. Two or three sessions a week work better than one long session, especially if the total volume is spread out. Two sessions separated by a rest day are usually enough.

Add weight when you complete every rep of every set through the full range. If you fall short on the last set, repeat the same weight the following week. If the weight does not move for weeks, check the range before adding sets: half a rep does not count.

High-intensity techniques belong at the end of a set, not in all of them. The [Weider principles](/en/weider-principles/) sort out the options, and the guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/) helps spread the sets without overdoing it. To choose between the two positions, the guide to [standing or seated raises](/en/standing-vs-seated-calf-raises/) sums up the rule in one page.
