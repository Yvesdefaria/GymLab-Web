A tub of protein is milk, soy or egg processed into powder, with fat and sugar removed to different degrees. That simple definition explains most of what follows: when it helps, how much to take and why most differences between products come down to price and tolerance, not results.

## The daily total before the tub

For strength training, the reference is 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight per day. An 80 kg athlete needs between 128 and 176 grams. For endurance the figure drops to 1.2 or 1.6 grams, because the goal is repair without chasing hypertrophy.

Distribution matters too. Each meal should carry 25 to 40 grams of protein, across three to five meals during the day. That pattern covers protein synthesis at each point better than one large evening meal. Powder comes in when food falls short of the number, not before.

## Concentrate, isolate and hydrolysate

Concentrate is the most common and cheapest form. It keeps some fat and lactose, mixes well and works for daily use. Isolate goes through more filtration: protein per 100 grams rises, lactose drops below 1 gram and fewer carbohydrates remain. It suits anyone who digests lactose poorly or wants a nearly pure powder.

Hydrolysate is treated with enzymes that break the chains into short peptides. It absorbs sooner and costs more. That speed does not turn into more muscle when the daily total is the same; the practical difference is price and, for some people, digestive tolerance. The full process is explained in the guide to [whey protein production](/en/whey-protein-production/).

## Casein, soy and egg

Casein is the majority protein in milk. It forms clots in the stomach and releases amino acids over several hours, so it fits a meal far from training or before bed. It is not mandatory: its advantage is satiety and slow release, not a magic overnight effect.

Soy is a complete protein and the most widespread plant option. It supplies all nine essential amino acids and a leucine density somewhat lower than whey. Egg protein has an excellent profile and suits anyone who does not tolerate dairy, with the obvious caution about egg allergy.

Bovine colostrum is sold as a recovery aid. The available studies are inconclusive and there is no established dose for improving muscle mass. The evidence does not reach the point of recommending it.

## When powder solves something

The supplement makes sense when total protein falls short. That happens during a cut, when calories and appetite both drop; for people with little time to cook; and for anyone who needs 150 grams a day and tires of chicken breast. It also helps people who cannot eat dairy, meat or egg because of allergy or preference.

It makes no sense if you already hit the number with food. A shake is a convenience, not a lever on body composition, metabolism or diet quality. The guide to [buying protein powder](/en/protein-powder-guide/) shows how to read the label and compare prices without looking at the brand.

## The post-workout shake

The idea that protein must arrive within thirty minutes of the last set does not hold up. The daily total and its distribution decide; the shake afterwards is a convenient way to add a serving. If you train near home, a normal meal does the same job.

Carbohydrates are not a required transport for protein. They are fuel, they refill glycogen and they help performance in the next session, but protein is digested and used with or without them. The claim that protein is burned or wasted without carbs is false.

## How to choose without a brand

Look at protein per 100 grams, protein per scoop, lactose and the price of 25 grams of protein. With those four numbers, two tubs at the same price compare in ten seconds. The protein type comes after: concentrate for daily use, isolate if lactose bothers you, hydrolysate only if the budget allows.

Protein does not compete with [creatine](/en/creatine-supplement/): one supplies raw material and the other energy for hard sets. Together they cover two different needs.
