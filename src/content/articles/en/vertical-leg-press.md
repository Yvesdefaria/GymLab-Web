The vertical press is the least common of the three presses. The body lies or sits in a carriage that runs on two near-vertical rails, and the platform sits above the torso. It takes less weight than the incline press and it is not as common as the horizontal one, yet it has a property the other two do not offer as cleanly.

## What the vertical press does

The carriage runs up and down on two rails, the back rests on a pad and the legs push the platform upward. The knee and the hip extend together, and the load is shared by the quadriceps, the glute and the hamstrings. It is a pushing movement with the torso supported, like its siblings.

## The fixed angle and the rep

The pad fixes the torso angle, and that angle does not change during the set. On the incline press the trunk can roll a few degrees depending on how the pelvis sits; here there is no room for that. The practical consequence is that the bottom of the rep is defined by the same pair of knee and hip angles every time.

That turns the machine into a measuring tool. If last month you reached a certain depth with two plates and today you reach the same depth with three, the comparison is honest. On a press where the torso moves, the bottom changes with posture and the comparison is lost.

## How to do it

1. Set the pad so the pelvis and the lower back are supported.
2. Put the feet in the middle of the platform, at shoulder width.
3. Hold the handles and keep the head neutral.
4. Go down by bending knee and hip, with the whole back on the pad.
5. Push through the whole foot and stop extension before the knee locks.

## Range and the pelvis

The descent ends while the pelvis stays on the pad. If the lower back peels off or the pelvis rolls forward, that is today's limit. On a press the pad supports, and when the pelvis moves the load changes place.

Range is measured by that signal. A shorter rep with a stable pelvis trains more than a deeper one with the back off the pad.

## What the machine does not do

The archive credited the vertical press with three properties that do not hold.

The first, that it maximises quadriceps isolation. A press extends knee and hip, and the hip is not fixed: the glute and the hamstrings join in on every rep.

The second, that it is popular in home gyms. It is the least common of the three presses, and its size does not make it more domestic than an incline.

The third, that it is safe for a painful lower back. The pad supports the back, and that is not a clearance. A supported back is not a diagnosis, and loading a machine with an active complaint is something to raise with a professional first.

All three share the movement and differ in the direction of the push. The [incline leg press](/en/incline-leg-press/) takes more load, the [horizontal leg press](/en/horizontal-leg-press/) is the easiest to start on and the [single-leg press](/en/single-leg-leg-press/) covers the unilateral work.

## Common errors

The first is lifting the pelvis to go deeper. The lower back loses the pad and the load goes where the pad was meant to protect.

The second is locking the knees with a jolt at the top. Extension stops before the lock, with the leg active.

The third is pushing with the toes. The whole foot shares the load and keeps the heel down.

The fourth is using a short range with a lot of weight. Without range, the machine delivers less than it promises.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose is three sets of ten to twelve reps with ninety to a hundred and twenty seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this is the guide's recommendation for a medium-load machine.

## Where it fits

It works as the second exercise of leg day, after the squat, and as the main lift in a machine session.
