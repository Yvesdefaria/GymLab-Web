Specialty coffee is sold with a story of origin, roast and cup. For training, what matters is the caffeine it carries and when it is taken. The rest is flavour, and flavour counts when it helps you skip sugar.

## What caffeine does in a session

Caffeine blocks the receptors that adenosine uses to build the feeling of sleepiness. Doing so lowers perceived effort: the same load feels lighter. It also raises adrenaline release and improves muscle contractility in laboratory tests.

The practical result is a small but measurable gain in endurance and, at higher doses, in strength and power. It is an aid for performance during the session; muscle and meals depend on other things.

## The dose is calculated by weight

The range supported by reviews on caffeine and performance is 3 to 6 milligrams per kilo of body weight. The figure uses body weight, not cup count, because an espresso and a filter coffee do not carry the same amount.

Turning milligrams into cups means checking the method. A 30-millilitre espresso delivers 60 to 80 milligrams. A 200 to 250-millilitre filter coffee sits around 90 to 120. A 75-kilo person aiming for 4 mg/kg needs about 300 milligrams: four short espressos or three cups of filter.

The arithmetic shifts with variety, grind, coffee-to-water ratio and extraction time. Measure once at home rather than trusting intuition.

## Sixty minutes before

Caffeine peaks in blood between 30 and 60 minutes after it is taken. For a session or a competition, the reasonable window is 45 to 60 minutes before the start.

Taking it five minutes before the first set does little: the peak arrives while training is already under way. Debuting the dose on competition day is a bad idea. Test it in training, with the same brand and the same method.

## The ceiling and tolerance

For healthy adults, the European Food Safety Authority sets the limit at 400 milligrams a day from all sources: coffee, tea, soft drinks, chocolate and supplements. Spreading that amount across several doses does not raise it. In practice, that ceiling cuts off the top of the table for many people.

Regular use builds tolerance. After several weeks the same dose delivers less, and going without brings a headache and fatigue for a day or two. Tapering the dose or taking a couple of caffeine-free days before a test helps restore the response.

Coffee is not a painkiller and does not treat an injury. The evidence on delayed muscle soreness is weak, and using it to train through pain hides the problem instead of solving it.

## The bill arrives as sleep

The half-life of caffeine is about five hours. Half of a 300-milligram dose is still in the body five hours later, and a quarter ten hours later. A coffee at six in the evening leaves almost half of that caffeine active at midnight.

Sleep is when training adaptations consolidate. A late coffee that delays sleep costs more performance than the session gains. As a practical rule, the last dose comes eight to ten hours before bed; slow metabolisers need more margin.

## Specialty coffee, without magic

Specialty coffee is defined by quality beans, traceability and a cupping score above 80 out of 100. It tastes better and is enjoyable without sugar, which is a real advantage for anyone watching liquid calories.

It has no extra properties because it is called specialty. Caffeine depends on dose and method, not on the seal. Torrefacto coffee is roasted with sugar, so it adds sugar and a more bitter taste.

Milk and syrups are the part of coffee that adds the most calories. A whole-milk cappuccino with two spoons of sugar can approach 200 calories without enough protein to count as a meal.

## Fitting it into the day

A coffee before training and a meal with carbohydrates and protein afterwards cover the session. Coffee does not replace breakfast or the pre-training meal; if the stomach tolerates it, drink it with something solid.

Anyone training at night chooses decaf or skips the dose. Caffeine does not compensate for a broken sleep plan.
