## What the guided bar changes

On a free-bar decline, part of the effort goes into keeping the bar from drifting sideways. On the Smith machine, that cost disappears: the bar travels in its track and the hooks catch it anywhere in the range. You can push a rep close without a spotter and slide out from underneath.

Everything else stays. The bar follows a fixed arc that does not adapt to your arm length, and the shoulder takes the same load as in any decline press. The machine removes the cost of balance, not the cost to the joint.

## The setup: bench, feet and safeties

Set the bench between 15 and 30 degrees of decline. More tilt adds nothing: the head drops too low, the body slides towards the shoulders and the range becomes awkward.

Secure the feet under the rollers or on the bench support, knees bent. The thighs stay fixed and the hips do not move during the set.

Shift the bench until the bar, resting in the hooks, sits over the lower chest. Take it out with the arms extended and bring it to a vertical line over the sternum, never crossing the neck. If the rack cannot give you that position, move the bench, not the head.

Set the safeties at chest height. On this machine they are the net that lets you push the last rep: if the bar will not rise, it rests on the safeties and you slide out. Returning the bar to the hooks on a decline is harder than on a flat bench, so the safeties get checked before the first set, not after.

## Execution

Lower under control for two seconds until the bar touches the lower chest. The elbows stay at about 45 degrees from the torso, never flared to 90. The bar touches without bouncing and rises along the same path.

Press without locking the elbow hard at the end. Breathe in before the descent and release the air once the bar passes the hardest point of the press.

The back does not lift off the pad and the hips do not rise to press. If the hips come up, the angle changes mid-set and the chest stops being the muscle that works.

## The cost of the head-down position

For the whole set, the head sits below the heart. That position is not a problem in itself, but it is not comfortable: blood pools in the head and some people feel pressure, congestion or a headache at the end of the set. If that happens, cut the set and change the variation.

The way out costs more too. With the head low, returning the bar to the hooks asks for a movement you cannot see and make blind. That is why the setup includes safeties or a spotter, without exception.

And the guided bar does not protect the shoulder. The arc is what it is, and a badly placed bench repeats the same forced position on every rep. The machine removes balance, not technique.

## Dose

Three or four sets of 8 to 12 reps with 90 to 120 seconds of rest and a two-second descent. The last set runs to the safeties: you lower until the bar will not rise and it rests.

The strength variant is five sets of 5 or 6 reps with 2 or 3 minutes of rest and the safeties checked. The weight goes up once 12 reps come out with the same range and no bounce.

## Where it belongs in the week

The decline press goes after the flat or incline press and before isolation. Open the session with it only when the lower chest is the priority of the block; in a normal week, use it as the second press.

The [decline barbell press](/en/decline-barbell-press/) is the reference for the movement and covers the unrack with a free bar. The [decline dumbbell press](/en/decline-dumbbell-press/) changes the implement and splits the load per side. The [Smith machine incline press](/en/smith-machine-incline-press/) covers the upper chest with the same guided bar, and the [Smith machine bench press](/en/smith-machine-bench-press/) is the flat version.

The [Smith machine routine](/en/smith-machine-routine/) combines these movements into a full session. [Chest dips](/en/chest-dips/) add a bodyweight press that does not depend on the bar.
