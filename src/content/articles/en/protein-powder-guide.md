The bigger tub is not always the cheaper one, and the powder that promises more grams per scoop does not always deliver more protein. The label settles both questions in under a minute, if you know what to compare.

## The numbers that decide a purchase

The nutrition table carries three numbers that matter and several that only decorate. The first is protein per 100 grams of powder. A whey concentrate sits around 70 to 80 grams; an isolate reaches 85 or 90. The second is protein per scoop, because the scoop may weigh 25 or 40 grams and the number of servings changes with it. The third is the total in the tub: multiply servings by protein per serving.

Those figures let you compare products without looking at the brand. A 2 kg tub with 80 grams of protein per 100 grams holds 1,600 grams of protein. At 60 euros, 25 grams of protein costs 0.94 euros. A 1 kg isolate with 90 grams per 100 grams holds 900 grams; at 60 euros a tub, 25 grams costs 1.67 euros. The concentrate wins on price, and the isolate only makes sense if lactose bothers you or the carbohydrate total has to stay very low.

## The price of 25 grams of protein

That sum is the only honest comparison between tubs of different sizes. It works for any product, including powder sold loose. Divide the price by the total grams of protein and multiply by 25. If the result is higher than the price of a chicken breast that supplies the same protein, the powder is expensive for what it gives.

## Leucine: the number that almost never appears

Leucine is the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis. The studies that measure that response use 2.5 to 3 grams of leucine per meal, and that amount appears in 25 or 30 grams of whey protein. The label rarely declares it, but it can be estimated: whey is about 10% leucine, casein 9% and soy 8%. A shake with 30 grams of whey protein supplies about 3 grams. You do not need the exact number on the tub; a generous serving of complete protein clears the threshold.

## Lactose, digestion and allergy

Concentrate keeps lactose, between 4 and 8 grams per serving depending on the filtration grade. Isolate cuts it below 1 gram, and so does hydrolysate. Anyone who digests lactose poorly notices fewer symptoms with isolate. Anyone with a milk protein allergy should use none of the three: whey is a dairy protein, not a dairy-free product. The guide to [whey protein production](/en/whey-protein-production/) explains that difference between intolerance and allergy in more detail.

## How much protein you need per day

For strength training, the reference is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day. An 80 kg athlete needs between 128 and 176 grams. Distribution matters: 25 to 40 grams per meal, across three to five meals, covers protein synthesis at each point. Powder closes the gap between what you eat and that number; it does not replace food. If eggs, meat, fish and dairy already cover the total, a shake adds convenience and nothing else.

## The post-workout shake does not open a window

For years the advice was to take protein within thirty minutes of the last set or the muscle would not grow. The evidence does not support the rush. What decides is the daily total and how it is spread out. The shake afterwards is convenient, especially if you train far from home, but a meal with protein an hour or two later produces a similar effect.

Carbohydrates are not a required transport for protein either. They are fuel and they refill glycogen; protein is digested and used with or without them. Adding sugar to the shake does not improve the delivery of amino acids to muscle.

## Signs of a label that hides something

Proprietary blends hide the individual doses. Amino spiking adds glycine or taurine to inflate the protein number the lab measures, even though those amino acids do not build muscle the same way. If the ingredient list includes glycine, taurine or creatine inside a protein blend, the protein figure per serving is worth less than it looks. Rankings and best-on-the-market badges are advertising, not evidence.

## When you do not need to buy

If you reach 1.6 grams per kilo with food and cooking is not a burden, powder adds nothing food does not give. Buying protein powder makes sense when the total falls short, when appetite drops during a cut or when there is no time to cook.
