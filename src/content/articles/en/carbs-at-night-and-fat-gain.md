The idea that dinner makes you fatter than lunch has intuitive appeal: you spend less energy at night. The missing detail is that overnight expenditure is a small part of the total, and the body does not keep its accounts in time slots.

## The clock does not change the sum

Metabolism keeps working while you sleep: breathing, temperature, heart, tissue repair. That expenditure is somewhat lower than during the day, but it does not switch off. A plate of pasta at nine provides the same calories as at two, and the body uses or stores them according to the daily balance.

Trials that compare the same diet with calories spread differently find no difference in weight when the total and protein are matched. The hour is an organisational variable, not an independent factor that moves fat.

What does change with the hour is context. At night there is more tiredness, less planning and a higher chance of snacking while cooking. Dinner is usually the meal with the most room for excess, and that explains many weight gains without blaming the carbohydrate.

## What decides weight

Three things rule: how many calories come in, how many go out and how long the plan lasts. Splitting them between breakfast, lunch and dinner is an organisational decision, not a variable that changes the physics of balance.

Sleep enters through behaviour. Sleeping little increases hunger, reduces satiety and makes it harder to choose well the next day. One short night does not make you fat on its own, but repeated over weeks it tilts the balance towards eating more.

## Why the myth survives

Many people who remove carbs from dinner also cut total calories, and weight falls. The explanation seems obvious, although the cause is the deficit rather than the hour. In a low-calorie diet, skipping dinner is a simple way to cut.

There is also a selection effect. People who eat late tend to eat more and worse: large portions, alcohol, desserts. The carbohydrate is not the problem; the whole dinner can be.

## When carbs at dinner make sense

If you train in the afternoon or evening, dinner is the meal that replaces spent glycogen. It is also a good chance to complete fibre and carbohydrates when the day fell short. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) gives amounts per kilo by goal.

If you train in the morning, dinner can be lighter and the bulk of carbs spread before and after the session. Neither option is superior because of the hour: you choose the one that fits the real schedule.

## The rebound is real

After a very restrictive diet, weight rises fast when normal eating returns. Part of that increase is glycogen and water: muscle refills its stores and holds fluid with them. Another part is accumulated appetite, which pushes you to eat more than planned.

The result is that the scale gains two or three kilos in a few days and it looks as if all the effort was lost. Most of it is not new fat, and it does not mean the body has a switch that hoards fat on purpose. The answer is to reintroduce food gradually and keep strength training.

Raising carbs all at once after weeks of restriction is not mandatory either. Adding them over two or three weeks, starting with the meals around training, lets appetite and weight settle without shocks.

## A dinner that works

A good dinner plate has a serving of protein, plenty of vegetables and a portion of carbohydrate adjusted to the day. A simple example: two-egg omelette with spinach, tomato salad and a slice of wholemeal bread. Or baked salmon with potato and broccoli.

The hour matters little; the gap before bed helps if there is reflux. Eating and lying down immediately feels bad for some people, and that is a digestive issue, not a metabolic one. The guide to [casein protein](/en/casein-protein/) covers the case of anyone who wants to close the day with an extra serving.

Dinners do not live apart from the rest of the day.
