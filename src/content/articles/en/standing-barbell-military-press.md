## What works

The anterior and lateral deltoids are the main engines. The triceps extends the elbow through the second half of the range and takes a large share of the work as the bar approaches lockout.

The clavicular head of the chest assists through the whole arc, not only at the start. Its line of pull follows the arm as it rises in front of the face, which is why the standing press also leaves work in the upper chest.

The traps and the serratus anterior stabilise and rotate the scapula so the arm has room above the head. The core and glutes keep the trunk rigid. In the standing version that last job is not optional. Without it, the bar does not travel in a straight line.

## The position

The feet go at hip width, with the weight spread between the middle of the foot and the heel. The knees stay unlocked and the glutes are tight.

The bar rests on the front of the shoulders, not on the collarbones. The grip sits just outside shoulder width, with the forearms vertical in the starting position. If the forearms lean in or out, the bar drifts away from the drive line.

Before unracking, lower the ribs and brace the abdomen. Breathe in, squeeze the glutes and push the floor away with the feet. The bar rises close to the face; as it passes the forehead, the head goes back and then through the gap the bar leaves.

Lockout ends with the bar over the mid foot, the elbows extended and the ribs where they started. If the ribs open and the lower back arches at the top, the press has become a standing chest push.

## Fixing the "thoracic hyperlordosis"

The thoracic spine is kyphotic at rest. It cannot be hyperlordosed, because its natural curve runs the other way. The cue to "lean the upper back back" does not describe a thoracic lordosis. It describes thoracic extension.

The difference matters. Thoracic extension makes room for the bar to pass in front of the face without the neck going back. It comes from ribs down and a braced abdomen, not from arching the lower back.

If the lower back arches, the bar finds a shorter path and the press gets easier for a while. The price shows up in the lower back, which holds the load in extension for the whole set.

## Standing or seated: an axial-load budget

The choice is an axial-load budget. Standing, the trunk and spine spend stability on every rep, and that stability is both the training effect and the limit.

Seated with a backrest, that spend drops and the shoulder gets more useful sets. Standing, the whole body takes part and the absolute load usually falls. Both versions train the same push with a different bill.

The Smith machine variant cuts the balance cost; it does not remove it completely. The bar is still guided and the body still has to hold the trunk. That trade has its own page in the [Smith machine behind-the-neck press](/en/smith-machine-behind-the-neck-press/).

## Dose

Four sets of 4 to 6 reps with 3 minutes of rest for strength. Or three sets of 6 to 8 reps with 2 or 3 minutes if the goal is hypertrophy and technique holds better with a few more reps.

The load goes up when the last set ends with the bar over the mid foot and the ribs down. If lockout breaks first, the weight has outrun the technique.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **Arching the lower back.** The bar finds a short path and the lower back pays for it.
- **Half reps.** Lowering only to the nose shortens the range and takes work away from the anterior deltoid.
- **Flaring the elbows too far.** The shoulder loses a stable position and the bar drifts off line.
- **Wrist back.** The bar sits badly and the drive is shared unevenly.
- **Looking at the ceiling.** The neck extends and the head stops following the bar's path.
