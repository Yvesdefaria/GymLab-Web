Two conversations get mixed together. One is about nutrients: protein, iron, zinc, B12 and creatine. The other is about risk: high intake, processed meat and cooking methods. Both are real, and neither makes sense without the other.

## What red meat provides

One hundred grams of lean beef delivers about 22 grams of complete protein, with every essential amino acid in useful amounts. That protein counts towards the 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo target, just like chicken or fish.

The iron in meat is heme iron and is absorbed better than iron from plants. B12 appears naturally only in animal foods, and the zinc in red meat is highly available. Creatine is there in real amounts too, although cooking destroys part of it and a steak is not the same as a supplement.

## The cut changes the fat

The supermarket label does not always help. The name of the cut does: some pieces barely pass 5 grams of fat per 100 grams, while others go beyond 20. The gap between the leanest and the fattiest in this table is about fourfold.

Filet mignon comes from the same muscle as the tenderloin, so it shares the lean profile. The idea that it is a fatty cut comes from its looks and its price rather than its composition. External fat can be trimmed with a knife; the fat inside the muscle, the marbling, cannot.

## Fresh or processed: two different categories

Fresh red meat and processed meat do not behave the same way in the studies. The International Agency for Research on Cancer puts processed meat in group 1, alongside tobacco and alcohol, and red meat in group 2A, probably carcinogenic. The group reflects the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk. A sausage and a cigarette are nothing alike.

What cohort studies show is an association between high, habitual consumption of processed meat and colorectal cancer. The association weakens when intake is occasional and the diet includes fibre, fruit and vegetables. Processed meat covers bacon, ham, sausages, salami and anything preserved by smoking, curing or salting. The guide to [cold cuts on a diet](/en/cold-cuts-on-a-diet/) goes into that category.

## How to cook it

At high temperature and in direct contact with flame, heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons form, two families of compounds that studies link to risk. There is no need to give up the grill: avoid white-hot charcoal, flare-ups and burnt patches.

Cooking at a lower temperature, turning the meat often, trimming the fat that drips and marinating beforehand all help reduce those compounds. The guide to [carcinogens in meat](/en/meat-carcinogens/) expands on the method.

## How much, and with what

A serving of 100 to 150 grams covers the protein for one meal. How often depends on the rest of the week: with fish, eggs and legumes in the rotation, red meat can appear less often without the diet losing anything.

It is worth serving it with vegetables and a source of vitamin C. The iron in meat is absorbed well on its own, but the whole plate improves. The guide to [food combinations](/en/food-combinations/) explains why.

## For anyone who trains

Red meat fits a gaining diet because of its protein density and because it satisfies. It also brings fat, and in a small surplus that counts. Choosing lean cuts most of the time leaves room for the rest of the meal.

The guide to [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) covers the food's historical role in training. If you want a cheap alternative, [beans](/en/beans-for-muscle/) bring protein and fibre without the fat.
