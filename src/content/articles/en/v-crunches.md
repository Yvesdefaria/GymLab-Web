It is the natural progression from the [floor crunch](/en/floor-crunches/): when twenty reps with the feet down come easy, lifting the legs changes the starting point and brings difficulty back to the same movement.

## What the raised legs add to V-crunches

The rectus abdominis is one muscle, from the pubis to the sternum. In a normal crunch it flexes the trunk with the pelvis supported. Here the hip flexors keep the legs up and the abs have to flex the trunk from that unstable base.

Raised legs shorten the hip flexors and pull the pelvis forward. For the torso to rise, the abs brake that pull and round the spine at the same time. The result is a shorter, more demanding movement than the basic crunch, with the same logic.

The split between an upper and a lower zone does not correspond to two muscles. What changes is the position of the pelvis: with the legs up, the hip is already forward, and the torso's range is measured from there.

## Starting position

- Lie flat on the mat with the legs straight and together in the air.
- Keep a slight bend in the knees, without locking them. The angle stays the same for the whole set.
- Stretch the arms over the torso, in line with the shoulders.
- Keep the chin off the chest and the gaze up and slightly forward.
- Press the lower back into the floor before the first rep.

## Execution

With a smooth movement, contract the abs to lift the torso and peel the shoulder blades off the floor. The ribs move towards the pelvis and the spine rounds forward.

Take the hands towards the feet as far as you can without pulling on the neck. The reference is the height of the sternum, not the fingertips: if the torso does not rise, reaching further with the arms does not fix the range.

Hold for a second at the top while you breathe out. Then lower slowly until the shoulder blades touch down, without dropping the legs or letting the lower back arch.

## Common mistakes

- Looking for momentum in the legs. If the knees dip and rise to help, the abs stop driving.
- Bending the knees mid-set to get higher. It changes the lever and you lose track of the set.
- Pulling on the neck with the hands. The neck pays for work the abs are not doing.
- Rising all the way to sitting. The useful range ends when the shoulder blades lift and the torso rounds.
- Holding your breath. Air goes out on the way up and comes back in at the bottom.

## Sets and how to finish it

Three sets of eight to twelve clean reps cover the exercise. The set ends when the torso stops rounding, even if the legs are still up.

The finisher in the original plan is a drop set: when no more reps come with the legs in the air, lower them and continue with regular floor crunches to failure. Dropping the legs shortens the lever and lets you squeeze the tired abs without changing exercise.

## How to progress

First, more reps inside the same range. Then more range: lift the torso a little higher without losing the curve of the spine. Finally, light load on the ankles or a plate on the chest, always with the legs still and the knee angle intact.

If the exercise gets messy, go back to the [floor crunch](/en/floor-crunches/) for a couple of weeks and regain control before adding difficulty.

## Where it fits

It is a bodyweight exercise for the middle of an abs session, when the range is warm and there is still strength to control the descent. It pairs well with a hip movement such as [reverse crunches](/en/reverse-crunches/), which work the other end of the same muscle.
