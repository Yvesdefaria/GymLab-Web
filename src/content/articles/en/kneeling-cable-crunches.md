The movement works the whole abdomen and allows something the floor cannot: adding load. With a rope and a high pulley the abs can be trained like any other muscle, with heavy sets and progress measured in plates.

It is close to the standing cable crunch, of which it is the kneeling version. The difference is momentum: from your knees it is harder to help yourself with the body, and that keeps the work where it belongs.

## Why it is the loaded lift of the block

In floor crunches the resistance is your own torso and it never changes. Here the resistance is a stack of plates and it goes up when it needs to. That opens the door to real hypertrophy: fewer reps, more tension and progress you can write down.

The range is longer too. The pulley pulls from above, so the bottom of the movement keeps tension and the abs do not rest until you let go of the rope.

## Kneeling or standing

The standing version is the more popular one and adds a balance demand: you have to brake the body so the weight does not drag you backwards. The kneeling version removes that problem and takes the legs out of it.

If you come from the standing version and struggle to feel the abs, try kneeling. The position forces trunk flexion and keeps the hips out of the movement.

## Starting position

Attach a rope handle to the top of the pulley and face the plate stack.

Stand far enough from the stack that the rope clears your head when you fold forward. That distance shows up on the first rep: if the rope grazes you, you are too close.

Keep the rope behind your head and locked in that relative position for the whole set. The hands do not move; the movement comes from the trunk.

Your thighs stay perpendicular to the floor. Fold slightly before you start so you feel tension in the abs, and keep the chin off the chest.

## Execution

Fold at the waist, contracting the abs and going as low as you can. The back rounds, and that rounding is what lets the abs shorten fully.

Do not sit back as you finish the movement. The thighs stay perpendicular to the floor for the whole rep; if the hips travel back, the exercise becomes a badly performed deadlift.

Hold the contracted position for a second. Then let the weight take you back to the start, controlling the return.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling with the arms and using the rope like a lat pulldown.
- Letting the hips travel back to go lower.
- Letting the rope slide away from behind the head.
- Loading too much and making up for it with half a range.
- Chasing failure before the technique is there.

## Loading and drop sets

This is the hypertrophy lift of the block: it takes more weight than any other ab movement and responds to classic progression. Add load when the higher reps come easily and hold the one-second contraction at the top.

It also takes a [drop set](/en/drop-sets/). At [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/), cut the weight by thirty per cent and keep going. The reduction is quick, with no rest, and the point is to accumulate reps with the same technique.

## When the set ends

The set ends when the fold comes from the hips rather than the abs, when the rope slips out of place or when the trunk stops coming down. Those are three signs that the work has gone somewhere else.

The original article sets no sets or reps. Weekly volume lives in the [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/) and in the [hypertrophy routine](/en/abs-hypertrophy-routine/), which places it under heavy load.

## Where it fits

It is the first movement of an ab session when the goal is load, and the last one when you want to finish with a drop set. Kneeling, the abs hit their limit before the legs do, so it does not steal strength from the squat or the deadlift.

The [ab machine](/en/machine-crunches/) is the alternative with a guided path and plates, easier to adjust and less demanding on coordination. If the goal of the day is moving kilos with the abs, the rope wins; if it is finishing a session tired, the machine will do.
