The lateral lunge travels to the side, not forward. That detail makes it one of the few leg movements that loads the hip in the frontal plane, and it explains the adductor and abductor work the squat leaves out.

## What the lateral lunge trains

The leg that bends does the main work: quadriceps and glutes brake the descent and push back up. The leg that stays extended keeps its foot on the floor and its adductors lengthen under load; on the way back to centre they help bring the body toward the midline. The abductors of the bent leg steady the pelvis so it does not drop.

The path is frontal, so the share changes compared with the forward lunge. The adductors and abductors take more load than in a narrow squat, and the hip moves in a plane a normal routine rarely loads. The goal is to load the movement, not to change the shape of one area of the thigh.

## The bodyweight version

Start standing with the feet at hip width and the hands on the hips or in front of the chest. Take a wide step to one side and plant the whole foot. The knee on the loaded side bends and the hip moves back, as if you were sitting on a chair placed to one side. The other leg straightens and its foot stays flat.

The torso leans slightly forward from the hip with the spine extended. The eyes look forward. From the bottom, push with the foot of the bent leg and return to centre. You can do all the reps on one side and change, or alternate.

The stepping foot has to travel. The cue not to lift the feet turns the lunge into a narrow squat and shares the work badly. The foot travels, but it does not drag: lift it just enough to place it and plant it whole.

## The barbell version

With the bar on the traps, the step shortens at first. The bar adds axial load and makes the demand on the pelvis in the frontal plane obvious: if the hip cannot hold, the torso drifts toward the step or the knee caves.

Set the bar as in a high-bar squat, with the hands a little wider than the shoulders. Take the step, descend under control and return to centre. The technique is the same as in the bodyweight version; only the load and the time you need to stabilise change.

## Common errors

The first is a short step. The lunge turns into a narrow squat and the extended leg loses its job.

The second is dragging the foot. The foot travels, lands flat and does not slide along the floor.

The third is letting the knee cave inward. The knee follows the line of the foot, especially at the bottom.

The fourth is rounding the back. The torso leans from the hip with the spine extended; if the back curves, the step is too long or the weight is too high.

The fifth is bouncing out of the bottom. On a loaded lunge the rebound shows up in the knee carrying the weight.

## Sets, reps and rest

Recommendation from this page: three sets of eight to ten reps per side, with ninety seconds of rest. The archive gave no numbers. Start with bodyweight until the step and the control of the pelvis match on both sides.

## Where it fits

It is a good unilateral exercise after the main lift, or the main lift in a session with little equipment. It pairs with the [dumbbell reverse lunge](/en/dumbbell-reverse-lunge/) and the [dumbbell lunge](/en/dumbbell-lunge/), which cover the sagittal plane; the comparison between [static and walking lunges](/en/static-vs-walking-lunges/) orders the other two variants.

[Cable hip adduction](/en/cable-hip-adduction/) loads the adductor group through a short range, and the [hip abduction machine](/en/hip-abduction-machine/) covers the other side of the hip. The [leg exercises](/en/leg-exercises/) guide places the lunge in the unilateral block.
