The advantage over the dumbbell is simple: the weight changes with a pin and you never lift anything off the floor. That convenience lets you work with medium sets, with very light high-rep sets or with drop sets at the end, without changing exercises.

## Why the pulley

With a dumbbell, the resistance changes direction as the body leans. With the pulley, the cable pulls towards the same point and tension stays similar through the range. The difference shows most at the bottom, where the dumbbell loses tension and the cable does not.

The exercise also works from your knees. The movement is almost identical and the only difference is the height of the body relative to the cable.

## The arm is a rigid link

This is the detail that decides whether the exercise works. The arm does not pull the cable: it is a rigid piece joining the cable to the ribs. The elbow stays fixed at roughly 90 degrees and the shoulder does not move during the set.

If you pull with the arm, the work goes to the lats and the shoulder and the oblique stops taking load. The test is simple: if your arm or shoulder hurts more than your side at the end of the set, the link has moved.

## Starting position

Attach a D-handle to the top of the pulley. Stand side-on to the machine with one shoulder close to the handle.

Take the handle with the palm up so there is already tension in the oblique before you start. The elbow stays bent at about 90 degrees and locked in that position.

Feet at shoulder width and knees unlocked so you can balance. Head neutral, in line with the trunk, eyes forward.

## Execution

Bend sideways by contracting the obliques, as low as you can. The movement is smooth and continuous, with no jerking.

Hold the contraction for a second. Then let the weight take you back to the start, controlling the return.

Between reps, do not let the plates touch. If the stack lands, the oblique rests and the set stops making sense. Stopping a few centimetres above the base is enough.

## Loading: one plate at 12 reps

The archive's progression is concrete: once you can do about 12 reps with good form, add one plate and work back up to 12. That way the load climbs week by week and the rep count does not run away.

[Drop sets](/en/drop-sets/) fit well here. At [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/), cut the weight by thirty per cent and keep going with no rest. The point is accumulating reps with the same technique, not adding kilos at any cost.

On the waist, it pays to be clear: the obliques grow under load and a wider waist is the expected result of heavy work. Anyone who wants a narrow waist is better off staying with medium and light sets, as the [dumbbell side bend](/en/dumbbell-side-bend/) proposes.

## The double-contraction rep

The archive offers a variant to squeeze the range. Do a full rep, release only a few centimetres and contract downwards again. That second contraction counts as part of the same rep.

It builds tension at the short end, where the oblique is most shortened. It does not replace the normal set: use it on the last set or once the weight feels comfortable.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling with the arm and shoulder instead of bending the trunk.
- Drifting the hip towards the weight to go lower.
- Leaning forward and losing the lateral plane.
- Loading too much and making up for it with half a range.
- Letting the plate stack rest between reps.

## Where it fits

This is the easiest loaded oblique exercise to regulate, so it works at the start of an abs session or as the second movement after a heavy crunch. It also serves as a finisher with a drop set when there is no strength left for anything else.
