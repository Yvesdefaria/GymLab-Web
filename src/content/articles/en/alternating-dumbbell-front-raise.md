## One arm at a time

The name says it and it is worth keeping: one dumbbell rises while the other waits. The alternating version has one concrete advantage. The resting arm cannot push, the opposite shoulder does not drift forward, and the torso cannot find the swing that appears when both hands rise together.

The price is time. An alternating set lasts twice as long as a bilateral set with the same reps per arm, which is why it fits better in an isolation block than in a session with a clock. If the goal is to pile up work fast, the two-dumbbell version solves something else.

## What works

The anterior deltoid is the prime mover. It brings the arm forward and up, and it does so in all three grip positions below. The clavicular head of the chest assists through the first part of the range, while the arm is still low. The scapula rotates upward to make room for the humerus, and the serratus anterior and the upper traps do that job.

The lateral deltoid takes part as a stabiliser. The claim that a pronated grip involves it more does not hold: the grip changes the rotation of the arm, and rotation does not split the work between heads that way.

## The position

Stand with the feet at hip width and the knees unlocked. The dumbbell starts in front of the thigh, with the palm facing down or in and the thumb closed around the handle. The elbow stays slightly bent and keeps that angle through the whole lift.

The shoulder stays down. Before lifting, move the scapula away from the neck and brace the abdomen. The dumbbell rises in front of the body, on a line that passes near the shoulder, not across the centre of the chest.

## The grip: what changes and what does not

There are three ways to hold the dumbbell and all three train the same movement. The difference is in wrist comfort and a marginal emphasis.

The anterior deltoid is still the prime mover in all three cases. What changes is the elbow angle and the wrist position. Saying that a neutral grip hands the whole effort to the anterior deltoid, or that a pronated grip shares it with the lateral deltoid, tells a story the mechanics do not support.

The range is the proof. If the elbow keeps the same angle and the dumbbell rises to shoulder height, the arm makes the same gesture with any grip. The palm only decides how the wrist feels on the way.

## The endpoint

The dumbbell rises to shoulder height, with the arm parallel to the floor. Higher than that, the traps and the upper chest take over and the anterior deltoid stops leading. The soft elbow stays: if it locks, the arm becomes a rigid lever and the shoulder loses control of the top.

## Dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps per arm, with 45 or 60 seconds of rest. The load goes up when the last set takes the same path as the first and the torso does not move.

## Mistakes that show up early

- **Swinging the torso.** The body follows the dumbbell and the shoulder gets less work.
- **Rising above the shoulder.** The traps take over and the anterior deltoid stops being the target.
- **Using hip drive.** A pull at the start unloads the shoulder and turns the raise into a throw.
- **Choosing a weight that bends the elbow.** The arm shortens, the range shrinks and the shoulder loses the arc.
- **Changing grip mid-set.** The reference is lost and sets stop being comparable.
