> **Notice:** This guide is general information and is not a treatment plan. If the pain is very intense, lasts more than a week or does not improve with rest, see a physiotherapist or a sports doctor.

Soreness shows up after almost any new routine, especially at the start. It does not stop you from training again, although it can be uncomfortable and even painful.

## What delayed onset muscle soreness is

What we all call soreness is technically delayed onset muscle soreness, or DOMS. The symptom is a localised pain, similar to small needles, that reduces muscle flexibility for one to five days. How long it lasts depends on the activity and your fitness level.

It appears because the muscle is not adapted to the exercise. The muscle is not used to working at that angle or that intensity, and the muscle and connective tissue suffer structural alterations. The phenomenon happens mainly with eccentric contractions, when the muscle lengthens under tension, and it affects beginners as much as experienced athletes who change their training.

Repeat the stimulus and the body adapts and becomes more efficient. After a short while those small tissue tears stop happening and waste products clear quickly.

## Why it happens: the theory left behind

For years it was explained with one idea that no longer holds.

The first blamed lactic acid, supposedly crystallised inside the muscle. Muscle biopsies have never found those crystals, and lactate crystallises below minus 5 degrees, which is impossible inside the body. The crystal theory is a locker-room legend passed from athlete to athlete.

The accepted explanation is simpler. New work, especially eccentric, produces microdamage in the sarcomeres, the units that make up the muscle fibre, plus a local inflammatory response. The pain comes from that inflammation more than from the microdamage itself.

The most affected areas are usually the muscle junctions and the tendons near the joints. The musculotendinous area concentrates weak fibres, receives more tension and holds more pain receptors.

The peak arrives between 24 and 72 hours after the session, and the ache fades within one to five days. Strength also drops in those days, mainly because of the inflammatory process.

## Can soreness be avoided?

It is almost impossible to avoid it entirely. The only thing that reduces how hard it hits is training progressively.

If you are not fit, do not make the first sessions very intense. Sudden jumps in intensity produce soreness in a beginner and in a trained lifter coming back from a break.

When a lifter drops their training level, many fibres atrophy. On returning, the weakest break and the body selects the best. That is why [progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) is the best tool against soreness: raise the bar slowly and let the tissue adapt.

## Pain does not measure the result

A widespread idea says that if there is soreness, the session worked. The logic sounds reasonable, because pain coincides with new adaptations. The link falls apart as soon as you look closely.

Soreness signals novelty, not effectiveness. You can grow without pain and you can leave a session wrecked without gaining anything. The guide on [muscle soreness and growth](/en/muscle-soreness-and-growth/) explains it: growth depends on mechanical tension, progression and calories, and pain is not part of that equation.

Pain does not measure damage either. Receptors can become less sensitive with repetition, so you stop noticing the ache while the damage is still there. And more pain does not mean more protein synthesis: when you are very sore, the body can use muscle as fuel.

## What actually helps

With very intense pain, an analgesic can be necessary to sleep and move. Anti-inflammatories have shown mild benefits in some studies, and aspirin has shown no effect in this context.

Physical therapies such as massage, specific exercise, cold, ultrasound or electrical stimulation seem to help, although it depends on pain intensity and timing. Cold relieves the ache temporarily and is the simplest option at home.

Water with bicarbonate or sugar does not prevent soreness or remove it. It is a home remedy inherited from the lactic acid theory. Overdoing sugar does not help either: it can cause digestive upset and changes nothing about recovery.

[Muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) depends above all on sleep, food and a sensible plan. Miracle remedies take up very little space on that list.

## How to train again with soreness

Training with soreness is worth doing: it is the only way to reduce it. Avoid the day of maximum pain. As soon as it starts to fade, go back without heavy loads. An easy session beats doing nothing.

Physical activity in a sore muscle increases blood flow, delivers nutrients and clears waste products. That is why moving brings relief sooner than staying on the sofa.

If you are a beginner, remember that the peak arrives between 48 and 72 hours. Wait to see the effect of the load before adding more. If at 48 hours you have little soreness, that is a good sign and you can increase gradually.

Staying short in the first days beats thinking you are ahead of schedule and paying for it on day three of the programme.

## Strategies that help

These points support recovery and reduce how intense the ache feels:

- An adequate [warm-up](/en/gym-warm-up/).
- A gradual increase in training level, from easy exercises to the hardest ones.
- Gentle [stretching](/en/stretching/) before and after the session.
- Loosening the muscles during training to help circulation.
- A cold shower afterwards, if you find it pleasant.

If soreness never goes away, performance drops for weeks and sleep breaks up, the problem may be something else. The guide on [overtraining](/en/overtraining/) explains how to tell normal fatigue from a state that is asking you to stop.

Soreness is neither a trophy nor an enemy. It is the sign that the body met something new, and it fades as you adapt.
