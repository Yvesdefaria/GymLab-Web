Coffee is the most common way to take caffeine and also the hardest to measure. A cup can carry 60 milligrams or 150 depending on the method, the bean and the size. Powder or capsules let you set the dose, and the effect on performance is the same.

## What caffeine does in the body

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. That molecule builds up during the day and produces the feeling of sleep; with the receptor occupied, the brain takes longer to feel tired and the session feels easier. The result shows in strength, power and endurance, with coffee or with isolated caffeine.

The effect on fat is small. Caffeine nudges lipolysis and resting energy expenditure, but the difference does not fix a messy diet and does not show in a single session. It does not replace rest either: it covers fatigue for a few hours and hands the bill back later.

## The dose that works

Studies that measure performance use between 3 and 6 milligrams per kilogram of body weight. Below 3 the effect fades; above 6 there is no extra performance, only more tremor, a faster pulse and more stomach upset.

Health agencies put the daily ceiling for a healthy adult at around 400 milligrams. At higher body weights, the top of the range goes past that figure, and the sensible move is to stay at 3 or 4 milligrams per kilo. For a 70 kilogram person, 210 milligrams is two filtered coffees.

The dose belongs to one session, not to the whole day. If you train in the morning and drink coffee after lunch, the total counts toward the ceiling and toward sleep.

## The 30 to 60 minute window

Peak blood concentration arrives around 30 to 60 minutes, and performance follows the same curve. The useful dose is half an hour or an hour before the start.

There is a popular idea that fat mobilisation peaks at three hours. That is a lab number about free fatty acids, not a training rule. At three hours caffeine is still active, but the performance peak is gone.

## Sleep sets the limit

Caffeine has a half-life of 4 to 6 hours: every four or six hours, half the dose is still in the blood. A coffee at six in the evening leaves a notable amount at midnight and reduces deep sleep even when the person falls asleep without trouble.

The practical rule is nothing with caffeine in the 8 to 10 hours before bed. Someone who trains at seven in the evening can use a small dose at six; someone who finishes at nine and goes to bed at eleven picks decaf. The guide to [how much sleep you need](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) explains why that hour weighs more than any pre-workout.

## Tolerance and coffee

The body adapts. With daily use, adenosine receptors multiply and the same dose delivers less; three or four days without caffeine bring back much of the effect.

Coffee performs slightly worse than pure caffeine at equivalent doses in some comparisons, because its other molecules modulate the effect. The difference is small, and coffee remains a cheap way to reach the dose.

## The numbers that scare people

The oral lethal dose is estimated at 192 milligrams per kilo. At 70 kilos that is 13.4 grams of pure caffeine, the equivalent of about 90 cups of 150 milligrams at once. Nobody drinks that by accident with coffee.

Drinking 100 cups spread over 24 hours does not reach it either. At one cup every 15 minutes, blood levels settle around 2.4 grams, far below the lethal level, because the liver clears caffeine as fast as it arrives. The real risk of an excess is different: palpitations, anxiety, insomnia and stomach upset.

## Doping and labels

Caffeine sat on the prohibited list until the World Anti-Doping Agency removed it in 2004. Since then it can be used in competition, and the agency still watches it through its monitoring programme.

The label does not always match the content. A filtered coffee carries 80 to 100 milligrams per 200 millilitres, an espresso 60 to 80, and a pre-workout can carry 200 or more in one scoop. Add coffee, tea, soft drinks and the supplement, and it is easy to pass the ceiling without noticing.

## Who should lower the dose

Pregnancy and breastfeeding have their own limit, around 200 milligrams a day. People with arrhythmias, poorly controlled hypertension, anxiety or stomach problems should ask before using high doses, and some medicines change caffeine metabolism and lengthen its half-life.
