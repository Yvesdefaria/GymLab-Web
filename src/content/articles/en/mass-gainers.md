## What they are and which problem they solve

A mass gainer is a powder with 60 to 80% carbohydrate, 20 to 30% protein and between 1,000 and 1,500 calories per serving. It mixes with water or milk and sells as a shortcut for anyone who cannot gain weight.

The name promises muscle and the contents are sugar with cheap protein. None of that is a problem on its own. The problem is the price and the belief that the powder does something food cannot.

## Kreider: same muscle, more fat

The clearest trial ran for 28 days. One group took 290 g of carbohydrate and 60 g of protein a day, about 1,500 extra calories; the other took 190 g of maltodextrin, about 760 calories. Both trained.

In the first week the gainer group added almost 700 g of mass and the other stayed flat. By two weeks the numbers matched, and at 28 days muscle mass was similar in both groups, around 700 g.

The difference sat in fat. The gainer group accumulated about 700 g of adipose tissue and the maltodextrin group stayed stable. The extra calories, packed into a product sold as muscle, ended up in the wrong place.

## Rozenek: the study usually read backwards

In 2002, a group of men followed an eight-week training programme. Two groups raised their intake by 70%, one with protein and carbohydrate and one with carbohydrate alone. A third changed nothing.

The control group gained 1.4 kg of lean mass, more than double the two supplemented groups. That result is sometimes cited as proof the gainer works, and it says the opposite: the group that ate more gained less lean mass on the same training.

What the study suggests is that the starting point rules. In people already eating near their requirement, stacking extra calories does not guarantee more muscle.

## 2,500 calories is not a rule

The figure of 2,500 calories gets repeated as if it were a universal threshold. It is the maintenance of one man in one study. A 60 kg person training three days does not need the same as a 95 kg person training five.

Harris measured what overfeeding does: daily expenditure rose 18 calories in the first week and 88 in the second, then fell back. The body does not burn the surplus by magic; it stores it.

## Extra fat slows progress too

Kelsey found that a high body fat percentage is associated with worse strength and hypertrophy progress. It is not a verdict, but it dismantles the idea that gaining fat during a bulk is neutral: the extra tissue does not help and sometimes gets in the way.

## The homemade shake

The same serving gets built in a kitchen for a fraction of the price: 80 g of oats, 400 ml of whole milk and 30 g of protein powder give about 680 calories and around 47 g of protein. With a banana and a spoon of peanut butter it reaches about 900.

## Who genuinely needs one

A mass gainer makes sense for very thin people who cannot eat enough because of satiety, schedule or appetite. There a liquid calorie source solves a real problem.

For everyone else the order is different: raise the calorie density of what you already eat, add oil, nuts, whole milk or bread, and review the training.
