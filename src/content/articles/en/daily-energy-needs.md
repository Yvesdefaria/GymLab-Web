Almost everyone reaches this question for a practical reason: they want to know how much to eat to maintain, gain or lose weight. The answer has three pieces and one warning. The pieces are resting expenditure, digestion and movement. The warning is that any figure is an estimate and that reality shows up on the scale over several weeks.

## The three components of energy expenditure

Daily expenditure splits into three blocks. The first is the energy the body uses to stay alive, and it is the largest. The second is the cost of digesting and absorbing food. The third is everything you move, from the weights session to the walk to the car.

Those percentages are orders of magnitude and vary from person to person. An office job with little movement leaves activity at the low end; a day of moving furniture sends it through the roof.

## Resting metabolic rate

It is the energy you would spend lying down all day doing nothing. It covers breathing, the heart, body temperature and tissue maintenance. Lean mass drives most of it, which is why a large body spends more than a small one under the same conditions.

Muscle mass, youth and growth push it up. Lean tissue loss, a very low intake over a long period and hypothyroidism pull it down. Between similar people there are differences of up to 20 % from genetics, and that figure is not an excuse: it changes the starting point, not the destination.

## The thermic effect of food

Digestion, absorption and storage also cost energy. That cost sits between 7 and 10 % of the calories you eat, and protein is the nutrient that spends the most to process.

The practical consequence is small but real: a diet with more protein burns slightly more in digestion and delivers more satiety. It does not turn one diet into another, and it certainly does not offset a calorie surplus.

## Physical activity is not just training

Here is the part that breaks the maths. Movement expenditure includes the weights session, the cardio and everything else: walking, stairs, cleaning, cooking, gesturing. That whole set is called NEAT, non-exercise activity thermogenesis.

Two people with the same training can differ by 300 or 400 calories a day on NEAT alone. One moves around the office, parks far away and walks after dinner. The other sits for eight hours and drives home. The meal plan is identical and the expenditure is not.

NEAT also shifts inside the same person. Under an aggressive deficit the body cuts spontaneous movement: fewer steps, fewer gestures, more sitting. That adaptation explains why a diet that used to work stops working without the food changing.

## Why your calculator misses

Equations such as Mifflin-St Jeor estimate expenditure from weight, height, age and sex. They are a good starting point and nothing more, because they know nothing about your NEAT or your history.

The first error is adaptation. When you eat less, the body spends less: resting metabolism drops, NEAT falls and digestion costs a little less. The calculator keeps returning the old figure while real expenditure has already moved.

The second error is logging. Portions are eyeballed, pan oil is ignored, the milky coffee is forgotten and Friday dinner is remembered low. The error accumulates without anyone noticing.

The third is NEAT, which appears in no formula and can move hundreds of calories. A change of job, home or daily routine alters expenditure without touching training.

## Using the estimate without obsessing

Treat the calculated figure as a hypothesis and test it with your own data. Weigh yourself under the same conditions three or four times a week and track the weekly average. If the average has not moved in two or three weeks, adjust intake by 200 to 300 calories, no more.

## Mistakes worth avoiding

Metabolism moves with lean mass and activity, and those two respond to training and daily movement, not to an infusion.

Eating every two hours to keep expenditure high does not help either. The thermic effect depends on the total amount of food, not the frequency, and grazing all day complicates portion control.

Another mistake is trusting the gym scale for everything. That scale measures weight, not composition, and a two-kilo jump can be glycogen, water or salt. The trend over several weeks says far more than a single reading.

## What to do with this

Pick a starting figure, eat with a repeatable structure and review every two weeks. If weight climbs faster than planned, trim a little. If it drops too fast and training suffers, add.
