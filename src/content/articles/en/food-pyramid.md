The United States Department of Agriculture published the pyramid in 1992 as a guide for the whole population. The idea was simple: the lower the group, the more servings a day. Criticism arrived early and concerned what the drawing left unsaid rather than the drawing itself.

## What the 1992 pyramid proposed

Six groups, with a serving range for each. Cereals formed the base, and the tip grouped fats and sweets under the phrase "use sparingly".

Servings were defined in household measures: one slice of bread, half a cup of cooked vegetables, one glass of milk. The system was easy to explain and hard to apply, because it forced people to count portions instead of composing a plate.

## The flaw that still stands

The main criticism concerned the absence of guidance inside each group, rather than the number of servings. Eleven servings of bread can be eleven slices of industrial white bread, and the pyramid accepted them. Two servings of meat could be two fatty steaks or two servings of legumes, and the drawing did not tell them apart.

The second complaint was scale. The 6 to 11 cereal servings were designed for an active adult and applied to everyone, with no adjustment for weight, age or physical activity. A sedentary person and someone training three days a week do not need the same, and the guide offered no clue for adapting it. The third was the absence of exercise from the model, which arrived years later with the next version.

## What the plate changed

MyPyramid arrived in 2005, with vertical bands and a staircase for physical activity. In 2011 came the version still in use: a plate divided into four parts, half for vegetables and fruit, a quarter for grains and another for protein, with a dairy product on the side.

The plate changes the unit. Servings per day give way to proportions per meal, and that can be applied by looking at the plate. It also puts vegetables on half the space, a position the pyramid never gave them.

## What the plate does not solve either

The plate keeps the category problem. "Grains" still includes sugary flakes and oats; "protein" includes processed meat and lentils. The graphic does not distinguish quality inside the group, and that was the central objection to the pyramid.

Nor does it say how much protein a person who trains needs or how the portion changes with body weight. Practical translation needs numbers the drawing does not give: 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight a day for strength training, and 25 to 30 grams of fibre. The guide to [dietary protein](/en/protein-guide/) develops the first figure and the one on [fats](/en/dietary-fats/) explains how to choose the fat source.

## How it translates to the daily plate

Half the plate, vegetables and fruit, with a range of colours. A quarter, a protein source: fish, eggs, legumes, lean meat or dairy. The other quarter, a wholegrain or a potato. Olive oil and nuts supply the fat, and fish appears two or three times a week, as the [fish guide](/en/fish-consumption-guide/) sets out.

The proportion adjusts with hunger and with the goal. Someone aiming to gain weight adds one more serving of grains and protein; someone aiming to lose trims the grains and keeps the protein and vegetables. The plate forbids nothing, and that is both its strength and its limit.

## The label matters more than the drawing

A package can carry a drawing that resembles a pyramid and contain a cereal with 30 grams of sugar per 100. The graphic on the box is advertising; the nutrition table and the ingredient list are the data. The guide to [light products](/en/light-products-guide/) teaches how to read the first and the one on [diet myths](/en/healthy-diet-myths/) reviews the phrases that survive without a source.

The pyramid served a public education purpose and became obsolete when the evidence asked for more detail. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) close the loop: no drawing, and no bottle, replaces the composition of what you eat.
