The calf reads as a single block. Inside it are two muscles with different origins, one shared insertion and one joint of difference: the knee. That detail explains almost everything that later gets decided in the gym.

## Two muscles at the back of the lower leg

Both produce plantar flexion, the push of the ground with the ball of the foot that you use to walk, run and jump. They also share the final insertion: the Achilles tendon, which reaches the calcaneus and carries the force of both into the heel.

That economy is misleading. The tendon is one, and the muscle bellies feeding it work under different conditions depending on the position of the knee.

## The gastrocnemius

It is the superficial, visible muscle, the one that draws two heads behind the knee. The medial and lateral heads arise on the posterior surface of the medial and lateral femoral condyles, above the knee joint. They travel down, join and end in the Achilles tendon.

Because it crosses the knee as well as the ankle, it also assists knee flexion. It is a two-joint muscle, so its length depends on what the knee and the ankle do at the same time.

## The soleus

It sits under the gastrocnemius and is the larger of the two by mass. It arises on the posterior aspect of the head and upper third of the fibula, and on the soleal line of the tibia. Like its partner, it ends in the Achilles tendon.

It does not cross the knee: it only passes the ankle. Its job is plantar flexion, and its length does not change when you bend the knee. That independence is what lets it keep working with the leg bent.

## Why one crosses the knee and the other does not

A muscle that crosses two joints shortens at both. Bend the knee and the gastrocnemius loses length from above and reaches the ankle with less room to contract. The soleus has no such problem: its length depends only on the ankle angle.

That anatomy produces the rule that organises calf training. With a straight knee the gastrocnemius is in a position to work; with a bent knee it hands the lead to the soleus. The page on [standing versus seated calf raises](/en/standing-vs-seated-calf-raises/) turns that rule into session decisions.

## Fibres: the soleus is slower

The soleus has a higher proportion of slow-twitch fibres. It makes sense: it holds the body up on every step and every minute of standing, so it works for hours at low intensity. The gastrocnemius, faster, shows up in explosive actions, sprinting and jumping.

That difference describes each muscle's job and rules neither out. Both answer to load; what changes is how much they tolerate and how often they recover.

## Where each one shows

The gastrocnemius is what you see when you stand relaxed, drawing the outer curve of the calf. The soleus shows in profile: when the calf thickens below the knee and the leg gains continuity down to the ankle.

A calf that only looks big from the front usually has gastrocnemius behind it, and one that gains lateral density has soleus underneath. The mass of the soleus pushes the skin outward even while the muscle stays hidden.

## The Achilles tendon and the ankle

The tendon takes the force from both muscles and passes it to the calcaneus. The longer the ankle range, the more the tendon stretches and the more the calf works in its low position. On the leg press, foot angle and platform height change that range, and the guide to [leg press foot position](/en/leg-press-foot-position/) covers the adjustment.

The tendon also sets a practical limit: it is one of the structures that accumulate the most load in jumping, and its tolerance grows more slowly than the muscle's.

## From anatomy to the session

Anatomy does not prescribe sets. It describes which muscle works in each position and why. With that base, the choice of exercise and volume is settled in the session.
