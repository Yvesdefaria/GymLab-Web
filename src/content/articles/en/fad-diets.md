Weight-loss diets are endless: high protein, low fat, low carb, a shake here, a bar there, fat burners, no food at night, ten small meals a day, one meal a day. The topic stays confusing because almost everyone knows someone for whom one of those methods worked.

## Why it looks like it worked for someone

Working for one person does not make it a good idea. It may have worked because that person ate less without noticing, because they lasted three weeks and the result was mostly water, or because the method suited them and they stuck with it longer than another.

## The calorie balance sets the direction

Any method that produces a negative calorie balance will cause weight loss. The balance sets the direction; the speed is decided by water, glycogen and, above all, how long you stay on the plan. Two very different diets can give the same result on the scale and a very different result in the mirror and in training.

## What comes off in the first week

When you start a very restrictive diet, the scale often drops two or three kilos in a few days. Almost all of it is water: cutting carbohydrates empties glycogen stores, and every gram of glycogen carries several grams of water with it. The result shows up fast and says nothing about fat.

When you eat normally again, the water returns and the scale goes up. Many people read that as failure when it is the body taking back what it had lent.

The nutrition information that comes with these diets fails too. Before you believe a number, check it against a serious source or a sports nutrition professional.

## The supplements that come with them

Many of these diets arrive with supplements. Weight-loss products often include stimulants, and that kind of product is not advised for people with high blood pressure or heart problems. The problem gets worse because the label does not always tell the whole story: read the ingredients and distrust what you do not recognise.

A supplement does not fix a badly built diet. If the plan works, it works through the food; if it does not work, the bottle will not save it.

The ingredient list is the only reliable information you have before buying. If a bottle mixes ten plants and does not declare how much of each, there is no way to know what you are taking or in what amount.

## How to assess it in ten minutes

Look at the foods and the nutrients, not the promises. Check whether there is enough protein, vegetables and fibre; whether the plan fits your schedule and your budget; and whether you can see yourself following it for six months.

The reading order helps: first the list of allowed foods, then the amounts, and last the testimonial. Almost always the testimonial is the only thing holding up the promise, and one person who lost weight does not prove the method is good for you.

If the plan does not say how much protein it carries, it probably has not calculated it.

## What to do instead

A plan that works usually has three things: an energy intake you can sustain, enough protein to preserve muscle and foods you like enough to repeat. With that, the diet stops being an event and becomes a way of eating.

Losing weight depends on a [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/) held over time, not on one food. [Healthy diet myths](/en/healthy-diet-myths/) cover the other half of the problem: the beliefs that make a sensible plan look boring.

If the diet promises to clean your body, the guide to [detox](/en/do-you-need-a-detox/) explains what the liver does without help. And if the claim is draining water, [natural diuretics](/en/natural-diuretics/) make clear how much of that is water and how much is marketing. For daily use, [portion sizes](/en/portion-sizes/) and the move from [shakes to real food](/en/shakes-vs-whole-foods/) give two concrete tools that do hold.

A diet that lasts two weeks and disappears leaves the problem where it was, with frustration added. The one you can keep for six months, even if it moves slowly, almost always wins.
