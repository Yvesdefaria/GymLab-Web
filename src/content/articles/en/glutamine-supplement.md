Glutamine has two stories that share the same tub. One is clinical: in burns, intensive care and major surgery, reserves fall and supplementation is part of treatment. The other is commercial: it is sold as a recovery aid, an immune shield and an anabolic accelerator. The first is documented. The second is not.

## What it is and where it comes from

It is a non-essential amino acid, which means the body builds it from others. Muscle produces around 70% of total synthesis and releases it into the blood for the gut, liver and immune cells to use. It is, in fact, the most abundant free amino acid in muscle.

It also arrives with food: meat, fish, dairy, legumes and nuts carry notable amounts. A diet with 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo covers glutamine along with every other amino acid.

## When it really is essential

The right adjective is conditional. In a healthy person, synthesis is enough. In a catabolic state, it is not: extensive burns, sepsis, major surgery, severe malnutrition or a transplant. There glutamine is given enterally or intravenously, under medical supervision, as part of nutritional support.

That is the context behind the studies the industry cites. Moving it to an adult who trains three or four times a week is a jump the data do not support.

## Why the results do not transfer

The first problem is the route. In clinical trials glutamine goes into a vein; in a supplement it goes through the mouth. An oral dose of 5.8 grams raises plasma glutamine by around 20% and does not change its concentration in muscle. The gut and the liver take most of it before it reaches the rest of the body.

The second problem is the measurement. The famous 11% drop in protein synthesis came from lowering glutamine artificially, and it describes a laboratory state, not what happens when you take a powder.

The third is the growth hormone pulse. Two grams of glutamine in sedentary people multiplied GH secretion. The hormone did rise. An isolated pulse does not build muscle, and the study measured neither strength nor mass.

## The trials that answer the question

Six weeks of supplementation in young adults who lifted weights changed neither strength nor body composition. A trial with wrestlers on a twelve-day hypocaloric diet found no benefit at 25 grams a day: no extra fat loss and no extra muscle. In maximal effort tests, acute glutamine did not improve performance.

There is one methodological exception: a conference abstract, with no full paper, reported better recovery with glutamine plus carbohydrate than with carbohydrate alone. An abstract is not enough to change the recommendation.

## Glutamine, overtraining and immunity

After a long, hard session, plasma glutamine falls. That drop is a marker of load, not the cause of the problem. Supplementation brings the level back up, but the trials on respiratory infections in athletes are small and contradictory.

What does move the needle is sleep, enough food and a programmed workload. The guides to [overtraining](/en/overtraining/) and [muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) explain where the limit sits.

## The practical verdict

For a healthy adult who already covers protein, glutamine adds nothing that food does not give. Money works harder on protein, carbohydrate and a good night's sleep. No dose has been shown to improve strength or lean mass in trained people, and that is the reason not to buy it.

With an illness, surgery or a hospital stay, the decision belongs to the medical team handling the case. There glutamine is part of treatment, prescribed and monitored.
