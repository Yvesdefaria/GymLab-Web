Cuttlefish is a cephalopod mollusc, not a fish. It belongs to the same group as octopus and squid, has ten tentacles and an internal shell, and in the kitchen it is treated as shellfish. That distinction is not minor: cuttlefish is lean, quick to cook and very different from a fillet of white fish.

Its value in a training diet is protein and low fat. What it does not have is a special effect on the thyroid or on definition. The iodine it brings is real and necessary, and the amount is modest.

## Composition table per 100 grams

The figures are for cooked, drained cuttlefish and come from USDA FoodData Central. Iodine comes from BEDCA and is in micrograms, as is selenium; zinc is in milligrams.

Raw, the same cuttlefish brings 79 kcal, 16.2 g of protein and 0.7 g of fat. Cooking concentrates the nutrients because it loses water, which is why the cooked table cannot be compared with a raw food without adjusting.

## The table in circulation had the units wrong

The original text printed 64 mg of iodine and 346 mg of zinc per 100 g. Those figures, as written, are dangerous: 64 mg of iodine is more than four hundred times an adult's daily need, and 346 mg of zinc goes far beyond the tolerable limit. The correct table reads 64 µg of iodine, 3.46 mg of zinc and 89.6 µg of selenium. The error came from losing the decimal comma and the unit while copying a table that was already in micrograms.

The correction does not change the advice: cuttlefish is still a lean, protein-rich food. It changes the magnitude, and that magnitude is what separates a meal from poisoning.

## Protein against chicken

The version in circulation compared the 32 g of protein in cuttlefish with the 24 g in chicken breast and concluded that cuttlefish wins. The comparison mixed cooked and raw. A hundred grams of cooked chicken breast carries about 31 g of protein, practically the same as cooked cuttlefish.

The real difference is fat: 1.4 g in cuttlefish against about 3.6 g in chicken, with similar calories. Cuttlefish also brings more iron and more iodine, and more cholesterol. Neither is better in the abstract; they are different options for the same dish.

## Iodine and the thyroid

Iodine is needed to make thyroid hormones, which regulate metabolism. An adult needs about 150 µg a day, and a 150 g serving of cuttlefish brings around 96 µg, close to two thirds of that figure.

That is all. Cuttlefish does not activate the thyroid beyond normal or speed up metabolism. It does not help you get lean on its own either. Excess iodine can also disturb the gland, so supplements without a medical indication make no sense.

## Cholesterol and salt

A hundred grams of cooked cuttlefish carries 224 mg of cholesterol. The old line that it is not recommended for people with high cholesterol is out of date: dietary cholesterol raises LDL modestly and variably, and the context matters more than the isolated food. Anyone with a lipid disorder should discuss it with their doctor.

Sodium is the other figure to watch: 744 mg per 100 g cooked, and griddling with coarse salt adds more. The page on [how to limit sodium](/en/how-to-limit-sodium/) gives the practical rules so the dish does not overshoot.

## How to cook it

Cuttlefish is cleaned by removing the shell, the beak, the eyes and the ink sac, which can be kept to add colour and flavour. The skin can stay or come off; leaving it keeps the piece juicier.

On a griddle, very hot and with little oil, two or three minutes per side. Cooked longer at medium heat, it turns rubbery. Large pieces do better in a long braise, 40 to 60 minutes, with potato, tomato or wine. It also cooks with rice or quinoa and is served with its ink.

## Buying and storing

Fresh, it should have firm flesh, a sea smell and shiny skin. Frozen is a practical alternative and keeps the texture well if thawed in the fridge. If you have a shellfish allergy, avoid cuttlefish and every cephalopod.

Mercury and fish consumption advice have their own guide in [fish consumption](/en/fish-consumption-guide/), and [shellfish and protein](/en/shellfish-nutrition/) extends the comparison with other molluscs.

## Where it fits

Cuttlefish is a light dinner that takes rice, potato or vegetables. The day's carbohydrate can come from [oats](/en/oats/) at breakfast and [bread](/en/bread-and-muscle/) at lunch, without loading dinner. If you want a meal with high protein and low fat, it is one of the most convenient options at the fish counter.
