Very few people know how important and effective pull-ups are, and most of us see them as something complex and hard to do. In many physical tests, a pull-up test is the true measure of strength, because it shows how strong you are relative to your bodyweight.

To consider your bodyweight strength good, the number of pull-ups you should be able to do is 20 or more for men and 8 or more for women.

## How to do pull-ups with correct technique

The most basic pull-up is the supinated version, with the palms facing you. It has the largest range of motion of all variations, both for the lats and for the arms.

The starting position begins with the palms supinated, at shoulder width or slightly closer. The arms are fully extended and the torso is in line with the arms.

To start the ascent, use the relatively strong muscles of the upper back and the elbow flexors while you drive the elbows back. The ascent continues until the chin passes the bar.

Before starting the ascent, inhale. On the descent, begin to exhale and bring the torso back to the starting position. At the bottom, the arms are fully extended and the shoulder blades rise. This is extremely important.

To complete the range of motion, the arms and the scapular abductors must stretch on every rep. Keep the legs in line with the torso as much as possible. Do not flex the hips, because that lowers the quality of the exercise.

Straps should only be used by lifters with very little forearm strength and a risk of losing the grip, or by those whose forearms fatigue before the upper back.

## Advanced cues for pull-ups

Do not use the biceps to move. Think of your hands as hooks and focus on the contraction of the lats.

To work the upper lats more effectively, keep the grip as wide as is comfortable. The wider it is, the more emphasis you put on the upper lat and the teres major. The closer it is, the more the lower lats and the pectoralis major are involved.

Bring your jaw to the bar or slightly above it while the elbows stay out. That works the upper lats and the other muscles involved through the longest range.

Do not drop your head or take the back of your neck behind the bar. That shortens the range a great deal, reduces muscle development and raises injury risk, especially at the shoulders.

Beginner bodybuilders and athletes without much upper-body strength usually skip pull-ups because lifting your own bodyweight is hard. To build the strength you need, you can do cable pulldowns, use an assisted pull-up machine or ask someone to hold your feet.

## Which grip is best for pull-ups

From experience, the people I know with the best back development use a wide variety of grips to recruit as much back muscle as possible. Just look at the upper back of the gymnasts who competed at the last Olympic Games.

That is why I firmly believe the best way to get the most from pull-ups is to vary your grips. Two variations have their own pages: [wide-grip pull-ups](/en/wide-grip-pull-ups/) and [close-grip pull-ups](/en/close-grip-pull-ups/).

## How to do more pull-ups

A pull-up specialization program builds a very wide, dense back, and it also adds centimetres of solid muscle to your arms by promoting growth in the biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis and teres pronator. Just look at the arms of Olympic gymnastics medallists Andreas Wecker and Yuri Chechi, known for their huge volume of pull-ups and bar push-ups rather than their work on the Scott bench.

You will improve the neural connections between nerves and muscles through the Law of Repetition. It is simple: more pull-ups improve pull-up performance without delaying the rest of your training.

You do not need more than one set to failure, with as many reps as you can, as long as you do it every day. Say each set lasts about a minute. If you do this for four weeks, the total is about 28 minutes of pull-up training in a month.

With such a simple strategy you can double the number of pull-ups you are able to do.

There are many types of pull-up. My recommendation is the wide-grip version, to the front or behind the neck, for the back development it provides. If you choose the behind-the-neck variation, bring your head down in front of the bar and keep your neck from going behind it.

## Pull-up progression: from assistance to added weight

Pull-ups require a certain amount of strength, so it makes sense to progress in levels.

**Level 1.** Start hanging from the bar with your knees bent. A partner holds your ankles during the ascent. If you need extra help, you can extend your legs against your partner's support base. When you can do 12 reps with minimal help, move to the next level.

**Level 2.** The position is the same, but the partner holds one ankle. The weight of the free leg adds overload, bearing in mind that you compensate with the other. A good partner holding you at the waist is a better alternative. When you can do 12 reps with minimal help, move up.

**Level 3.** The position does not change, but the partner holds your waist. As you get stronger you will need less help and only in certain parts of the range. At that point you can do the full range unassisted and are ready to add weight.

Added overload comes in three ways: a dumbbell between the legs, power hooks hanging from a weighted belt, or straps hanging from the neck with plates.

## Why pull-ups beat cable pulldowns

Someone always says they are going to swap pull-ups for the lat pulldown. They can, but they will not get the same result.

The difference is the type of chain. A pull-up is a closed-chain exercise: the body moves toward a resistance. The pulldown is open-chain: the resistance moves toward the body. Although they look similar, they demand completely different recruitment patterns.

The point is clear. A great back is built faster with pull-ups than with lat pulldowns.
