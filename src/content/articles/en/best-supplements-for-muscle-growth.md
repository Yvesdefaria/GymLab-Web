## The two that survive scrutiny

Protein powder is food with a label. Creatine monohydrate is a compound with more than three decades of trials behind it. Neither performs magic: protein covers a need the diet sometimes misses, and creatine adds a little strength and some mass once training already works.

Everything else sold for size competes for a space these two already occupy.

## Protein: the sum that decides

The starting figure is 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight per day. An 80 kilo lifter aiming at 1.8 grams per kilo needs 144 grams, split across four meals of 36.

Food covers the sum before powder does. Two eggs, a Greek yoghurt, 150 grams of chicken or a tin of tuna bring between 12 and 35 grams per serving, and the day is built from four of those pieces. Powder enters when the day falls short or when cooking does not fit the schedule.

## Protein: which form to buy

Whey concentrate is the cheapest option per gram of protein and carries some lactose. Isolate costs more and suits anyone who digests lactose badly. Hydrolysate costs more again and has shown no practical advantage over the other two for building muscle.

Plant blends work when they are combined well and the dose makes up for the lower leucine density. The [protein supplements](/en/protein-supplements/) guide sorts the types, and [protein pros and cons](/en/protein-pros-and-cons/) covers the rest of the decision.

## Creatine: the dose and the calendar

Three to five grams of creatine monohydrate a day, every day, with no cycles. Loading is optional: 20 grams a day split into four doses for five days fills the muscle sooner, and then the daily dose takes over. Without loading, the tank takes a few weeks longer to fill and the end result is the same.

The time of day changes nothing. Creatine works by saturation, not by peak, and taking it with or without carbohydrate does not alter the deposit over weeks. The [creatine](/en/creatine-supplement/) page gathers dose, form and safety, and the [evidence](/en/creatine-evidence/) page reviews the studies that get quoted badly.

## Three ideas that cost money

**The post-workout shake matters more than the daily total.** Protein synthesis stays elevated for hours and the daily total weighs more than the split. The shake afterwards is convenient, not compulsory.

**Creatine needs a month off every two.** No trial supports the cycle. Saturation holds with the daily dose, and stopping only returns the deposit to its baseline.

**Creatine needs insulin to enter the muscle.** The transporter works with or without an insulin spike. Adding 50 grams of fast carbohydrate to go with it is an expensive way to complicate something that already works on its own.

## What does not make the list

**Glutamine** does not raise protein synthesis or strength in healthy adults who eat enough protein. The [glutamine](/en/glutamine-supplement/) page explains why the quoted figures do not transfer.

**Tribulus terrestris** is sold as a testosterone booster. The two controlled trials in trained men found no hormonal or strength changes, and the [tribulus](/en/tribulus-terrestris/) page reviews what they measured.

**BCAAs** are three of the nine essential amino acids and already come inside any complete protein. Adding them separately when protein is in range has shown no advantage. The [branched-chain amino acids](/en/bcaa-supplements/) guide details the studies.

## What a week looks like

Four meals with 30 to 40 grams of protein each, one strength session with logged weights and three to five grams of creatine a day. If food falls short on a given day, a shake closes the gap. The scale and the training notebook show in eight weeks whether the plan works.
