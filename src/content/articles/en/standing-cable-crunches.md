The difference from the [floor crunch](/en/floor-crunches/) is the resistance. On the floor the weight of the torso never changes and progression depends on the lever. On the cable the resistance moves with a pin, and the abs can work in the five-to-ten rep range, which is where they grow.

The price is technique. A standing crunch allows hip momentum, arm pulling and half a range, and all three are plain to see once the load goes up.

## What the high pulley adds

The pulley pulls from above and behind, so the resistance never disappears. At the bottom, with the trunk extended, the cable stays tight; at the top, with the abs shortened, the plate still hangs. That continuous tension is the advantage over bodyweight.

It also makes progress measurable. A plate on the chest cannot be logged with the same precision as a five-kilo plate on the stack, and the cable leaves the number in plain sight for the next session.

## Standing or kneeling

Both versions use the same rope and the same path. Standing asks more balance: the weight drags you backwards and the feet and the abs have to brake the body. The [kneeling version](/en/kneeling-cable-crunches/) removes that problem and takes the legs out of the movement.

If you come from the standing version and cannot feel the abs, try kneeling. If the station has padded stops for the glutes, use them: they steady the pelvis and stop you sitting back on every rep.

## Starting position

Attach a rope to the top of the pulley and stand with your back to the plate stack.

Set the feet wider than hip width and keep the knees unlocked. A wide base gives balance when the weight pulls backwards.

Lean the trunk slightly forward before you start, just enough to feel the cable pulling on the rope. Take the rope with a neutral grip and bring it to the sides of the head, elbows open and hands at ear height.

The hands stay there for the whole set. The chin stays off the chest and the neck stays in line with the spine.

## Execution

Flex the trunk at the waist and drive the elbows towards the knees. The back rounds, and that rounding is what lets the abs shorten fully.

Do not arch the back to go lower. Do not push the hips back either: if the hips travel, the fold comes from the joint and the abs lose tension.

Hold the contracted position for a second. Then let the plate take you back to the start with the trunk under control; if the weight straightens you in one go, the eccentric is gone.

Exhale as the ribs close, on the way into flexion, and let the air come back at the end of the range.

## Common mistakes

- Pulling with the arms and using the rope like a lat pulldown.
- Letting the hips travel back to go lower.
- Changing hand position halfway through the set.
- Loading too much and making up for it with half a range.
- Snapping the spine straight on the way down.
- Chasing failure before the technique is there.

## How much weight, and when to add it

The rule from the original source is simple: when fifteen reps come with good form, add weight. Fifteen reps through half a range do not count.

Heavy sets also take tempo variations: holding the contraction for two seconds at the top or lowering over three seconds changes the stimulus without touching the stack. Alternating ranges across the month, one high-rep session and one low-rep session, splits the work between endurance and strength.

## Where it fits

It is the first movement of an ab session when the goal is load, because the abs arrive fresh and the technique holds. In the [hypertrophy routine](/en/abs-hypertrophy-routine/) it appears with heavy sets and few reps; in the [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/) it takes the cable block.

After the cable, a bodyweight movement such as bicycle crunches or side crunches finishes the session without adding load to the spine. The [ab exercises guide](/en/ab-exercises/) explains which pattern each family trains and in what order to place them.
