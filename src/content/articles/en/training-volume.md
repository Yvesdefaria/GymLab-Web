Many coaches and strength specialists describe volume using the number of sets, the reps performed in each set and the amount of weight per set. To keep it simple, volume here means the **total number of working sets** (warm-ups excluded) in **one training session** and, more specifically, for **one particular muscle group**.

That distinction matters because volume is not shared equally between large and small muscles, or between different goals.

## Training volume by muscle group

If you want to gain muscle mass, in other words, to **hypertrophy** your muscles, you need **high-volume** training:

- 9 to 20 sets per working session for the larger muscle groups (chest, back and shoulders).
- 6 to 16 sets for the smaller muscle groups (triceps, biceps, traps and forearms).

These ranges are sometimes wider, but most exercise scientists versed in hypertrophy use these parameters because they rest on both **formal research** and **anecdotal observation**, that is, on people who have applied those methods in the gym and reported their experience.

The reason for training the **large muscle groups** with higher volumes is that they contain more muscle fibres and need a bigger stimulus to develop than smaller muscles.

Smaller groups also work when the larger ones do. On a chest press, the shoulders and triceps assist the movement. Likewise, the biceps and forearms work when you do rows and pull-ups on back day. That is why smaller muscle groups need less volume when you train them specifically.

## How many sets strength needs

Powerlifters whose **only goal** is to gain strength do fewer sets than lifters chasing volume.

The reason is that, as the number of sets in a workout rises, strength and power levels **drop**. When those levels fall, a powerlifting session has little use for future training; it is better to stop the session so the muscles recover and arrive **strong** for the next one. When overall strength is the goal, keep a moderate total number of sets:

- 9 to 16 for the large muscle groups per training session.
- 6 to 12 sets for the small ones.

## Volume and muscle definition

People often separate volume from muscle definition as if training could divide one from the other.

However, when muscles grow through weight training and high volume, **body metabolism** increases because muscle is metabolically active tissue. When metabolism rises, you **burn** more fat and get leaner, so **muscle definition** improves.

To improve definition you also have to train the same way as you would for volume. Almost every competitive bodybuilder who wants to be more developed and defined trains with high volumes.

Unlike bodybuilders, many people want to improve **definition** without a considerable increase in muscle volume. Achieving that depends more on the amount of weight used, the number of reps per set and training frequency than on volume. **Diet** and adding **cardiovascular exercise** are especially critical.

Volume is one part of the plan, not the whole plan. The [frequency at which you train each muscle](/en/training-frequency/) and [progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) decide whether those sets produce an adaptation. If your goal is to [build muscle](/en/build-muscle/), the high range is the one that matches that goal; if you train for strength, stay in the moderate range and prioritise the quality of every set.
