The bar rests on the front delts, not on the neck and not on the hands. The arms make a shelf and hold it; the torso carries the weight. That placement changes how the work is shared and also where the set breaks down.

## The bar on the front delts

The bar crosses the middle of the front delts, in front of the throat, and rests on the shelf formed by the shoulders and the upper chest. The elbows rise until they are high, with the upper arms almost parallel to the floor. If the elbows drop, the bar rolls forward.

There are three ways to hold it. The weightlifting grip puts the hands under the bar, fingers outside and wrists extended. The crossed-arm grip brings the arms across the chest and the hands to the opposite shoulders. Straps hold the bar without asking for wrist extension.

## Stance and descent

The feet go at shoulder width, toes slightly out. The stance is narrower than in the back squat because the bar is already in front and balance is more demanding.

Descend by bending ankle, knee and hip together. The torso stays upright and the bar travels vertically over the midfoot. Depth goes as far as the bar position holds: if the elbows drop or the upper back rounds, the limit is up top, not in the leg.

Stand up by extending all three joints and keeping the elbows high past the hardest point.

## What it actually trains

The quadriceps work harder than in the back squat at the same weight, and they work as a group. The bar in front increases knee-flexion demand and forces a more upright torso; the four heads share one tendon and are recruited together. The idea that the front squat selects the vastus lateralis and builds the outer sweep does not hold.

[Quadriceps anatomy](/en/quadriceps-anatomy/) explains why the four heads do not separate with posture, and [quadriceps training myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) dismantles the idea of isolating one area of the thigh.

## The grip as a limit

Wrist stiffness is a constraint, not a diagnosis. If the wrist cannot reach the extension the grip needs, the crossed-arm grip and straps let you train the front squat without forcing it. What does not work is holding the bar with the hands while the elbows drop: the bar moves forward and the torso loses its vertical line.

The set usually fails in the upper back and the grip before it fails in the quadriceps. That is normal: the front squat also trains the torso's ability to stay upright under load.

## Common errors

The first is resting the bar on the neck. The bar sits on the delts with the throat free; if breathing is hard, it is too high.

The second is letting the elbows drop on the way up. The bar rolls, the torso leans and the rep is lost forward.

The third is stopping at a half squat. The quadriceps lose the range where they work hardest and the set looks easier than it is.

The fourth is lifting the heels. It is almost always the ankle: a few centimetres of heel lift or a slightly wider stance fixes it while mobility improves.

## Sets, reps and rest

Recommendation from this page: three or four sets of five to eight reps, with two or three minutes of rest. The archive gave no numbers. This is a strength and size dose; if the upper back fails before the legs, lower the weight and move up the rep range.

## Where it fits

It can be the first exercise of leg day or the second after the back squat. With less absolute weight than the back squat, it asks more of the torso and the quadriceps, so it suits blocks with a knee emphasis. [Quad routines by goal](/en/quad-routines-by-goal/) shows where to place it.

If the upper back tires before the legs, alternate with the [Smith machine squat](/en/smith-machine-squat/) or the [hack squat](/en/hack-squat/), which take balance out of the equation. The [sumo squat](/en/sumo-barbell-squat/) covers the wide stance and the [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/) is the reference for the pattern. [Squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) holds the general framework.
