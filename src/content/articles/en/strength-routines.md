Strength routines and hypertrophy routines use the same equipment and the same exercises. What changes is the mix of load, reps and rest, and with it the stimulus and the way progress is measured. Strength is measured in kilos; hypertrophy, in centimetres.

## What separates strength routines from hypertrophy

A set of 15 reps on the leg extension machine works the quadriceps, but it is not strength training. It is hypertrophy: moderate load, high reps, short rests and metabolic fatigue. Maximum strength works the other way round. The load goes up, the reps come down and the rest gets longer so that every set looks like the one before.

There is no magic in that difference. Muscle grows with mechanical tension and enough volume, and strength improves when the nervous system learns to recruit more motor units and coordinate the movement. A lifter can add kilos without changing shirt size and a bodybuilder can add inches without moving more weight. They are different adaptations of the same tissue.

## The three rules of strength training

**Intensity.** Work between 80 and 100 percent of your one-rep max and stay in sets of one to five reps. If you can do 8 reps with a weight, that weight belongs to another kind of session. Percentages are calculated from a real or estimated mark, and the guide to [one-rep max and volume routines](/en/one-rep-max-volume-training/) explains how to get it without risking your back.

**Rest.** Three to five minutes between heavy sets. The phosphagen system, which fuels efforts of a few seconds, needs that time to recover. Resting a minute to keep your heart rate high turns the next set into an endurance set.

**Exercise selection.** The squat, the bench press and the deadlift are the three lifts contested in powerlifting. The overhead press completes the fourth basic pattern: pushing above the head. The "Big 3" label comes from competition and does not describe a closed list: it leaves out a vertical push that also moves kilos.

## How to choose by level and by available days

If you have less than a year under the bar, start with a full-body linear plan and two or three sessions a week. Technique comes first and the load rises little by little. If you already have a base, you can move to three or four days with weekly progression and percentages. Block plans and high frequencies come later, once the body knows the lifts.

There is no need to train maximum strength every day. The nervous system needs to recover between heavy sessions for the same pattern, and high-frequency programmes exist because they spread the load, not because the body tolerates more intensity without paying a price. The guide to [training periodisation](/en/training-periodization/) explains how to order those loads across the weeks, and the guide to [central nervous system fatigue](/en/central-nervous-system-fatigue/) describes the signs that call for a deload.

With two days available, a full-body A/B is enough. With three, the same scheme or a push, pull and legs split. With four, an upper and lower split leaves room to recover. The number of days matters less than consistency: three well-planned sessions over twelve weeks beat six improvised sessions over three.

## A three-day linear progression example

This is a classic linear progression example, not a named method. You train Monday, Wednesday and Friday, alternating two sessions.

**Day A**

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | 5 | 5 |
| Bench press | 5 | 5 |
| Barbell row | 5 | 5 |

**Day B**

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | 5 | 5 |
| Overhead press | 5 | 5 |
| Deadlift | 1 | 5 |

The weight should be challenging and leave one rep in reserve. Rest at least 3 minutes between sets and do not add biceps curls or machines at this stage. The skeleton is not the programme. What turns these two sessions into a progression is adding load when the last 5x5 is clean, a principle developed in the guide to [adaptation and progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/).

## The directory: nine strength routines

There are nine routines across eight guides, because the 5x5 page covers two programmes. Start with the one that fits your level and your days, not the one that promises more kilos.

- [5x5 routine: StrongLifts and Madcow](/en/stronglifts-madcow-5x5/): two linear programmes with five exercises. StrongLifts for the first months and Madcow when session-to-session progression runs out.
- [Starting Strength](/en/starting-strength-guide/): the three-sets-of-five plan for novices, with the progression and deloads from Rippetoe and Kilgore's book.
- [Bilbo method](/en/bilbo-method/): one fast set at 50-70 percent plus accessory work. An option to break a plateau, not a shortcut to never loading again.
- [Wendler 5/3/1](/en/wendler-531/): four weeks of percentages over a training max and two templates to choose from.
- [Upper-body strength programme](/en/upper-body-strength-program/): twelve weeks, four sessions and three phases that bring the reps down.
- [Powerlifting training](/en/powerlifting-training/): twelve weeks built around the squat, the bench press and the deadlift.
- [Six-week strength ramp](/en/six-week-strength-ramp/): a short ramp with three phases and a single maximal set at the end.
- [Korte 3x3](/en/korte-3x3/): two four-week phases with high frequency and moderate loads.

## Recovery and technique before you start

Recovery has no trick: sleep seven to nine hours and eat enough protein to support the training. The rest are details you adjust when symptoms appear. If the weight does not move for two weeks, technique degrades or sleep breaks, check the load before adding another set.

Technique on the four basics is learned with light reps and with someone who corrects what you cannot see. The guide to [exercise technique and execution](/en/exercise-technique/) reviews the points that repeat across every lift. With that settled, pick a routine, give it twelve weeks and log every session. Strength arrives when the plan repeats, not when it changes.
