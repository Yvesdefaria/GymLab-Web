The shake does not build muscle on its own. Its value is practical: it packs calories and protein into a glass you drink in five minutes, when a fuller meal would be hard to finish. That is the difference between a useful shake and one that only adds sugar.

## What a weight-gain shake needs

Three parts: a protein source, a carbohydrate source and a liquid that brings calories. Protein can come from milk, yogurt, cottage cheese or a whey supplement. Carbs from oats, banana, fruit or bread. The liquid decides much of the total: whole milk adds more energy than water and brings extra protein.

With those three parts, any reasonable combination lands in range. A shake for gaining weight should sit between 300 and 600 kilocalories, with 30 to 40 grams of protein. Below that, it is not worth the effort; above it, it becomes a full meal you may not need.

Oats add fibre and make the shake more filling, which helps if it replaces a meal and gets in the way if you drink it right before training. Banana sweetens without added sugar and adds potassium. Berries bring flavour and very few calories, so they are the choice for the lighter version.

## How much to add per day

The shake is the tool; the surplus is the goal. To gain weight, eat 200 to 300 kilocalories above maintenance, not a thousand. A large surplus does not speed up muscle gain and does increase the fat that comes with it.

Gaining two kilos of muscle a month is not a realistic goal. Muscle is built more slowly than that, and the rate depends on experience, genetics and training. The guide to [building lean muscle](/en/lean-muscle-gain/) explains how to adjust the surplus without losing sight of the scale or the mirror.

## When it fits best

The first slot is breakfast. Anyone who eats nothing on waking drinks in five minutes what they would not eat in twenty. The second is after training, especially if the next meal is far away. The third is at night, when the day has fallen short on calories and a full dinner no longer fits.

It also works as a partial substitute: half a serving to finish a light meal, or a full serving instead of a meal when time runs out. What it does not fix is a disordered diet. The guide to [bodybuilding meals](/en/bodybuilding-meals/) shows how to build the plates a shake only complements.

## What most recipes do not need

Forum recipes pile on additions. Thirty grams of branched-chain amino acids add nothing new if the day's protein already covers 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo. The amino acids are already in the shake. Five grams of glutamine have not shown an effect on hypertrophy in healthy people who eat enough protein.

The 75 to 100 grams of fast-digesting carbohydrate after training belong to another context, the one of athletes with two sessions a day or endurance events. For anyone lifting once and eating afterwards, that amount only adds a burst of calories.

And the shake does not license eating anything else for the rest of the day. A large fast-food menu does add weight, mostly as fat. The guide to [mass gainers](/en/mass-gainers/) compares those products with real food, and the one on [shakes before and after training](/en/pre-and-post-workout-shakes/) covers timing around the session.

## Adjusting the recipe to the goal

For fat loss, the same structure works with fewer calories: skimmed milk, less oats and whole fruit instead of nuts. For gaining, add 20 grams of oats and swap water for milk. If your stomach complains, split the shake into two glasses and move it away from training.

Check the rest of the diet before adding a second shake. Once protein is covered and calories rise, the next step is usually more solid food, not more powder. The guide to [nutrition to get in shape](/en/nutrition-to-get-in-shape/) orders those priorities, and the one on [what to eat to build muscle](/en/what-to-eat-to-build-muscle/) lists the foods that do the heavy lifting.
