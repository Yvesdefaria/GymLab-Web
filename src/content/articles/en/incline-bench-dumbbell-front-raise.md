## The bench moves the range

Standing, a front raise takes the hand from the thigh to shoulder height. The arm travels about 90 degrees and the anterior deltoid works from its longest length to its shortest. On an incline bench the start is different: the chest is supported, the torso sits at 30 or 45 degrees, and the arm hangs away from the body line. That detail spreads the range differently; the muscle at work is the same.

At 45 degrees, the hand that rises to shoulder height no longer traces the same arc as standing. If the endpoint is measured against the pad, with the arm perpendicular to the torso, the range is half. If it is measured against the floor, with the hands level with the shoulder, the arm ends past that perpendicular. Both readings fit the same cue and produce different exercises. That ambiguity is the problem. The incline only makes it visible.

The incline works as a range dial. Pick an endpoint, measure where the hand lands and repeat it on every set. The phrase "greater isolation" sells a shorter arc as if a shorter arc were an advantage.

## The trapezius keeps working

The archive says the bench reduces help from the trapezius and the chest. The chest part is true in a limited sense: with the torso supported there is no swing to drive the dumbbell. The trapezius is another story.

As the arm rises, the scapula rotates upward. Past about 90 degrees of flexion that rotation is what makes room for the humerus under the acromion, and it happens with the chest on the pad too. The supported position removes leg drive and the spinal arch. It does not erase scapular rotation or turn the raise into a single-muscle drill.

What the bench buys is precision. The torso does not move, the angle repeats and failure arrives through the shoulder before the back. That is strict work, and it is reason enough to programme it.

## The support is comfort

With the chest on the pad, the lower back does not carry the weight of the torso or brake the swing. That is a comfort and control advantage, and it can be said that way.

That is a step away from a treatment. A supported raise does not fix a back, and the line "beneficial for people with lower-back problems" promises something the exercise cannot deliver. If the back hurts, that is a clinical decision and choosing a bench does not settle it.

## The execution

Set the bench between 30 and 45 degrees. The more upright it is, the shorter the range left ahead; the flatter it is, the closer it gets to a press. Pick an angle, write it down and do not change it mid-block.

Rest the chest on the pad and the feet on the floor. Hold a dumbbell in each hand with a neutral grip, palms facing each other, or a pronated grip, palms facing the floor. The neutral grip leaves the shoulder in a comfortable rotation; the pronated one looks more like the standing version.

The elbows stay soft and stay that way. Raise the dumbbells to shoulder height without lifting the chest off the pad and pause for a moment. Lower with control until the arms hang. If the last rep needs the chest off the pad to get there, the weight is too heavy.

## Dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 45 or 60 seconds of rest. The load goes up when the set ends with the chest on the pad and the same endpoint.

This is an isolation lift and it does not need to reach failure. The guide to [full range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) explains how to fix the endpoint without moving it between sets.

## Where it fits

It goes after the presses and before or after rear-delt work. The incline does not replace the standing version: it complements it with a different arc and a fixed torso.
