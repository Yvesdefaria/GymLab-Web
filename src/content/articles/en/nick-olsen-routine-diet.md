Nick Olsen is a fitness model and competitor. His week combines presses, rows, cables and a finisher of short sets. His off-season diet is a sample of how he spreads food across the day, not a closed plan. The two things should be read apart: the training can be adapted; the menu cannot, because it lacks the number that would allow adjusting it.

## A six-day week

The split leaves two rest days and six sessions. The order puts chest and calves first, back with biceps second and legs third. After a day off come shoulders and abs, and finally arms and abs.

The frequency is high: five sessions in a row before the first rest day. That works when sleep and food keep up. With less recovery, move the rest day to day three or four before cutting sets in the middle of a session.

## The one-minute rest and the FST-7 finisher

The article repeats the same rest on every day: about one minute between sets and between exercises. That is short, comfortable on machines and isolation work, and demanding on free-weight presses or squats, where two or three minutes allow more weight with better technique.

The FST-7 finisher appears twice: cable crossovers for chest and rope pushdowns for triceps. That is fifteen reps for seven sets with thirty seconds of rest. It is a finisher, not the body of the session: it comes after three or four exercises and shows up as a pump and as fatigue, not as strength.

Abs also go high-rep: one hundred crunches, fifty leg raises and fifty side crunches in some sessions. That is endurance work, not hypertrophy; it completes the day and does not replace load.

The order makes sense: protein at every meal, carbohydrate around training and vegetables in the big meals. Olive oil and nuts cover fat without adding food volume.

## Why this menu is not copied as it stands

The number that would scale it is missing: body weight and total calories. Without them, the portions belong to someone else. Copying them as they stand can leave a 90-kilo athlete short or a 65-kilo one overfed.

What can be copied is the structure: six meals, protein in all of them, vegetables in the main meals and a fat source in each. From there, the total is calculated from each person's weight and expenditure, as the guide to [calories for muscle gain](/en/calories-for-muscle-gain/) explains.

## What the calling card does not say

The article tells his story: he started training at 20, played football from the age of three, and blames the bad habits of his teens for a gain of almost 25 kilos. Those are details he recounts, not a verified record.

His stated goal was the IFBB Men's Physique pro card, and according to the text he earned it in Europe after five years in the United States. The record does not change the diet; it explains why he trains with that frequency and why the food changes every week.

## How to take this sample into your week

If you train five or six days and sleep enough, Olsen's split gives an idea of how to order volume: one large group per session, one accessory and one finisher. If you train three or four days, the guide to [volume routines](/en/volume-routines/) has shorter templates and the [Jeff Seid routine](/en/jeff-seid-routine-and-diet/) shows another six-day split.

For food, start with the total and the protein. The page on [diets to build muscle](/en/diets-to-build-muscle/) turns percentages into grams, and the [ectomorph guide](/en/ectomorph-muscle-gain/) explains how to raise calories when weight stalls. Olsen's menu is a reference for order, not a recipe to copy without numbers.
