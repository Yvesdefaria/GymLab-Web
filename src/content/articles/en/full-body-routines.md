The idea is simple and has not changed in seventy years: few exercises, heavy weight and a session that covers the whole body. What changes between plans is the number of days, the load and the level of the person doing them.

## What a full-body routine is

A full-body session has a squat or a deadlift, a push, a pull and one or two accessories. Four or five exercises cover the whole body. An example: squat, bench press, barbell row, biceps curl and abs. Three sets each and a session of about forty-five minutes. Anything on top of that is usually volume another day would do better.

The difference from a split is not effort but distribution. A split gives each group a lot of volume once a week. Here each group gets less per session and more often, and the body handles that frequency better when the load is not maximal.

## The three rules of the block

**Three sets per exercise.** More sets make the session longer and spread effort worse. Three effective sets per exercise are enough when the whole body is trained two or three times a week.

**Two or three days.** Three for anyone starting or coming back after a break. Two for anyone already training with real intensity who needs to recover between sessions.

**Never two days in a row.** The session hits every muscle group. At least one day sits between sessions, and if the goal is strength or size, that rest also covers other sports.

Those three rules are the filter for the whole block. If a plan breaks one of them, it belongs to another section.

## How to choose

If you are coming back after a break, start with the three-day plan to get back in shape. With a base and three days available, the mass plan. With four days and an appetite for heavy weight, the heavy days.

Functional overreaching is a four-week block you run once or twice a year, not a permanent plan. After that block, go back to a sustainable plan for at least two months. With two days available the block still works: pick a two-session plan and leave a day between them. Frequency matters less than consistency across twelve weeks. A plan only works if you can repeat it next month, so pick by the days you actually have.

## How the block progresses

Every plan shares one rule: add weight when the last set comes up clean, with every rep and technique intact. Big lifts go up 5 kilos; small ones, 2.5. If you miss two sessions in a row on the same exercise, drop 10 % and build again.

The deload appears every four to six weeks depending on the plan. It is not a lost week: it is what lets the next jump in weight happen. Log the weight and reps of every set; memory fails and the log is what tells you when to add load.

## Neighbouring blocks

The [strength routines](/en/strength-routines/) work with heavier loads and fewer reps, and the [volume routines](/en/volume-routines/) spread more sets per muscle group. This block sits in the middle: enough frequency to progress and enough variety to avoid repeating the same session all year.

When the block runs short, the next step changes the goal: more load, more sets or more days. The two neighbouring blocks cover those paths with specific plans.
