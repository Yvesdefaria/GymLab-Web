Here are the advantages and the limits of cable exercises you can do in the gym or at home.

## Cable exercises versus free weights: the angle of resistance changes

Choosing between cables and dumbbells is partly a matter of preference, but it also depends on your training goals. You need a little physics to understand why 15 kilos of resistance with dumbbells may not equal 15 kilos with cables.

### Dumbbell resistance

Imagine you do a dumbbell curl with 15 kilos. Absolute resistance stays constant at that figure, but relative resistance changes during the movement. From the lowest position, relative resistance rises as you lift the dumbbell and peaks when the **forearm** is parallel to the floor. From there it falls as the elbow joint angle shortens and the dumbbell comes closer to the shoulder.

That is why the load feels heavier at the sticking point and lighter at the end position.

### Cable resistance

The same principles apply to cable movements, but the difference is the **angle of resistance**. Dumbbell resistance depends on gravity, with force always directed towards the floor. A cable's force can come from other directions.

The relative resistance of the cable also changes with the elbow joint position, though the changes are less dramatic when you use the right cable angle. The most efficient angle is the one that **follows the exercise movement as closely as possible**. The result is **continuous, more consistent tension**, the basic benefit of cable movements.

## Advantages of cable exercises

### They isolate large muscle groups

Many of the **free-weight** exercises you do for chest, back and shoulders also depend on other muscles: the triceps in chest and shoulder movements, the biceps in back exercises. Cables offer many single-joint movements for those areas.

For example, **cable crossovers** are one of the best ways to isolate the pecs. Cable lateral raises are excellent for the delts, and cable pullovers are superb for the lats.

### They finish a muscle and add detail

You do not have to limit cables to your final exercise, but they are very useful for **pump sets**. Suppose you do three biceps exercises. You start with heavy barbell curls, 6 to 8 reps, move to dumbbell curls, seated or standing, 8 to 12 reps, and finish with cable curls, raising the reps to 15 or 20.

Use a similar protocol for any other muscle and make the most of the continuous tension cables produce.

### They add variety to a fixed routine

There are countless exercises you can do on cables when you want to be creative, something essential for breaking out of a rut. Try to invent a new cable exercise, or work with cables only from time to time.

## When not to use cable exercises

### To build your base as a beginner

When you start training you need a basic programme. It is not the moment to base your work on cables. Use the **barbell** and **dumbbells** for presses, rows, deadlifts and squats, and mix in cables only sometimes.

### To work with big loads

Advanced or not, you use [big loads](/en/how-much-weight-to-lift/) when you want to increase strength and size. Here too you need **free weights**.

There are exceptions, such as lat pulldowns, which can be done with heavy loads and still deliver great results. The same goes for cable rows, excellent for adding density to the back.

## Conclusion: cables or free weights?

If you ask which one produces more development in the long run, the answer is **both**. Variety is one of the critical ingredients of a successful [muscle-building](/en/build-muscle/) programme.

Variety comes from constantly exposing the muscles to different stimuli, including work from different angles. Cables and dumbbells have their own advantages, but the benefits are not significant enough to exclude either.
