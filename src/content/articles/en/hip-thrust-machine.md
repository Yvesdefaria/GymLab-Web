The hip thrust machine, also called a glute drive, performs the same movement as the [barbell version](/en/barbell-hip-thrust/) with one clear advantage: the carriage guides the load and there is no bar to mount on the pelvis. The downside is that the body sits trapped between the backrest and the pad, and a bad adjustment sends the force where it does not belong. The benefits do not change and live in the [hip thrust benefits guide](/en/hip-thrust-benefits/).

## The pad goes on the crease, not the crest

The landmark is the anterior superior iliac spine, the bone you can feel at the front of the hip. The pad or belt goes below that point, on the hip crease. The iliac crest sits above and behind it, and that is the wrong spot.

The difference is not cosmetic. On the crease, force travels to the hip and the drive moves the pelvis. On the crest or the abdomen, the pad compresses the belly, the torso locks and the load ends up pushing the spine. It is the machine's most common fault and the one that leaves the feeling that the exercise never reaches the glute.

## Backrest and feet by femur length

Set the backrest so the knees sit around ninety degrees at the top. If it is too high, the pelvis stays seated and the range is cut; if it is too low, the back arches to reach the top.

The feet go under the knees. Moving them forward adds hip range and some hamstring; moving them closer adds knee flexion and quadriceps. The neutral position is the starting point, not a fixed rule.

The test is the same for everyone: at the top, the hip ends extended without the lower back arching and the knees sit near ninety degrees. If either fails, move the backrest before touching the weight. Change one thing at a time: backrest first, then feet, then load.

## Range and tempo

The body lowers as one block, ribs down and abdomen braced. The pelvis does not roll back to chase the bottom: it goes as far as the hip can without losing position. The head stays in line with the spine; there is no reason to look up at the ceiling.

At the top, the glute squeezes for one to two seconds with a slight posterior pelvic tilt. There is no need to hyperextend the lower back to reach the machine's stop. The recommended tempo is 3-1-1: three seconds down, one second pause at the top and one second up with intent. The top pause is the part that changes the feel of the exercise most: without it, the machine returns the load before the glute finishes contracting.

The feet push the platform without leaving the backrest. If the back lifts to reach the top, the range is too long for that setting. The pelvis rises as one block and the backrest only follows.

## How to start with no plates

The first session runs with no plates. The machine already provides resistance and many are heavier than they look. One unloaded warm-up set checks the range and the backrest height. If the model has stops or a lock, check them before the first set.

Week zero is three sets of ten to twelve reps at a five or six out of ten effort. Once three sets of twelve close with the same position, add five to ten kilos to the total. A linear progression of two and a half to five kilos a week works when sets close at a seven or eight effort. [Progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) orders the rest.

## Two working templates

Template A spreads the work over two sessions and keeps the glute in the plan alongside everything else. Template B concentrates more volume in three days and fits a block with a hip emphasis.

## Mistakes the machine does not fix

- **Pad on the abdomen.** Lower it to the hip crease and check that it does not cut your breathing.
- **Hyperextending at the top.** Think ribs down and finish with a neutral pelvis, not an arched back.
- **Leaving the carriage locked with the weight on.** Check that the lock is released before the set.
- **Keeping the same setting on a different machine.** Every model has different levers: give it two sessions before judging the load.
- **Feeling nothing in the glute.** Lower the weight, add the top pause and shorten the bottom range a little.

## Machine or bar

The machine wins on learning curve and convenience: the path is fixed, the load is measured and the setup does not depend on a free rack. The bar wins on freedom and transfer if you compete or if the free-weight movement is part of the goal. Both allow the same dose, so the choice can change by block. If both are available, alternate them by blocks: eight weeks on the machine, then a block on the bar.

## Where it fits

It goes after the main lift of the day, or first in a glute session. The [leg routines](/en/leg-routines/) show where to place it, and accessory work is split between [glute bridges](/en/glute-bridge/), [hip extension](/en/hip-extension-glutes/) and [hip anatomy](/en/hip-anatomy/) as a reference for what is missing.
