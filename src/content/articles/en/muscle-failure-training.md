**Muscle failure** is the point where you cannot complete one more rep. Bodybuilders have argued about it for a long time. Many believe it is bad for strength because the muscle learns not to complete the rep, and they fear that pattern stays in muscle memory and shows up on a hard set.

Research indicates that one set to failure matters for strength, but doing more than one per exercise can work against you. On muscle development, few studies confirm its role. What is clear is that the number of sets to failure needs controlling: too many disrupt hormone levels and the growth process.

## What happens in the muscle when you reach failure

At the moment of failure you need one hundred percent effort intensity to complete the last rep. The muscle sits at its functional limit and must contract at full capacity. That one hundred percent intensity produces a huge stimulus and a hypertrophic adaptation.

During exercise, muscles get energy from a substance called adenosine triphosphate (ATP). That energy unit has three sources, phosphocreatine, glycogen and oxygen, which act in sequence, like dominoes falling in a line.

On the first rep, phosphocreatine supplies the energy. It is stored inside the muscle and ready for action, but its supply lasts only about 10 seconds. From then on, glycogen takes over.

The chemical conversion of glycogen releases lactic acid as a waste product. Overproduction of that acid causes the burning pain and leads to fatigue in two or three minutes. The first two sources are anaerobic, so they need no oxygen.

On a set to failure, the tired muscles complain about the excess acid. Breathing becomes harder and speeds up to supply the third source, oxygen. Blood flow increases, releasing oxygen to cover the tissue deficit and clearing the lactic acid. That rapid supply inflates the muscle like a balloon and produces the pump.

An intense set to failure is an assault. It empties the energy stores and causes microscopic damage to the tissues. That fibre disruption produces delayed-onset muscle soreness, which peaks a day or two later.

During recovery, glycogen and phosphocreatine are restored from dietary carbohydrates and creatine. The damaged fibres are repaired through protein synthesis using amino acids from the diet, and the process rebuilds larger fibres. Fibrillar disruption is essential for hypertrophy and takes five to seven days to repair after high-intensity weight training. After an assault like that, adequate rest is essential.

## The case against failure training

When a muscle contracts against a weight, it faces two kinds of stress. **Metabolic** stress comes from changes in the cell's chemical composition while you lift. **Mechanical** stress is the force of the weight against the muscle; the more sets and reps you do, the longer that force lasts and the greater the mechanical stress.

Both are probably the cause of the increase in muscle cell size, and both appear in normal training up to the point of fatigue.

Training to failure gives the muscle more metabolic stress across the session, because greater fatigue produces more chemical by-products such as lactic acid. When you do not train to failure, the muscle can take more mechanical stress, because you can do more reps and lift more weight on the last sets.

Which is better for strength and size? Science has no conclusive evidence, but mechanical stress seems to weigh more. Although many consider failure training superior, the physiological evidence disagrees: motor unit firing rate drops when contraction duration increases, as in the last reps of a set to failure. Motor units enter a state of inhibition and produce a slower, weaker signal. That seems to be the biggest flaw of training to fatigue.

A practical example. You load 80 kilos and do 12 reps to failure. You rest two minutes and can only manage about 9 on the next set. You rest two more minutes and reach 7 on the third. The total weight lifted across the three sets is about 2000 kilos.

Now use 90 percent of 80 kilos, about 72 kilos. Even though you lowered the weight, you lift more in total: you do not reach failure on the first set of 12 and you can repeat the same reps on the following sets. The total is about 2500 kilos, 20 percent more. Add that up across a chest routine of 9 to 12 sets and the muscles receive considerably more mechanical stress.

Many bodybuilders centre their training on reaching failure, but other athletes who lift weights rarely do. Jay Cutler trains with relatively light weights to focus on the feel of each rep and almost never reaches failure. Fatigue affects metabolic pathways and the nervous system itself, which is why you cannot repeat the same reps after hitting the limit, even with enough rest.

Failure training has three disadvantages: less total weight lifted; higher injury risk, because the stabilisers tire and the joints are overloaded on the final reps; and higher overtraining risk, because failure delays recovery and is sometimes not paired with extra rest.

If your progress is slow or non-existent despite training as often as you should, and you habitually train to the limit, the answer is simple: train without reaching fatigue.

## The case for failure training

Classic bodybuilding states it bluntly: if you want to gain muscle, you must work the muscle to the point of failure. Failure training is linked to lactic acid buildup and to soreness. Fibres get damaged and enter a repair cycle: the muscle adapts and grows. The more stress it tolerates, the bigger it gets; with less stress, it atrophies.

When lactates rise, so do other metabolites, such as phosphate and hydrogen ions, which feed contractions and boost growth. Muscles can grow to a point without forming metabolites: sedentary volunteers gained size and strength training in a lab without lactates, phosphates or hydrogen ions. But those metabolites are important precursors of growth.

The purpose of bodybuilding is to stress the body systematically so it improves its ability to build stronger, bigger muscles. If the stress is not enough to overload the body, there is no adaptation.

Think about the true set. If someone stops a set at rep 10 and could have done 4 or 5 more, did they do a good set? A set is only good when you cannot do one more rep. If you do 10 and fail on the 11th, you did a real set of 10. Strength is directly proportional to muscle size: with the same diet, rest, time of day and temperature, if that person tries the same set a month later and does 12 reps, they have most likely gained size. Someone who avoids failure has less control over their progress.

Intensity works the same way. People picture huge weights, but failure is the inability to keep a given intensity, whatever the weight. Speed skaters have big legs and train with submaximal loads that keep rising. Even if you reach failure with a submaximal load, use a weight close to your maximum so you recruit enough fibres.

The most developed men in the world train to failure. The ones who grow most are those who work regularly to absolute failure. Beginners gain similarly without reaching failure, but once they become intermediate and advanced they stop progressing until they use true sets. People whose purpose and profession is gaining strength and size train to fatigue.

## What the 16-week study found

Spanish scientists trained 42 men for 16 weeks. Some reached failure on every set and others on none. They measured strength and power, plus blood levels of the anabolic hormones IGF-1 and testosterone and the catabolic hormone cortisol, before the programme and at 6, 11 and 16 weeks.

In the *Journal of Applied Physiology* they reported that both groups improved strength and power similarly, but the failure group had much lower IGF-1, somewhat lower testosterone and higher cortisol.

Training to failure on too many sets can lead to overtraining, as the drop in IGF-1 and testosterone and the rise in cortisol show. The suggestion is to reach failure only on the last set of each exercise, a protocol that has improved strength and should do the same for muscle development.

## How to go beyond failure

How can you be sure you have recruited every fibre and that the muscle is exhausted? If you want to destroy it completely, you can go past failure and produce an even bigger hypertrophic response. Training beyond failure extends the working set into the hyperintensity zone, an extreme stimulus that demands 110 percent effort.

When you reach failure on the positive phase of the last rep:

1. Stop the weight in a **static contraction**, isometric but without locking the joints. Contract the muscle as hard as you can and hold the weight as long as possible, until you fail in the static hold too.
2. Lower the weight as slowly as you can, forcing a **final negative** eccentric contraction.
3. Ask a training partner to help you through one last **forced rep**.

The assault happens after positive failure: the muscle fails in the static hold and fails again on the negative. The set starts when the muscle fails.

These methods are very intense and, used well, produce large hypertrophic adaptations. To avoid injury you need a partner, especially with free weights. Do not use them on every set: a couple of sets for one muscle group is enough to stay out of overtraining.

## Rules for putting failure to work

- **Use cycles.** Instead of training to failure all the time, do it every one or two workouts.
- **Save failure for the last or last two sets of an exercise.** Chase it on every set and you will end up overtraining.
- **Take one or two extra rest days after a draining workout.** You will feel sore for a couple of days; listen to your body.
- **Always train with strict form.** Reaching failure does not mean lifting badly.
- **Use forced reps in isolation.** They extend failure training, excellent for strength and size, but occasional.
- **Do not train to failure after a long break.** Get back in shape before working your muscles to the limit.
- **Do not train to failure on chest, shoulder or leg without a partner.** Nobody wants to get trapped under the weight.
- **Do not train to failure on the squat.** You can, but the injury risk is too high; if you try, have two spotters watching. For legs, practise failure on the leg press or hack squat.

Failure is a tool, and it works best in small doses. Overloading muscles to absolute fatigue is necessary for strength and size, but using failure alone can produce the opposite effect and lead to atrophy. Keep it in your programme and use it with moderation.
