The snatch is the first movement of a weightlifting competition and the harder of the two. The bar leaves the floor and arrives overhead with no intermediate stop, and the lifter receives it in a squat with the arms straight. A bad catch cannot be pressed into place.

## What sets the snatch apart

It is a power lift with a high technical demand. The bar travels further than in the clean and the weight it accepts is lower, so relative load matters less than position. A beginner needs weeks with an empty bar to put the pulls in order: mastering the movement takes months.

## The grip and the start position

The snatch grip is the widest in the sport: around one and a half shoulder widths. One way to find it is to hold the bar standing, arms relaxed, and check that it sits in the hip crease, where the thigh folds. If the bar lands at the pubis, the grip is too narrow; if it climbs to the navel, too wide.

With the bar on the floor, the feet sit at hip width or slightly narrower, toes turned out a little. The bar goes over the middle of the foot, the shins almost touch it and the hips sit between knee and shoulder height. The shoulders lean slightly in front of the bar, the lats are set and the spine stays neutral.

A narrow grip forces the catch with bent elbows and makes the receiving squat impossible. The width is not a cosmetic preference.

## The first pull: floor to knee

The first pull is a push with the legs. The bar rises close to the shins and the torso angle stays almost constant; the knees move back as the bar passes them to make room.

Push through the whole foot. Rising onto the toes early brings the hips forward before the bar moves and sends it out in front; the heels stay down until full extension.

## The second pull: knee to hip crease

When the bar reaches mid-thigh, the torso straightens and the bar accelerates to the hip crease. Triple extension happens there: ankle, knee and hip. "Feet in motion" sums up the instant: the feet leave the floor, reset and land in the receiving position while the bar rises.

The elbows stay high and out, with the bar close; if it drifts away here, the catch travels forward.

## The catch in the overhead squat

After extension the lifter pulls under the bar instead of waiting for it: the hips drop fast, the knees open and the bar is received in a full squat.

The depth reference is the one from the [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/): thighs parallel to the floor or slightly below, torso and shin at a similar inclination and the bar over the middle of the foot. The snatch goes deep because that is where the bar ends closest to the floor. Full ankle and hip range is what lets the lifter descend without losing the vertical, and the [range of motion guide](/en/range-of-motion-training/) explains why that range matters.

From there the lifter stands up with the bar in the vertical and the back firm. The rep ends on both feet with the bar overhead.

## The three most common errors

The first is pulling with the arms. The arms are ropes: they stay straight and the body moves under the bar. If the elbows bend before the catch, the bar slows and the weight stays out front.

The second is cutting the second pull short. The bar needs height, and height comes from full extension; a lifter who does not finish the push ends up pressing the bar with the shoulders.

The third is receiving on raised heels or with the knees caved in. The catch looks like any other squat: feet flat, knees in line with the feet, weight spread across the foot.

## Sets, reps and load

The technical dose is four to six sets of two to three reps at 70-85 % of the maximum, with full recovery between sets. Two or three minutes are enough for the next set to come out clean.

The percentage matters less than the speed. If the bar slows, drop the weight: a slow snatch teaches the movement badly. With learning loads, five sets of three with an empty bar or at 50 % are worth more than one heavy set done badly.

## Where it fits in the week

The snatch goes at the start of the session, after the warm-up and before any accessory, because it needs a fresh nervous system. Two sessions a week are enough to progress on technique and leave room for the rest of the training.

It shares a family with the [clean](/en/clean/), which receives the bar on the shoulders, and the [clean and jerk](/en/clean-and-jerk/), which adds the jerk. The [power clean](/en/power-clean/) uses the same pull with a higher catch. For general strength work, [strength routines](/en/strength-routines/) place these lifts in a weekly plan. The [ballistic training guide](/en/ballistic-training/) explains why they are programmed with few reps and long rests.
