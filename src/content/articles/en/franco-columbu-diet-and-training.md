Franco Columbu was born in Sardinia and came to bodybuilding through boxing and powerlifting. Before he ever stepped onto a posing stage he was already moving weights that did not match his size, and that mix of strength and shape defined his career.

He stood around 1.65 metres and competed at about 84 or 85 kilos. At that height he won the [Mr. Olympia](/en/mr-olympia-winners/) in 1976 and again in 1981, the second one after a serious knee injury.

## Who Franco Columbu was

Raised among sheep in post-war Sardinia, Columbu settled in Germany, where he met an Arnold Schwarzenegger who was not yet famous. They competed together, trained together and stayed friends for decades.

His résumé was not the usual bodybuilder's. He boxed as an amateur, competed in powerlifting and trained as a chiropractor. That knowledge of anatomy helped him design his own rehabilitation after dislocating a knee at the first edition of the World's Strongest Man in 1977, while carrying a refrigerator on his back in one of the events.

Four years later he won the Mr. Olympia again, and that victory closed his career on stage.

## Strength as the base

Columbu believed a muscle only looks dense if it can move heavy loads. His numbers, at under 85 kilos of bodyweight, went past 230 kilos on the bench press, neared 300 on the squat and went over 320 on the deadlift.

The structure of his sessions was what later became known as powerbuilding. He opened with basic, heavy movements at low reps to stimulate the nervous system and the fast fibres, then moved to more volume and less weight chasing the pump. The [Weider principles](/en/weider-principles/) describe that second half: sets, angles and techniques he was using before they had a name.

## Chest and back in the same session

Part of Columbu's physique came from pairing chest and back in one session. He alternated the two groups in antagonist supersets, so the torso stayed full of blood and pumped from start to finish.

The horizontal cut across the upper chest came from heavy bench press, incline press and dumbbell flyes with a wide range of motion. He built the back with weighted pull-ups and heavy rows: width came from [pull-ups](/en/pull-ups/) and thickness from barbell rows and T-bar rows, always with a contraction at the end of the range.

## The Romanian deadlift and the posterior chain

Columbu used the Romanian deadlift as posterior-chain work, not just as a leg exercise. The dumbbell version, the [stiff-leg deadlift with dumbbells](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-dumbbells/), is the most accessible starting point: it teaches the hip hinge without the load of a barbell.

The idea was to stabilise the hips and the lower back so he could pull harder on rows. A deadlift done well supports back day instead of taking strength away from it.

## Every muscle two or three times a week

Columbu's frequency was high for his era. He trained each muscle group two or three times a week, which demands a recovery capacity nobody improvises.

That distribution has defenders and critics. The guide to [training frequency](/en/training-frequency/) explains how much weekly volume matters compared with the number of sessions, and why two days per group can work better than one when rest is covered.

## The Golden Era diet

He ate like the country man he had been. Whole eggs in quantity, red meat, fish, cheese and milk, all cooked at home and dense in calories per bite.

Red wine appeared now and then, a small glass, and he defended it for circulation. That is what he declared, not a recommendation.

The logic of the diet was density. Simple, abundant food to support a workload that would be hard to replicate today.

## The Arnold connection

The pair he formed with Arnold is the most cited in bodybuilding history. They checked each other's technique, pushed each other on heavy sets and competed for every rep.

That kind of partner changes a session. The guide to [training with a partner](/en/training-partner-benefits/) covers what it adds and what it demands; Arnold's shoulder tricks, in [this guide](/en/arnold-shoulder-training/), came out of that same stretch of shared sessions.

## What you can take and what you cannot

Columbu's method reads as a list of priorities: heavy basics, high frequency, real food and a partner who will not let you slow down.

The numbers are the part nobody can copy. That routine assumes years of base and a body that tolerates the volume without breaking; for almost any reader, the starting point is different. Columbu's two Mr. Olympia titles were the result of a decade of work, not of a twelve-week plan.
