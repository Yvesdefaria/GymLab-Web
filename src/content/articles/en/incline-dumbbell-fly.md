Flys are sold as an isolation exercise and performed as a light press. The difference sits in the elbow: if it bends to press, the chest stops following the arc.

## Setup: a 30-degree bench and palms facing each other

Backrest at about 30 degrees. On an incline bench the upper chest gets more work, and above 30 degrees the front deltoid starts to take over. The angle is measured on the pad; the full argument is in the [incline chest exercises](/en/incline-chest-exercises/) guide.

Sit with a dumbbell on each thigh. Lie back and bring them up with a knee drive until the arms are extended over the upper chest. The palms face each other and the elbows hold a fixed bend of 10 to 15 degrees. That bend does not change during the set: the elbow is a lever that transmits what the shoulder does.

The shoulder blades come together and stay on the pad. If the shoulders lift off, the arc turns into a press with very wide dumbbells.

## The arc

From the extended position, open out to the sides along a wide arc. The range ends when the arms are level with the shoulders. Lower than that, the weight moves away from its support and the shoulder stops being in a comfortable position; the extra stretch does not make up for it.

On the way back, the same arc in reverse, with the wrists not rotating. The dumbbells come close without touching and without one crossing in front of the other.

The load is light. On this exercise weight is not the variable: the arc is, and one extra kilo shortens it.

## Where the tension drops off

With a dumbbell, resistance pulls towards the floor through the whole range. At the top of the arc the arms end up almost vertical over the shoulder and the dumbbell loses its lever: the weight is still there, but the chest no longer has to hold it. The bottom is the opposite, with the arm open and the weight far away, and the stretch at its peak.

The incline bench makes that split worse because the torso is already tilted back: the top of the arc ends up even closer to vertical.

That is why the [incline cable fly](/en/incline-cable-fly/) exists and why it does not replace this one. The cable covers the top; the dumbbell covers the stretch.

## Execution and mistakes

- **Bending the elbows on the way in.** The arc becomes a press and the triceps join the movement.
- **Going below shoulder line.** You gain stretch and lose control of the shoulder.
- **Crossing the hands.** It adds rotation at the shoulder and shortens the useful range.
- **A bench at 45 degrees or more.** The front deltoid takes the work and the upper chest loses the angle.
- **Loading it like a press.** With too much weight the arc shortens and the fly stops being a fly.
- **Shrugging the shoulders.** The shoulder blade rides up and the chest loses its base.

## Dose and where it goes in the session

Two or three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. Pick a weight that lets every rep follow the same arc; once 15 reps hold it, add a kilo per hand.

It goes at the end of the session, after the [incline dumbbell press](/en/incline-dumbbell-press/) or the [incline barbell press](/en/incline-barbell-press/), once the chest is worked and weight is no longer the point. It also works as a first movement in a short upper-chest session.

For the same movement on a flat bench, the [flat dumbbell fly](/en/flat-dumbbell-fly/) keeps the arc and changes the angle. The difference between the two heads of the chest is in [chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/).
