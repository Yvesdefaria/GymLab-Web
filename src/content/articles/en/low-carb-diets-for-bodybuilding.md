The debate usually turns into a war between camps. One side argues that carbohydrates make you fat; the other, that without them there is no muscle. Neither sentence survives the detail. What exists is a range of intakes, each with a different metabolic response and a different cost for training.

## What very low carbohydrate means

There is no single border. A diet is called low-carb when carbohydrates fall below 30 % of calories, and ketogenic when intake stays under about 50 grams a day for several days. At that point the liver produces ketone bodies from fat and the body uses them as fuel, brain included.

Ketosis is a normal metabolic pathway that switches on when glucose is scarce, and ketones are fuel for the brain and muscle. The idea that fat cannot be burned without carbohydrates circulates widely, and ketosis proves the opposite.

## The cost for strength training

Muscle glycogen is the fuel for heavy sets and repeated efforts. When the diet barely provides carbohydrate, stores fall and training changes: the first set goes well, the fifth costs more and total volume drops. In a hypertrophy plan with ten sets per muscle group, that loss of quality shows within weeks.

There is a second effect that gets less attention. Each gram of glycogen carries about 2.7 grams of water, so emptying the stores sheds several kilos of weight in a few days. The scale drops fast and the mirror improves, but much of that loss is water. That is a reason not to read the first week as fat loss.

## How much carbohydrate a lifter needs

The general reference for regular strength training is 3 to 5 grams per kilo of body weight per day. That figure supports glycogen and allows training with volume. A very low-carb diet sits far below it, and the reasonable question is what minimum keeps the session standing.

For an 80-kilo lifter, the 100-150 gram band equals 1.2 to 1.9 grams per kilo. It is low, but it leaves room to place most of it around the session: one portion before, one after and the rest spread across the day. That distribution preserves training quality without giving up the deficit.

## Ketosis: possible, but adherence decides

A ketogenic diet can work for fat loss. Trials comparing diets with the same calories and the same protein find small differences in loss, and the advantage that sometimes appears is adherence: some people find it easier to eat this way because they are less hungry.

The problem appears when the goal includes performance. A bodybuilder in a deficit who also trains hard needs to sustain intensity, and ketosis makes that difficult in long sessions. For someone training three days a week with moderate volume, it can be a reasonable option. For someone doing twenty sets per session, the cost rises too far.

## Saturated fat, metabolism and libido, with nuance

Very low-carb diets tend to raise dietary fat, and with it saturated fat. That is neither automatic nor compulsory: the diet can be built on olive oil, nuts, avocado, fish and low-fat dairy. The general recommendation remains that most fat should be unsaturated, and that rule does not change because carbohydrates fall.

Metabolism adapts to the calorie deficit and to weight loss, not to a lack of carbohydrates in particular. A smaller body spends less, and any diet with many weeks of restriction spends less. Libido falls mainly when available energy is very low for a long time, and that is a reason not to stretch an aggressive deficit.

## How to build a low-carb diet without losing performance

Start by setting protein at 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo, which is what protects muscle in a deficit. The guide to [protein](/en/protein-guide/) covers the spread and the sources. Then set fat at a reasonable minimum, around 0.6 to 1 gram per kilo, and fill the rest with carbohydrate. If the total comes out low, prioritise carbohydrate before and after training and leave vegetables and fruit for the rest of the day.

Fibre is the weak point of these diets. Without wholegrains or legumes, vegetables, nuts and seeds have to compensate. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) explains how to cover fibre without raising calories, and the one on [intermittent fasting](/en/intermittent-fasting-and-weights/) shows another way to organise meals that also depends on energy balance.

## When to choose each option

A very low-carbohydrate diet makes sense in a short cutting phase, in someone who tolerates ketosis well and trains with moderate volume. It also makes sense for anyone who needs appetite control and finds more satiety in fat and protein. It does not make sense as a permanent rule if the goal is to gain strength and muscle at the same time.

Carbohydrate cycling is the middle ground: high days around hard sessions and low days for the rest. The guide to [carb cycling](/en/carb-cycling/) develops it with numbers. In any case, the main lever remains total calories and protein, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) remind you that no product fixes a badly built plan.
