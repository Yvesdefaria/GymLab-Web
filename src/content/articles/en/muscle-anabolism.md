Two processes run in the body at once. Anabolism builds large molecules from smaller ones and consumes energy. Catabolism does the opposite: it breaks large molecules down to release energy. Neither switches off completely, even at rest.

## Anabolism and catabolism at the same time

Muscle is in constant turnover. Every day proteins are broken down and others are made, and what matters for size is the difference between them. That difference is called net protein balance.

If synthesis exceeds breakdown, the balance is positive and muscle can grow. If the opposite happens, the balance is negative and tissue is lost. A single day defines nothing: the sum of many weeks does.

## The session opens the door

Strength training is the stimulus. A session with loads raises muscle protein synthesis for hours and also raises breakdown, because muscle repairs damage. Without food, net balance usually stays negative even when the stimulus was good.

That is why the plan combines two things. Weights tell the body where to build; food provides the materials and the energy to do it. Neither replaces the other.

## Leucine gives the signal and amino acids supply the material

Leucine, one of the nine essential amino acids, activates muscle protein synthesis. The practical threshold is around 2.5 to 3 grams per meal. It is reached with 25 to 40 grams of quality protein, animal or well combined plant sources.

The signal alone is not enough. Muscle needs the other essential amino acids as bricks, and a meal with leucine but without the rest leaves the work half done. That is why the daily total matters so much: 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo of body weight, spread across three or four servings. The guide to [BCAA amino acids](/en/bcaa-supplements/) explains why three out of nine are not enough.

## Insulin is permissive

Insulin carries glucose and amino acids into cells and slows protein breakdown. It is a condition that allows growth. The cause is the stimulus of training with amino acids and energy available. A diet with little insulin and enough protein still builds muscle, and a meal with plenty of insulin but no amino acids builds nothing.

The idea that anabolism switches on with a hormone spike comes from confusing a signal with a result. What counts is the availability of amino acids and energy across the day. A one hour peak weighs little.

## Energy sets the sign of the balance

At maintenance, with high protein and strength training, the body can rebuild tissue slowly. Gaining muscle weight at a reasonable pace requires a small surplus, 200 to 300 kcal above expenditure, and a rise of 0.25 to 0.5 % of body weight per week.

In a deficit the opposite happens: net balance approaches zero and growth slows, although protein and weights reduce muscle loss. The guide to [calories for muscle gain](/en/calories-for-muscle-gain/) develops the calculation, and the one on [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) organises the training plan.

## How long it takes and how to measure it

Anabolism does not show in a week. A beginner can gain muscle at a good pace for months; someone with years of training moves much more slowly. The scale alone is useless because it mixes fat, water and glycogen. Strength on the basic lifts, a tape measure and comparison photos every four to six weeks do work.

Sleeping 7 to 9 hours and hitting protein every day does more for the balance than any fine tuning of timing. [Sports performance](/en/sports-nutrition-for-performance/) depends on the same base: enough energy, protein spread across the day and hydration.

## What does not change the result

No supplement replaces food or training. Creatine has support for strength and mass, and the rest of the shelf adds little when protein is covered. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) review the most common ones.

Growth is the consequence of a sustained positive balance. A perfect session or an exact meal does not decide it. The [anabolic window](/en/anabolic-window/) matters less than its name suggests, and the daily total matters more.
