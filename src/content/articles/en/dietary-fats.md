For decades public advice was to cut fat without distinguishing types. Then came the opposite reaction, which defended saturated fat as harmless. Both positions confuse type with amount. Sorting those two sums into order clarifies the debate and leaves rules you can use in a shop.

## Two sums that do not mix

Fat type changes the lipid profile, inflammation and cardiovascular risk. Total fat changes calories and, with them, weight. A diet can be high in fat and healthy if the fat is good and the calories add up. It can also be low in fat and poor if sugar and refined flour fill the gap.

## How much fat per day

Population guidance places fat between 20 and 35 percent of calories. Within that range, saturated fat stays under 10 percent of calories and industrial trans fats stay as close to zero as possible.

Twenty percent is a floor rather than a target: below it, covering essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins gets harder. Thirty-five percent is a reasonable ceiling for someone who trains and wants more calories without more food volume.

## The types, without surnames

Saturated fat is solid at room temperature and common in butter, cured cheese, fatty meat and coconut oil. It raises LDL when it replaces unsaturated fat, which is why moderation makes sense.

Monounsaturated fat is liquid and stable, with olive oil as the reference. Polyunsaturated fat includes omega 3 from oily fish and omega 6 from seed oils; both are essential and must come from food.

Industrial trans fats are the only group with no defence. They raise LDL, lower HDL and are associated with higher risk at small doses. The guide to [trans fats](/en/trans-fats/) explains how to spot them on a label.

## The lipid panel in plain language

Blood tests scare more than they should because the initials are never explained. Each measure describes one part of how fat travels through the blood:

Total cholesterol adds fractions that move in different directions, so a high figure can hide a high HDL and a normal LDL. LDL carries cholesterol to tissues and its excess deposits in arteries. HDL returns it to the liver. Triglycerides reflect leftover energy and respond to alcohol and sugar.

Targets change with each person's risk. A blood test is read alongside the clinical history, and one isolated number does not define treatment.

## Sources worth keeping close

Virgin olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish and seeds cover most of the unsaturated fat in a week. Vegetables and legumes supply little fat and plenty of fibre, and complete the plate.

Meat and dairy supply saturated fat along with protein and micronutrients. The guide to [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) and the one on [muscle for vegetarians](/en/vegetarian-muscle-gain/) show how to cover protein with or without animal products.

## What fat does not do

Dietary fat does not choose where it gets burned. No food reduces abdominal fat on its own, and the idea that eating fat speeds up fat loss does not hold: a calorie deficit remains the condition.

Nor are there magic portions. The serving of nuts or olives depends on the day's calories and on what the rest of the plate provides. The daily total and the quality of the source are what count.

## How to spread it

Spread fat across main meals and keep unsaturated sources for dressing oil, nuts and fish. If you train and eat little volume, fat helps raise calories without filling the stomach. If you are in a deficit, measure it, because it packs a lot of energy into little weight.
