## The machine owns the path, you own the distance

In a Smith machine shoulder press the bar does not travel through a plane you choose. Two vertical guides force it up and down along a fixed line. The only thing you can place is yourself: the backrest angle, the bar height on the hooks and the distance from the bench to the bar line.

That last setting is the exercise. The bar rises and falls in front of the face; if the bench is too close it passes through the chin. If it is too far it passes behind the head. The right distance keeps the bar in front of the jaw and the nose for the whole rep, with no need to tip the head back.

## The backrest and the bar height

The backrest sits between 80 and 85 degrees. A vertical backrest forces the head back to let the bar through; a very reclined one turns the press into an incline chest press.

The bar is racked at eye height. From there the first drive starts near the top of the range and the descent has a known endpoint. If the bar sits low, the press starts behind the head and the range changes without you noticing.

## The distance to the bench

With the backrest at 80 degrees and the bar at eye height, move the bench forward or back until the bar passes in front of the chin. Check it without weight: sit down, grip the bar and lower the elbows. The bar should pass a hand's width from the face.

That setting is the part of the exercise the machine does not solve. The rails fix the path and, with it, the relationship between the bar and your head. You decide where the head sits.

## The rep

The hands sit just outside the shoulders, with a pronated grip and the wrists aligned. Unhook the bar and drive the elbows up without locking out at the top. The joint does not need to hit the end stop to close the rep.

The descent ends at ear height. Lowering to the chest is a chest press; lowering to the neck is a behind-the-neck press with another label. The ribs stay down and the back stays on the pad. If the shoulders climb toward the ears on the drive, the backrest is wrong or the weight is too heavy.

## What the rails do not do

The guided bar takes care of balance and path; joint load and scapular rotation stay in the shoulder. The shoulder still holds the humeral head in the socket, and the scapula still rotates as the arm passes overhead.

A fixed path can also hold you in a bad position: if the bench is set wrong, the bar passes where it should not on every rep, not on one. The backrest and the bar at eye height are the part you can control.

The behind-the-neck variant has its own depth criterion and its own mobility test; the argument lives in [military press vs behind-the-neck press](/en/military-press-vs-behind-neck-press/) and does not need repeating here.

## Dose

Three sets of 8 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The load goes up when the last rep ends at ear height and the torso is still on the pad.
