The one-dumbbell version is rarely used as a main lift and often used as a test. Its value is that it forces the arm to stay vertical while the hips descend, and that exposes restrictions a back squat can hide.

## What the overhead dumbbell squat measures

It measures three things at once: shoulder flexion, thoracic extension and ankle dorsiflexion. It also shows how the squat organises itself when the weight is overhead rather than on the back.

The legs can usually go deeper than the arm position allows. The usual limit shows up in the shoulder that will not reach, the upper back that stays stiff or the ankle that will not follow, before it shows up in the quadriceps. Leg anatomy is in [quadriceps anatomy](/en/quadriceps-anatomy/).

## How to do it

Feet slightly wider than the shoulders, toes turned slightly out. One dumbbell in one hand, the arm straight overhead and the elbow locked. The other hand rests on the hip or moves out for balance.

Ribs stay down, the abdomen is braced and the gaze is forward. Descend by bending the knee and the hip as if sitting down, heel planted and arm vertical. The descent ends where the position holds: if the heel lifts or the arm drifts forward, you have gone past it.

## Why the position fails before the leg

If the shoulder cannot reach full flexion with the ribs down, the dumbbell drifts forward and the arm loses vertical. If the thoracic spine will not extend, the torso tips and the weight moves away from centre.

If the ankle lacks dorsiflexion, the heel lifts or the weight shifts to the toes. These are position signals, not diagnoses: a coach or a physiotherapist can assess each one. The [squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) guide covers the general pattern and [head position in the squat](/en/squat-head-position/) covers neck control.

## The one-rep screen

Do one rep per side with a light dumbbell. Descend to the point where the first compensation appears and note the depth and the difference between sides. Read the screen from the side, where the arm line, the ribs and the heel are easiest to compare. Repeat after the warm-up to see whether the position improves.

The two-dumbbell version shares the load, but the one-arm version is the better screen: one side works alone and the difference between sides is easy to see. Flat shoes and a stable surface help you read the ankle.

The screen is most useful as a before-and-after: one rep at the start of the session and one after the warm-up. If the depth improves, the restriction was temporary; if it does not, the drill stays light.

## Sets and reps

As a screen, one rep per side. As a warm-up or shoulder-block drill, two or three sets of 5 to 8 reps per side with a light dumbbell and no attempt at failure.

It is not a strength lift. If the load goes up, the position becomes the limit and the squat stops measuring what it was meant to measure.

## Common mistakes

The first is loading the dumbbell as if it were a press. The second is flaring the ribs and arching the lower back to reach deeper. The third is letting the arm drift forward and compensating with the torso.

The fourth is forcing depth with the heels up. The squat is measured by what the position holds, not by what a bounce can reach.

## Where it fits

It goes in the warm-up for a leg or shoulder day, and as a shoulder-block drill before a press. It is not a hamstring or glute exercise, so it does not replace the hinge or the curl.
