There are many ways to **train**, and the constant bombardment of methods ends up confusing you. High volume, low volume, high frequency, infrequent training, to failure, not to failure, heavy loads, pump training. The list could go on forever.

## The six Charles Poliquin strategies

**Charles Poliquin** firmly believes that having 100% confidence in the effectiveness of a programme is one of the most important factors for success with a routine. That confidence affects the degree of effort, concentration and dedication you put into training.

That is why he argues that each of these programmes delivers results: if you believe in them, you make big gains. Below you have six training strategies and the moment when each one is worth applying, depending on your goal.

## Heavy loads, high frequency and low volume

The first strategy is based on **heavy weights**, within 85-100% of your one-rep max (1RM), with **basic exercises** performed frequently.

Full-body routines are usually done three times a week, or an upper/lower split of four sessions a week. Each session includes either a **full-body** routine or an **upper/lower** routine.

### Full-body method

- A basic exercise with **quadriceps** dominance (a form of squat, leg press, etc.).
- A basic exercise with **hip** dominance (Romanian deadlift, good mornings, stiff-leg deadlift, sumo deadlift, etc.).
- A basic **horizontal pull** exercise (any row variation).
- A basic **horizontal push** exercise (flat bench press, decline with dumbbells, barbell or machine, or dips).
- A **vertical pull** exercise (pull-ups or cable pulldowns with any grip).
- A **vertical push** exercise (incline chest press with barbell, machine or dumbbells).
- An abdominal exercise.

### Upper/lower method

**Lower-body day (legs)**

- A **squat** variation (front, close stance, medium stance, etc.).
- A basic exercise with **hip** dominance (Romanian deadlift, good mornings, stiff-leg deadlift, sumo deadlift, etc.).
- A secondary compound exercise for the **quadriceps** (leg press variation, hack squat, etc.).
- A secondary exercise for the **hamstrings** (leg curl, stiff-leg deadlift, etc.).
- A **unilateral** exercise for the lower body (lunges, Bulgarian split squat, split squat, etc.).
- A **calf** exercise.

**Upper-body day (torso)**

- A basic **horizontal pull** exercise (any row variation).
- A basic **horizontal push** exercise (flat bench press, decline with dumbbells, barbell or machine, or dips).
- A **vertical pull** exercise (pull-ups or cable pulldowns with any grip).
- A **vertical push** exercise (incline chest press with barbell, machine or dumbbells).
- A **biceps** exercise.
- A **triceps** exercise.

### Methods that fit this strategy

- **Normal lifting:** with 85-100% of your max (1-3 and 4-6 rep ranges).
- **Clusters:** use 87-92% of your max and perform sets of 5 reps with 10 seconds of rest between reps.
- **Rest-pause:** perform a set of 4-6RM; when you reach failure, rest 10-12 seconds and then do a few more reps.
- **Alternating sets:** two exercises that work different muscle groups, with 60-90 seconds of rest between each exercise. They are not supersets.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- Very significant motor learning due to the **high frequency** of a movement pattern, with rapid strength gains.
- Emphasis on the **neural** aspects of force production thanks to **heavy lifting**. An advantage if you are an athlete, and less so if you only train to improve your physique.
- **Very efficient:** every exercise is basic, involves a lot of muscle mass and lets you train the whole body with few exercises. A big advantage when you have little time.

**Disadvantages**

- **It can lead to unbalanced development.** Using only compound movements may leave some muscle groups understimulated, because the body will use the best-adapted muscles to do the work during compound movements. Your weak points will not improve as much as your strong ones.
- **Lower performance in some exercises.** After hard squats and hip movements, your energy levels will be low. If you still have exercises to do, they will suffer.
- **Limited to one type of gain.** With heavy weights only, strength-endurance and hypertrophy gains will be limited or below optimal.

**Good or bad: high energy expenditure.** Compound exercises require more energy, and training the whole body at once raises the metabolic rate for hours. It is an advantage if you are trying to lose fat or have a slow metabolism, and a disadvantage if you are trying to gain a lot of weight or have a fast metabolism, though you can compensate by eating more.

### When to use this strategy

- If you are an athlete who trains mainly for sports performance.
- If your main goal is strength.
- If you have little time to train.
- As a transition from a more conventional bodybuilding period.

## Undulating load, high frequency and low volume

The second strategy is very similar to the first. The big difference is the selection of the training load.

While the first only relies on lifting heavy, this one uses two zones (in an upper/lower split) or three (in a full-body routine) with different **rep ranges** and loads.

**Strength emphasis zone**

- **Day 1:** relative strength zone (1-3 reps).
- **Day 2:** limit strength zone (4-6 reps).
- **Day 3**, if you use full body: functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).

**Functional hypertrophy emphasis zone**

- **Day 1:** limit strength zone (4-6 reps).
- **Day 2:** functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).
- **Day 3**, if you use full body: total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).

**Maximum hypertrophy emphasis zone**

- **Day 1:** functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).
- **Day 2:** total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).
- **Day 3**, if you use full body: strength-endurance (12-15 reps).

**Strength-endurance emphasis zone**

- **Day 1:** total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).
- **Day 2:** strength-endurance (12-15 reps).
- **Day 3**, if you use full body: endurance-strength (15-20 reps).

**Mixed or all-purpose training zone**

- **Day 1:** limit strength zone (4-6 reps).
- **Day 2:** total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).
- **Day 3**, if you use full body: endurance-strength (15-20 reps).

Volume and muscle-group split are the same as in strategy one. Loads are calculated from your **one-rep max (1RM)**.

### Methods that fit this strategy

**In an absolute or relative strength session**

- **Regular lifting** with 85-100% of your max (1-3 and 4-6 reps).
- **Clusters:** 87-92% of your max, sets of 5 reps with 10 seconds of rest between reps.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 4-6RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds of rest and a few more reps.
- **Alternating sets:** two exercises for different muscle groups with 60-90 seconds between each. They are not supersets.

**In a functional hypertrophy session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 6-8RM.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 4-6RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 4-6RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.

**In a total hypertrophy session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 8-12RM.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 8-12RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 6-8RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.

**In a strength-endurance or endurance-strength session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 12-15RM or 15-20RM.
- **Double drop set:** set of 8-12RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure, reduce it by another 25% and do a few more reps.
- **Pre-fatigue:** superset of an isolation exercise (8-12 reps) with a compound one (6-8 reps) for the same muscle group.
- **Post-fatigue:** superset of a compound exercise (6-8 reps) with an isolation one (8-12 reps) for the same muscle group.
- **Pre and post-fatigue:** superset of an isolation exercise (8-12), a compound one (6-8) and another isolation one (8-12) for the same muscle group.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- Very significant motor learning due to the **high frequency** of a movement pattern, with rapid strength gains.
- **Very efficient:** every exercise is basic, involves a lot of muscle mass and lets you train the whole body with few exercises. A big advantage when you have little time.
- It allows you to develop several physical capacities at the same time.

**Disadvantages**

- **It can lead to unbalanced development.** Compound movements leave some groups understimulated, because the body uses the best-adapted muscles to do the work.
- **Lower performance in some exercises.** After hard squats and hip movements, available energy drops and the following exercises suffer.
- The **high volume** of work delays recovery compared with method number one.

**Good or bad: high energy expenditure.** The cost is even higher than in the first strategy because of the greater volume, with more reps on some days. It is an advantage if you are trying to lose fat or have a slow metabolism, and a disadvantage if you are trying to gain a lot of weight or have a fast metabolism, though you can compensate by eating more.

### When to use this strategy

- If you are an athlete in an early phase trying to build a good base of size and strength.
- If you are at a relatively complete level of development.
- If you do not have much time to train.
- As a transition from a more conventional bodybuilding period.
- If you are a beginner, with an emphasis on maximum hypertrophy or strength-endurance.

## High volume and high frequency: the golden era method

The third strategy is based on how many bodybuilders of the **golden era** trained when they prepared for a competition. It is **high-volume** and high-frequency training. **Arnold** and his crew, **Serge Nubret** and **Mohammed Makkawy** are just a few examples.

Many champions trained every muscle group twice a week with a very high volume in each session. **Serge Nubret** used this split:

- **Day 1:** chest, quadriceps and abs.
- **Day 2:** back, hamstrings and abs.
- **Day 3:** shoulders, arms and abs.
- **Day 4:** rest.
- **Day 5:** repeat.

He performed sets of 12 to 15 reps, 6 to 8 sets of 4 to 6 exercises per muscle group, resting one minute between sets for the upper body and two minutes for the lower body. Biceps and triceps were specialised as supersets.

**Other possible splits**

- **Day 1:** chest, delts and triceps; **day 2:** quadriceps and hamstrings; **day 3:** back, biceps and rear delts; **day 4:** rest; **day 5:** repeat.
- **Day 1:** back and triceps; **day 2:** legs and shoulders; **day 3:** chest and biceps; **day 4:** rest; **day 5:** repeat.

These bodybuilders routinely did between 16 and 20 **sets** per muscle group, and sometimes more.

Many people would say that an average person could not get optimal results from this way of training. The results of hundreds of past **champions** cannot be dismissed.

It is true that some of them, perhaps all, used **drugs** to support their training. But all over the world tens of thousands of people use **steroids** without coming anywhere near the physique of the high-volume champions.

### Methods that fit this strategy

This strategy, with high frequency and high volume, already puts a tremendous amount of **tension** on the body. Only a few **natural** lifters will be able to follow it and grow properly.

Adding intensity techniques is not a great idea, because it will probably lead to **physical annihilation**. **Supersets** for two opposing muscles, such as biceps and triceps, are acceptable, but the **natural bodybuilder** should keep it as simple as possible.

### Advantages and disadvantages

This type of training induces a ton of microtrauma in the muscles.

- If you are a recovery machine who can tolerate a huge amount of physical work, or if you are chemically enhanced, it is an **advantage**: it will lead to a significant gain in mass.
- It lets you use a wide variety of exercises per muscle group, which minimises the risk of severe imbalance. It is an **advantage** if you can recover from the volume.
- If you are natural and have average genetics, like 90% of the population, it is a severe **disadvantage** that leads to rapid plateauing and even a reduction in gains.
- The superhuman volume means a large energy expenditure and a metabolic increase. It is an **advantage** if you want to lose fat and do not want to do aerobic training, and a **disadvantage** if you struggle to gain size.
- Because of the high volume per muscle group, it can easily lead to **tendinitis**, especially in the elbow joints, which are involved in almost every session. That is a clear disadvantage.

### When to use this strategy

The right question would be **who** should use this strategy. It is for the **genetic elite** using **steroids**.

However, a short phase of **very high volume** once or twice a year for four weeks is not such a bad idea, as long as you switch to a **low-volume** method afterwards. You induce a state of **over-reaching** (short-term overtraining) during those four weeks and then get an **anabolic** rebound when you switch to low volume, the so-called rebound effect.

## High volume and low frequency: once a week

In the fourth strategy, each muscle group is trained with a relatively high volume of work, 12 to 20 **sets** per group, but **only once a week**.

Sets are normally performed in the **total hypertrophy** zone (8-12 reps) or the **functional hypertrophy** zone (6-8 reps).

You do around 3-4 sets of 2 to 5 exercises per muscle group. The **large, complex muscle groups** (chest, back, quadriceps) get more exercises (4-5) than the smaller ones (shoulders, triceps, biceps, hamstrings, calves), which are trained with 2-3 exercises.

You use at least one **multi-joint movement** per muscle group, and two or three for the larger groups. Isolation exercises are chosen to focus on a specific weak point within the group.

### Training splits

**Split 1**

- **Day 1:** quadriceps, hamstrings and calves.
- **Day 2:** chest and back.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** shoulders and traps.
- **Day 5:** biceps and triceps.
- **Day 6:** abs and lower back.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Split 2**

- **Day 1:** chest, shoulders and triceps.
- **Day 2:** quadriceps and abs.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** back, biceps, traps and rear delts.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** hamstrings, lower back and calves.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Split 3**

- **Day 1:** quadriceps and chest.
- **Day 2:** back, lower back, hamstrings and calves.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** shoulders, traps and abs.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** biceps and triceps.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Split 4**

- **Day 1:** chest and biceps.
- **Day 2:** quadriceps, hamstrings and calves.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** back, lower back and traps.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** shoulders, triceps and abs.
- **Day 7:** rest.

### Methods that fit this strategy

- **Regular lifting** with your 6-8RM or 8-12RM.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 4-6RM (or 6-8RM); at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 4-6RM (or 6-8RM), reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.
- **Pre-fatigue:** superset of an isolation exercise (8-12 reps) with a compound one (6-8) for the same muscle group.
- **Post-fatigue:** superset of a compound exercise (6-8) with an isolation one (8-12) for the same muscle group.
- **Antagonist superset**, if you train two opposing groups in the same session: an exercise for one muscle (for example, chest) with one for its antagonist (for example, back), with sets of 6-8 or 8-12 reps.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- It lets you use a wide variety of exercises per muscle group, which minimises the risk of severe imbalance.
- It can create a lot of microtrauma in each session, an important training stimulus.
- The high volume leaves room to stimulate hypertrophy through cumulative fatigue and through growth hormone release induced by the rise in lactate.
- Training few muscle groups per session lets you give equally high quality effort to each one. In a full-body routine, the muscles trained last do not get the same effort as the first ones.

**Disadvantages**

- There are fewer **neuromuscular improvements** than with high-frequency training, because each muscle group is trained less often.
- Some people will not train with **maximum intensity** on every set because, unconsciously, they think they still have many sets to go.
- If a session is not productive for any reason, you have to wait a whole week to **stimulate** that muscle again.

### When to use this strategy

It is more appropriate for individuals who **have already built a decent muscular base** through basic training and have decided to focus their efforts on maximising their development.

If you train for **bodybuilding** or **aesthetic** gains, it is usually the best method.

## Moderate volume and frequency

In the fifth strategy, both **volume** and **frequency** are moderate, without being low.

This series of strategies is meant for advanced weight-training practitioners. It is a very common way to train in competitive athletes during their **muscle-gain** phases and also in powerlifters. Bodybuilders can use it too, especially in strength phases.

You train twice a week with a moderate volume, 6 to 12 sets, per muscle group or movement pattern. The focus is on **multi-joint exercises**, with the possible inclusion of some isolation work to prevent muscle imbalances and correct weak points.

Because each muscle group or movement pattern is trained twice a week, you can work different physical capacities in each session.

**Athletic training, early in the season**

- First session of the week: total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).
- Second session: strength-endurance (12-15 reps).

**Athletic training, main part of the season**

- First: absolute strength (4-6 reps).
- Second: power training (explosive lifting, ballistic and plyometric exercises).

**Athletic training, in season**

- First: power training (explosive lifting, ballistic exercises, but no plyometrics).
- Second: endurance-strength or restoration work (15-20+ reps).

**Powerlifting, first stage of preparation**

- First: absolute strength (4-6 reps).
- Second: functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).

**Powerlifting, limit strength emphasis**

- First: relative strength (1-3 reps).
- Second: absolute strength (4-6 reps).

**Powerlifting, strength-speed emphasis**

- First: relative strength (1-3 reps).
- Second: power training (explosive lifting, ballistic and plyometric exercises).

**Bodybuilding, strength emphasis**

- First: absolute strength (4-6 reps).
- Second: functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).

**Bodybuilding, hypertrophy emphasis**

- First: functional hypertrophy (6-8 reps).
- Second: total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).

**Bodybuilding, high volume emphasis**

- First: total hypertrophy (8-12 reps).
- Second: strength-endurance (12-15 reps).

### Training splits

**Athletic split, which also works for bodybuilding**

- **Day 1:** lower body.
- **Day 2:** upper body.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** lower body.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** upper body.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Powerlifting split**

- **Day 1:** squat and deadlift emphasis.
- **Day 2:** bench press emphasis.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** squat and deadlift emphasis.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** bench press emphasis.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Bodybuilding split**

- **Day 1:** quadriceps dominant.
- **Day 2:** upper body with horizontal push and pull exercises.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** hip dominant.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** upper body with vertical push and pull exercises.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Bodybuilding split 2**

- **Day 1:** lower body.
- **Day 2:** upper body with horizontal push and pull exercises.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** lower body.
- **Day 5:** rest.
- **Day 6:** upper body with vertical push and pull exercises.
- **Day 7:** rest.

### Methods that fit this strategy

**In a relative or absolute strength session**

- **Regular lifting** with 85-100% of your max (1-3 and 4-6 reps).
- **Clusters:** 87-92% of your max, sets of 5 reps with 10 seconds of rest between reps.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 4-6RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Alternating sets:** two exercises for different muscle groups with 60-90 seconds between each. They are not supersets.

**In a functional hypertrophy session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 6-8RM.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 4-6RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 4-6RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.

**In a total hypertrophy session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 8-12RM.
- **Rest-pause:** set of 8-12RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds and a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 6-8RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.

**In a strength-endurance or endurance-strength session**

- **Regular lifting** with your 12-15RM or 15-20RM.
- **Double drop set:** set of 8-12RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure, reduce it by another 25% and do a few more reps.
- **Pre-fatigue:** superset of an isolation exercise (8-12 reps) with a compound one (6-8) for the same muscle group.
- **Post-fatigue:** superset of a compound exercise (6-8) with an isolation one (8-12) for the same muscle group.
- **Pre and post-fatigue:** superset of an isolation exercise (8-12), a compound one (6-8) and another isolation one (8-12) for the same muscle group.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- Although it is not as effective for improving the central nervous system as frequent training, it has a significant neural component.
- It is useful for training several different capacities during the same cycle.
- Fewer muscle groups are trained per session than in a full-body routine, so each group gets better concentration and effort.
- By training each group twice a week, you do not need as much volume as if you trained it once a week. The increase in frequency compensates for the lower amount of work per session.
- If you do not perform well in one session, you still have a second one in the same week that targets the same muscle group.

**Good and bad**

- You can concentrate more on each muscle group or movement pattern than with a full-body strategy, but infrequent training, with each group only once a week, is still superior in this respect. It is a disadvantage compared with infrequent training and an advantage compared with full body.

### When to use this strategy

Basically, it is a good training system. It is not at the top in terms of **benefits**, but it is not at the bottom either.

For most people who always train doing the same things, this strategy can be close to optimal in many circumstances.

## Low volume, low frequency and high intensity

In the sixth strategy, both **volume** and **frequency** are **low**, with **high intensity**. A clear example is **Heavy Duty**.

It is the type of training adopted by **Dorian Yates**, Lee Labrada, David Henry and Marc Dugdale. It is a variant of the HIT system that keeps low volume and high intensity but reduces the frequency per body part: once or twice a week instead of three times, as in traditional HIT programmes.

Volume per muscle group stays very low. Normally you use 1 or 2 work sets of 1-4 exercises per muscle group, with a total of 2-8 sets per body part.

Work **sets** are normally taken **beyond failure** with high-intensity techniques such as rest-pause, drop sets, forced reps, negatives, statics and partial reps. The key is to induce the maximum amount of muscle microtrauma in those few sets and then let the body grow.

### Training splits

**Yates split**

- **Day 1:** shoulders, traps, triceps and abs.
- **Day 2:** back, rear delts and lower back.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** chest, biceps and abs.
- **Day 5:** quadriceps, hamstrings and calves.
- **Day 6:** rest.
- **Day 7:** rest.

**Dugdale split**

- **Day 1:** chest, biceps and calves.
- **Day 2:** quadriceps and abs.
- **Day 3:** rest.
- **Day 4:** back, hamstrings and abs.
- **Day 5:** delts, triceps and calves.
- **Day 6:** rest.
- **Day 7:** rest.

### Methods that fit this strategy

- **Rest-pause:** set of 8-12RM; when you reach failure, rest 10-12 seconds and perform a few more reps.
- **Double rest-pause:** set of 6-8RM; at failure, 10-12 seconds, a few more reps, another 10-12 seconds and a few more reps again.
- **Drop set:** set of 6-8RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure.
- **Double drop set:** set of 8-12RM, reduce the load by 25% and perform reps to failure, reduce it by another 25% and do a few more reps.
- **Forced reps:** set of 6-8RM (or 8-12RM); at failure, your partner helps you complete 2-3 more reps.
- **Negatives:** set of 6-8RM (or 8-12RM); at failure, perform 2-3 **negative or eccentric** reps slowly, over 5-6 seconds, while your partner helps on the **positive or concentric** part.
- **Statics:** set of 6-8RM (or 8-12RM); at failure, hold the weight at mid-range or at peak contraction, depending on the movement, for as long as you can.
- **Partial reps:** set of 6-8RM (or 8-12RM); at failure, continue with partial reps, without the full range of motion, until failure.

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

- It has a very good **work-to-recovery** ratio, which makes it very effective for anyone with a low recovery capacity.
- It brings a **do-or-die** attitude when used for a relatively long time. That reinforces any training task you have to perform and, used well, can make you train harder in the gym.
- Because of the low volume of work there is little accumulation of **systemic fatigue**, and that lets you give maximum effort on every exercise.

**Disadvantages**

- It requires a very **intense** mental stimulus. Since you have to cause as much microtrauma as possible, you cannot afford to waste a set with less than optimal effort.
- If you do not **push** hard enough in a session, you will have to wait a week to stimulate that group again. On top of that, the low volume can turn into undertraining if the intensity level is not appropriate.
- Because every set is taken to very deep levels of effort, it can be hard on the central nervous system, joints and tendons over time. Listen to your body and take rest when you need it.

**Good and bad: low metabolic demand.** Compared with high-volume training, this strategy asks less of your metabolism. It is an advantage for someone with a fast metabolism who struggles to gain size, and a disadvantage for someone with a slow metabolism.

### When to use this strategy

This way of training works especially well when alternated with phases of relatively high **volume**, strategies four and five. Used that way, it lets the body keep gaining while it builds a strong mental attitude that improves the following phases.

It also fits people with a fast metabolism. Because of the nature of the programme, it is convenient for hyperactive individuals who can give a **superhuman effort** in a short period but get bored easily in the gym.

## Conclusion: how to choose a strategy

This is not a complete guide to every available **training** system, but most of them can be placed within one of these categories. The most important point is that these systems work as long as three elements are present:

- **Progression:** challenge yourself and try to improve in every session.
- **Effort.**
- **Dedication.**

Each of these **training strategies** has its pros and cons, which make it more suitable for some situations and goals. Now that you know their strengths and weaknesses, it will be easier to choose the one that suits you at any given moment.
