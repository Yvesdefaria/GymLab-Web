The supplement market sells energy, immunity and recovery in capsules. Real nutrition is less glamorous: small amounts, varied sources and a blood test when something does not add up. This review sorts out the references and the gaps that actually show up in practice.

## The reference intakes, without megadoses

A healthy adult needs small amounts of each micronutrient. The daily reference for vitamin C is around 80 milligrams, for zinc around 10, and for vitamin D 10 to 15 micrograms. Taking ten times more does not improve performance.

The body absorbs a limited amount of each nutrient per meal. Water-soluble vitamins that are not used leave in urine; vitamins A and D, plus iron, accumulate. That difference explains why megadoses are not harmless.

## Vitamin D: the one usually missing in winter

Skin makes vitamin D from sunlight. In autumn and winter, with fewer daylight hours and more clothing, production falls and food provides little: oily fish, egg and fortified dairy. A blood test decides whether to supplement and how much.

The reference of 10 to 15 micrograms a day, between 400 and 600 units, covers most adults. Higher doses only with a test that justifies them.

## Iron: only with a measured deficiency

Low iron without anaemia reduces performance, and the shortage is more common in menstruating women and vegetarian diets. Even so, supplementing blindly is a mistake: the excess accumulates and can damage the liver.

Ferritin and haemoglobin are the tests that settle it. With a deficiency, the fix is food plus a prescribed supplement; without one, red meat, legumes and leafy greens cover the need. The guide to [legumes in the diet](/en/legumes-in-your-diet/) shows plant sources and how to improve their absorption.

## Calcium, magnesium and potassium

Calcium supports bone and takes part in muscle contraction. Two or three dairy servings, sardines with bones or tofu get close to a thousand milligrams a day. Anyone avoiding dairy needs a fortified alternative.

Magnesium is in nuts, legumes, cocoa and whole grains. The reference is around 300 to 350 milligrams. Magnesium cramps are less common than advertising suggests; sodium and potassium explain more cases.

Potassium appears in potato, banana, legumes and vegetables. A diet low in vegetables and high in salt tends to invert the ratio: too much sodium and too little potassium. In long hot sessions, the [homemade sports drink](/en/homemade-sports-drink/) replaces sodium and some carbohydrate without a commercial product.

## B vitamins and vitamin C

B vitamins take part in energy metabolism. Anyone training hard and eating little is at more risk of falling short, especially in long deficits. The answer lies in whole grains, meat, egg, legumes and leafy greens.

Vitamin C is covered by a pepper, a kiwi or an orange. Doses of 500 milligrams or more have not prevented colds in the general population.

## Beta-carotene: the exception for smokers

Carotenoids from fruit and vegetables are safe. High-dose beta-carotene supplements are associated with more lung cancer in smokers, so that group avoids the supplement and gets carotenoids from food. Food sources carry several carotenoids at once, and that mix is the point.

## Food first, supplement for the gap

A multivitamin covers the gaps of a messy diet, but it does not replace the fibre, protein or phytochemicals in food. If the diet is varied, the complex adds little.

Supplements with iron, vitamin D or B12 only make sense when a test exists or the diet does not cover the nutrient. The guide to [sports supplementation](/en/sports-supplementation-guide/) orders the priorities, the one on [dietary supplements](/en/best-supplements-for-the-gym/) explains what each product is, and the one on [vitamins and the immune system](/en/immune-system-vitamins/) reviews defences. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) avoid the purchases that add nothing to the plan.

## When to review the diet

Persistent tiredness, repeated cramps, brittle nails or hair loss are reasons to review food intake and seek advice. None of them is diagnosed with a jar of vitamins, and none improves with a dose that does not fix the cause.
