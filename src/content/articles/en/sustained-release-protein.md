There is no sustained-release protein that is different from the rest. There are faster and slower sources, and a product that combines them. The ladder can be ordered by how long the amino acid peak takes to arrive in blood and how long it lasts.

## What sustained release means

A sustained-release product aims to keep amino acids in blood for longer. It does that by combining a fast source with a slow one, or by adding egg protein and casein. The result is a flatter curve, not a larger amount of protein.

Protein synthesis does not respond to that curve in a straight line. After a meal, the response rises and then falls even while amino acids remain in blood. Spreading protein across 25 to 40 gram servings during the day does more for synthesis than chasing a perfect curve in one shake. In practice there is no need to measure the curve: what matters is whether the daily total arrives and whether tolerance is good.

## Blending is convenience, not physiology

A whey and casein blend is reproduced with two tubs or with a whey shake and a meal containing cottage cheese. Added enzymes do not change digestion decisively; the body already has its own. The real advantage of a blend is having one product that works at any hour.

That has practical value if you travel, if you do not want to decide which tub to open, or if the budget allows only one. It has no magic value. Two tubs and a scoop measure reproduce that convenience at home, usually for less money than a special formula. The page on [whey protein](/en/whey-protein-production/) explains the fast source, and the one on [casein](/en/casein-protein/) the slow one.

## Isolate is not superior to casein on its own

The idea that isolate builds more muscle than casein often rests on comparisons with different doses or with blends that do not match leucine. When protein and leucine are matched, the hypertrophy difference shrinks to little or nothing. Isolate has more protein per gram and less lactose; that is a format advantage.

The same applies to milk protein concentrate. It contains whey and casein and its speed falls between the two, but it is not the superior supplement. It is a convenient ingredient, with lactose and fat that vary by batch. No serious table crowns it above the rest.

## When a blend makes sense

It makes sense when you want a single protein for the whole day, when the price per 25 grams is good, or when digestion improves with a mixed source. It makes no sense if you already have whey and casein at home, or if food covers the total.

The dose is the same as with any other protein: 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day, in servings of 25 to 40 grams. A blend does not allow you to lower that number or replace a full meal. For a mass plan, the guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) organises protein with calories and training.

## How to read a blend label

Look at the ingredient order, protein per 100 grams, leucine per serving and lactose. A product with whey as the first ingredient will be faster than one with casein first. Price per 25 grams of protein compares better than the price of the tub.

The guide to [protein supplements](/en/protein-supplements/) compares types and doses, the one on [buying protein powder](/en/protein-powder-guide/) shows how to run that sum, and the one on [protein pros and cons](/en/protein-pros-and-cons/) places each type on the ladder. If saving is the goal, two simple tubs usually cost less than a sustained-release formula.
