A rep has two phases. In the positive or concentric phase you raise the weight and the muscle shortens. In the negative or eccentric phase you lower the weight against gravity and the muscle stretches. The negative rep focuses on that second phase.

## What a negative rep is

In a negative rep, the lifter lowers the weight unaided to the starting point, fighting gravity and slowing the descent until the phase lasts four to five seconds. At the bottom, one or more partners help return the weight to the starting point so the next rep can begin.

The original article notes that a person can use 10% to 30% more weight in the negative phase than in the positive. That margin explains why the technique is so demanding.

The difference from [forced reps](/en/forced-reps/) is where the help arrives. In forced reps, the partner helps on the way up to get past failure. In negative reps, the partner does the lifting and you control the lowering.

Nor is it a [partial rep](/en/partial-reps/), which shortens the range, or a [cheat rep](/en/cheat-reps/), which gains momentum from the body: here the range is full and the help comes on the way up.

## How much weight to use

Take 10% to 30% more weight than in the positive phase as a reference. In practice, choose a load you can lower in four or five seconds without it getting away from you, and that your partner can return safely.

The weight is chosen for the lowering, not the lifting. If the negative phase speeds up or your partner has to make an extra effort to return it, lower the load.

With partner methods, one person may not be enough. The original article mentions one, two or even more helpers depending on the load.

In method 1, the positive phase should not cost you any effort: the partner does it.

## Types of negative reps

There are two main methods and one variant that is not a true negative.

**Method 1: negatives only.** It is the least common and the most dangerous. It needs a thorough warm-up with several positive sets, sometimes up to six. You do low reps, always fewer than ten, and the partner lifts the weight. The positive phase involves no effort. It is reserved for advanced lifters and for those chasing neural strength.

**Method 2: negatives after forced reps.** You reach [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/), do two or three forced reps with help and continue with two or three negatives fighting gravity. It is the usual method for working a large muscle.

### The variant that is not a negative rep

The original article describes a third method: holding the negative phase for longer. It does not quite count as a negative rep, because the lift is not assisted and the weight does not exceed the positive phase. It is slow eccentric work, safer on isolation exercises and it needs no help on the way up. We include it as a variant, not as a third type.

## How to do them with a partner

In method 1, the partner lifts the weight and you lower it. In method 2, the partner helps on the forced reps and then lets you lower it alone. In both, the help has to be just right: too much and you are not training; too little and the rep does not happen.

One, two or more partners may be needed depending on the load. For a heavy barbell, two people, one on each side, share the weight and reduce the risk.

Without a partner, methods 1 and 2 cannot be done safely. The slow eccentric variant can, because it needs no help on the way up.

Pick a partner who can return the weight and listen to your signal. This is not the moment to try it with someone who has never done the technique.

## When to use them and how many sets

Like any high-intensity technique, it should not be used indiscriminately. Use it at specific moments, to add variety and work the fibres differently.

One example: one week a month you can use negative reps as your high-intensity technique.

If you do a chest routine with incline bench press, flat dumbbell press, high-cable flyes and incline dumbbell flyes, the recommendation is to apply method 2 to the compounds, with a maximum of two sets per exercise. That would be four high-intensity sets: two on the incline press and two on the dumbbell press. The rest, at normal intensity.

You can also use the slow variant on isolation exercises, slowing the lowering for several seconds.

Method 1 is left for the most advanced lifters and for those seeking a neural strength increase.

This page's limit is two sets per exercise. It is the ceiling the article proposes to avoid piling up too much high-intensity work.

## Risks

Method 1 is the most dangerous. It demands a thorough warm-up and a load your partner can control. If you do not have that help, do not attempt it.

Methods 1 and 2 need a partner. Without one, the negative turns into an uncontrolled lowering or an impossible lift.

Negative reps leave the muscle beaten up. Allow recovery time before working it again.

If you feel sharp pain in a joint or a pull, stop. The technique is not worth an injury.

Do not use them in every session or on every exercise. Keep method 1 for very specific moments and method 2 for compounds you can control.

To understand the role of the eccentric phase, see [muscle contractions](/en/muscle-contractions/). [High-intensity training](/en/high-intensity-training/) places this technique alongside the others and explains when to take a step back.
