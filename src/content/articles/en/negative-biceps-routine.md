A biceps routine needs exercises that stimulate the muscle, not a round of half-hearted curls. The four movements below cover a barbell, a cable, a preacher bench and a neutral grip, and they share the same protocol.

## What a negative is

Every rep has two phases. The concentric phase lifts the weight, when the muscle shortens. The eccentric phase lowers it, when the muscle lengthens while holding the load.

The negative is the eccentric phase. The biceps can brake more weight than it can lift, so a longer lowering adds tension without adding kilos. The simplest way to apply the principle is this: lift in about three counts and lower in four to six.

## The safety rule

A slow negative is controlled from the start. The weight you lower has to be stoppable halfway down, and you check that on the first rep of the first set.

If the weight runs away, take a plate off. Never release the bar at the end of the range or drop the dumbbell: the brake is the whole exercise.

On the barbell curl and the preacher curl, a helping hand on the way up lets you work with more load on the way down. On the cable and with dumbbells, a controlled lowering is enough.

## The routine in detail

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barbell curl | 4 | 6 to 10 |
| Preacher curl | 3 | 10 to 12 |
| Hammer-grip curl | 3 | 10 to 12 |
| One-arm cable curl | 3 | 12 to 15 |

> **\*** Every set goes to failure and follows the same rhythm: three seconds up, four to six down.

## Barbell curl

It is the main exercise of the routine and the one that takes the most weight. With the bar at shoulder width, the knees slightly bent and the torso upright, lift until the hands reach shoulder height.

The elbows stay at the sides and do not drift forward. If the torso leans back to push the bar, the negative stops making sense because the biceps is no longer braking the weight.

## Preacher curl

No multi-joint movement isolates the biceps completely. The squat works glutes, hamstrings and back; the bench press works the triceps; barbell and cable curls use the shoulder too. The preacher bench reduces that help.

Set the bench so the top of the pad sits at the middle of your sternum and the arms rest flat. Lift until the biceps contracts fully and lower slowly to the start. The lowering is longer here and worth counting.

## Hammer-grip curl

The arms gain structure when the brachialis is developed. It is the short, flat muscle that forms on the upper forearm and ends under the biceps, and a neutral grip loads it more than a curl with the palms up.

Stand with a dumbbell in each hand and the palms facing the hips, then lift alternating arms in a semicircle towards the shoulder. The elbow stays pinned to the side, the torso upright and the palm facing in through the whole range. Squeeze at the top and lower slowly.

## One-arm cable curl

Bars and dumbbells share one problem: gravity pulls in a single direction, so tension drops at the top and the bottom of the range. A cable keeps resistance through the whole arc, which allows a longer, more even negative.

Hold the handle of a low pulley with one hand and stand far enough away that the weight stacks slightly above the plates, with the arm extended. Lift while turning the wrist until the biceps touches the forearm, keep the elbow close to the body and lower slowly. Repeat with the other arm.

It is the highest-rep exercise of the routine and the best way to close the session, because it builds fatigue without demanding the technique of the bar.

## How to progress

Add weight when you complete the top of the rep range on every set without losing control of the lowering. If the negative shortens or the bar drops on the last rep, the weight is still too high even when the rep count says otherwise.

Swap an exercise for its variant when the plan stalls: one-arm dumbbell preacher curl instead of the barbell version, or a cable curl instead of the hammer curl. The stimulus changes and the elbow gets a rest.
