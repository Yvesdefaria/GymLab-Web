The forearm works on nearly everything you grab. Every row, every pull-up and every deadlift asks for grip strength, and still almost nobody gives it direct work. There are two reasons to add a short block: grip becomes the limit on back work as the load rises, and the wrist flexors and extensors respond to their own stimulus.

The routine below is a finisher. Do it after back or at the end of a biceps day, it takes about ten minutes and it needs nothing beyond a barbell and two dumbbells.

## How the session is built

Start with a classic, heavy movement for the flexors: the barbell wrist curl behind the back, three sets of 10 to 15 reps through a full range.

Then come the two grip exercises, joined in a superset. The hammer-style wrist curl and the reverse-grip wrist curl run back to back with no rest between them, using dumbbells heavy enough to reach failure between 12 and 20 reps. On the reverse grip you may need to drop the weight: the wrist pushes with less force in that position.

Range comes first. Lower the weight before you shorten the range, and do not cut reps to finish sooner.

## Barbell wrist curl behind the back

Stand with a barbell behind you, just under the glutes, palms facing back. Move only the wrists: lift the bar as high as you can and lower it until the joint is fully extended. Let the bar roll down the fingers without losing control at any point.

It is the heaviest of the three exercises. Even so, the load should not force the elbows to move or the torso to lean.

## Hammer-style wrist curl

Grab two dumbbells and kneel in front of a flat bench, forearms resting on top and palms facing each other. The wrist hangs over the edge of the bench.

Lift and lower the dumbbells moving only the wrist, with the elbows still. The range is short and worth using in full: the easy mistake is bouncing through the top half.

## Reverse-grip wrist curl

Same position as the previous exercise with one change: the palms face down for the whole movement. Lift both dumbbells together and keep them level.

Here the extensors work, and they are usually weaker than the flexors. That is why the weight drops and the technique gets more demanding. If the wrist drifts to one side, the weight is still too high.

## The routine in one table

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barbell wrist curl behind the back | 3 | 10 to 15 |
| Hammer-style wrist curl | 2 | 12 to 20 |
| Reverse-grip wrist curl | 2 | 12 to 20 |

> **\*** The second and third exercises run as a superset: two sets of each with no rest between them, and all five sets of the routine go to failure.

## When to place it

After back is the best slot: the forearm is already warm from the rows and pulldowns, and the direct work does not compete with any big lift. It also fits at the end of a biceps day, before you leave.

What does not work is putting it before a back day or a deadlift day. A tired forearm lets go of the bar before the back finishes the set, and the training loses the part that matters most.

If you train grip separately, leave at least 48 hours between that session and this one. The wrist tendons accumulate load easily and need more time than the muscle to recover.

## What to expect from direct work

The forearm grows slowly, like the calf. The advantage is that almost anyone can train it without complicated equipment, and the improvement shows up in grip before it shows in the mirror: the bar stops slipping, pull-ups hold more reps and the deadlift stops escaping your hands.
