It is an anti-extension isometric: the trunk resists the tendency to arch while the weight pushes down. The rectus abdominis, the obliques and the transversus work together to hold the position, and the load is measured in time, not reps.

## What the weighted plank trains

The plate on the glutes or on the lower back lowers the centre of gravity and raises the demand on the whole abdominal wall. The rectus brakes extension of the spine, the obliques control lateral tilt and the transversus keeps the pressure inside the abdomen.

The difference from the unweighted plank is magnitude. The position is the same and so is the technique, but the margin for error narrows: with ten kilos on top, a hip that drops shows up earlier and a lower back that arches appears on the first rep.

## Before you add weight

Hold the unweighted plank with clean technique for a full minute. Hips in line, glutes tight, head neutral and breathing calm.

If the lower back arches or the hip sinks at thirty seconds, it is not time to load yet. Adding kilos to a position that already breaks only buys more time in bad posture.

## Starting position

Lie face down with the body straight and the forearms on the floor, elbows under the shoulders.

Ask someone to place a 10, 15 or 20 kilo plate on your glutes or lower back, centred and flat. The weight should not sit on the hip bone or on the floating ribs.

Lift the hips off the floor until the body is supported on the forearms and the toes. The back stays flat for the whole set.

## Execution

Keep the abs drawn in and tight. As you breathe out, pull the navel towards the spine as if you wanted to press it against the lower back, and hold that tension while you breathe.

The glutes help: squeeze them so the pelvis does not drop. The shoulders stay away from the ears and the neck follows the line of the spine.

The set lasts as long as the position holds. When the hip drops, the lower back arches or the shoulders climb, the set is over even if there are seconds left on the clock.

## Sets and progression

Sixty seconds is the reference target. Three sets of one minute cover the dose and leave room for the rest of the session.

When the minute comes out easy, add 10 or 15 second blocks. When a minute and a half also comes out easy, add weight before more time: load is measured precisely, and a long stopwatch only teaches you to endure.

The sensible progression: a stable position, then more time, then more plate and finally less support. If you go back to bodyweight after a break, start again from the clean minute.

## Training alone: the backpack

Placing a plate on your back without help is awkward and unsafe. A backpack loaded with plates or water bottles solves it: it sits on the glutes, the straps adjust it and you take it off without turning the trunk.

Start with five kilos and build slowly. The weight is distributed differently from a plate, and the first set is there to check it.

## When load beats time

Past a minute, stretching the set trains tolerance to the position more than abdominal strength. Load keeps the stimulus inside a short, measurable window and lets you compare sessions with a clear number.

> **\*** A badly placed plate, or a set stretched out with the hips sagging, turns this exercise into a bad idea. Execution outranks weight.

## Where it fits

It is a loading exercise for the abs, not a warm-up. Put it after the dynamic movements and before the accessories, or at the end of an abs session that already includes flexion and rotation.
