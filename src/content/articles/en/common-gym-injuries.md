> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. If the pain is sharp, appears suddenly or comes with a loss of strength, get advice before you keep training.

You go to the gym to improve your health and your physique, and you end up collecting niggles in the same joint. The stab in your shoulder or the twinge in your lower back points to something in the routine that does not fit.

## Why gym injuries happen

Most injuries appear when load exceeds what the muscle, tendon or ligament can tolerate at that moment. The specific exercise matters less than the dose. There are three usual routes to that point:

- **Too much volume or intensity too soon.** Going from no training to six days a week close to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/).
- **Poor technique sustained over time.** Lifting with compensations that raw strength covers for months.
- **Poor recovery.** Sleeping five hours, eating little and expecting the body to repair yesterday's damage.

Prevention starts with two modest decisions: managing your ego and managing [training volume](/en/training-volume/). You are neither made of glass nor made of titanium.

## Shoulder, lower back and knee: the three usual areas

Draw a pain map in any weights room and three areas light up above the rest. Each one has its mechanism and its fix.

### Shoulder: bench press, flared elbows and rotator cuff

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body and, for that reason, the least stable. Pain at the front or the side usually comes from a specific mix: too much bench press, elbows flared to 90 degrees and little pulling work to balance it.

Two corrections cover much of the problem. Retract the scapulae, pulling the shoulder blades together, every time you press. Keep the elbows at about 45 degrees from the torso. Add rows and pull-ups to the plan to even out the pressing.

> **Note:** If supraspinatus tendinopathy is already there, removing all load atrophies the tendon. Current evidence prioritises isometric analgesia in the early stages, with static pushes against a wall, then controlled eccentric work to reorganise the collagen fibres.

### Lower back: the unfounded fear of the deadlift

Low back pain is the king of complaints. The back improves when you move it and make it stronger. The deadlift and the barbell row build a back, and they turn dangerous when you lose spinal neutrality under heavy loads.

The fault usually sits in two details. Bracing is missing, that diaphragmatic breath with abdominal tension that stabilises the trunk. And the hip hinge is missing: if you feel the weight in your lower back and not in your hamstrings and glutes, you are lifting with the wrong structure. Before you add kilos, check [how much weight to lift](/en/how-much-weight-to-lift/).

### Knee: squat, bounce and valgus

Pain at the front of the knee, in the patellar tendon, shows up when you overload the frequency of knee-dominant exercises with heavy loads and uncontrolled descents. Bouncing at the bottom of a heavy squat multiplies shear force on the joint.

Control the eccentric phase, the descent. Watch that the knees do not collapse inwards on the way up; that valgus spreads the load badly. Lowering the weight and slowing the descent fixes more cases than changing the exercise.

> **Note:** Patellar tendinopathy is often managed with the heavy slow resistance protocol. It uses loads of 70% to 85% of 1RM on exercises such as the leg press, with tempos of 3 to 4 seconds down and 3 to 4 seconds up, about three times a week.

## What to do when it already hurts

Instinct says that, if something hurts, you stay on the sofa until it goes away. That is the worst advice for the musculoskeletal system. Absolute rest weakens tissue: an immobilised tendon loses load capacity within days. When you come back after three weeks off, the tissue is weaker and you try to lift the same weight. The result is a relapse.

What works is relative rest, also called active rest. You modify training to dodge the pain and keep the surrounding tissue active:

- If the shoulder hurts on the military press, try an incline dumbbell press or a shorter range.
- If the knee hurts in a deep squat, try a box squat or an isometric leg press.
- If the deadlift hurts, switch to a variation with less range and less load.

The key is finding a painless stimulus, or one with a very mild ache that fades fast. That stimulus still sends blood and nutrients to the area.

> **Note:** A common traffic-light rule in rehabilitation uses the 0 to 10 pain scale. Pain from 0 to 3 during the session is green, provided it returns to 0 within 24 hours. If it goes past 4 or gets worse the next day, it is red: you overshot the load and need to step back.

## How to return to training without a relapse

The move from the treatment table to the squat rack has to be gradual. You will not recover a lost month in a week, and the [progressive overload principle](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) rules here more than anywhere else.

Start with less volume, less intensity and a range of motion you tolerate. Increase one variable per week. If you add weight, keep the sets. If you add sets, keep the weight. When pain shows up during the session and disappears within a day, you have found the pace. When it stays with you for two days, you went too fast.

If the pain does not improve in one or two weeks, limits your sleep or returns every few weeks, ask for an assessment. The guide on [when to see a sports doctor](/en/when-to-see-a-sports-doctor/) explains when the appointment is worth it, and the one on [chronic pain and physiotherapy](/en/chronic-pain-physiotherapy/) covers the case where the ache has been there for months.

## Frequently asked questions about gym injuries

**Should I train with severe soreness?** Intense [delayed onset muscle soreness](/en/delayed-onset-muscle-soreness/) points to recent muscle damage. Active rest works better, with a walk or easy cycling, or training another muscle group. Insisting on the same muscle delays recovery.

**Does the deadlift injure the back?** It is one of the best exercises for building a strong back. Risk appears when you load 100 kilos without mastering technique or knowing how to brace; that is fixed with load and practice.

**When should I use ice?** Current evidence does not support routine ice for recovery after a session: inflammation is part of the repair process. After an acute trauma, follow a professional's advice.

**Is joint pain normal when lifting weights?** Never. Burning, fatigue and congestion in the muscle are the goal. Sharp pain or a pinch inside a joint is a stop signal.

**Do knee sleeves and belts prevent injuries?** They give support, joint warmth and intra-abdominal pressure, and they improve performance. They will not save you from disastrous technique or an irrational progression.

Treat training as a long race. Technique and patience take you further than five extra kilos on today's bench press.
