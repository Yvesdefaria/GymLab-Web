The body loses water through urine, sweat, breathing and stools, and replaces it with what you drink and what you eat. When the two accounts stay close, performance holds. When they drift apart, strength drops before intense thirst appears.

## Hydration for training: how much water you need a day

A reasonable reference for an adult who trains is 2 to 3 litres of fluid a day, counting the water in food. Fruit, vegetables, soup and dairy bring a significant share, so the bottle does not have to cover everything.

Sweat is the variable that changes the total. An easy session in winter may cost half a litre; a long session in summer may pass two litres. That is why no fixed figure works for every day of the year.

## Three signals that work

Thirst is the first warning and is usually enough in short sessions. In long sessions or in high heat it arrives late, so it is worth drinking before you feel it.

Urine colour is a cheap check. Pale yellow suggests a reasonable state; a dark colour suggests fluid is missing. The first urine of the morning is the most concentrated and does not serve as a reference for the day.

Body weight before and after training measures what you actually lost. A kilo of weight lost equals roughly a litre of sweat. If the difference passes 2 % of body weight, the session ran at a deficit and needs replacing.

## Before, during and after

Before training, a glass or two of water is enough if urine is pale. During the session, small sips when thirst appears work better than a strict schedule: the stomach tolerates volume better when it is spread out.

Afterwards, food and drink in the following hour cover most of the replacement. If you sweated a lot, drinking a little more than thirst asks and adding salt to the meal speeds recovery.

## When a sports drink makes sense

Below 60 or 90 minutes, water covers the need. The glycogen and electrolytes from your previous meal are still available, and a sugary drink only adds calories.

Past that duration, a drink with carbohydrates and sodium keeps glucose stable and helps you drink more. Concentration matters: above 6 % carbohydrates, gastric emptying slows and stomach discomfort appears. The guide to [training in hot weather](/en/training-in-hot-weather/) explains how the session changes when the thermometer climbs.

## The ceiling: more than 5 or 6 litres is a risk

Drinking in excess does not improve performance. Above 5 or 6 litres a day, and especially when drinking without thirst during a long effort, sodium in blood is diluted and hyponatraemia appears. Symptoms run from nausea and headache to confusion and seizures, and it is a medical emergency.

The risk is higher in events of several hours, in endurance sports and in anyone who drinks out of obligation. The safe strategy is to replace what was lost, not to exceed it. The page on [the week before competing](/en/pre-competition-week/) insists on the same point: manipulating water and sodium to change weight is a bad idea.

## Coffee, tea, milk and soft drinks

Coffee and tea hydrate. The idea that they dehydrate comes from studies with high doses of isolated caffeine, and it does not hold with two or three cups a day. They count towards total fluid like any other drink.

Milk rehydrates well thanks to its sodium and protein, and it is useful after training. It does not burn fat or speed up metabolism, even if it is a good option for its protein. Sugary soft drinks and alcohol are the drinks that help least: they add calories, and alcohol worsens recovery.

Creatine does not require drinking more than usual, and its link to cramps is weaker than it is repeated. The guide to [creatine and cramps](/en/creatine-and-cramps/) separates what the evidence supports from locker-room folklore.

## How to set it up in practice

A large bottle on the desk, another in the bag and a glass with each meal cover the base without thinking. In summer, adding a pinch of salt to food and drinking before heading out lowers the risk of reaching the second half of the session dry.

If the goal is to lose fat, water helps with satiety but does not slim on its own.
