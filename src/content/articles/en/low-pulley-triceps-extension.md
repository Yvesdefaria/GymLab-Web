It is an isolation exercise suitable for beginners and advanced lifters. The difficulty lies in keeping the torso still and the elbows in a position that does not turn the extension into a shoulder movement; moving the rope is the easy part.

## How to do the low-pulley triceps extension

Attach a rope to the low pulley and select a weight that lets you complete the range without moving your body. Stand with your back to the machine.

Grab the rope behind your neck with a neutral grip, palms facing each other. Keep your feet shoulder-width apart and your forearms parallel to the floor. Your elbows point at the ceiling.

Extend the rope upwards, moving only your forearms, until your arms are fully straight. As you rise, spread the rope until your hands reach shoulder height. Pause briefly and return slowly to the start, letting the hands come back together.

## The elbows go in front, not behind

The most common position mistake is pulling the elbows back to stretch further. At that point the humerus sits behind the line of the torso and the shoulder hyperextends. The triceps gains no range; the shoulder is the one doing extra work.

The correct reference is an elbow slightly in front of the body line, with the hands travelling behind the head. From there the extension is felt in the triceps and the lower back does not need to arch to follow the movement.

## The long head and the stretch

The three heads of the triceps share a single distal tendon and contract together in every elbow extension. Arm position changes the lever arm, the stretch and the point of the range that feels hardest. No position isolates a head.

The long head is the only one that crosses the shoulder, so with the arm overhead it is more stretched than in a pushdown. That stretch is why overhead extensions feel different, not proof that they work the muscle more. The [triceps long head](/en/triceps-long-head/) and [triceps anatomy](/en/triceps-anatomy/) guides cover the detail.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking the elbows behind the torso.** It is shoulder hyperextension and it takes tension off the triceps.
- **Arching the lower back.** If the back arches to lift the rope, the weight is too high.
- **Leaning forward.** The torso stays upright and still; only the forearms move.
- **Pulling with the shoulders.** The movement starts at the elbow, not the deltoid.
- **Letting the rope snap back.** The eccentric phase is controlled just like the lift.
- **Cutting the spread at the top.** The hands reach shoulder height; stopping short leaves the end of the range undone.

## The cable does not protect the elbow by itself

The cable changes the type of demand. It removes the balance work a dumbbell requires and fixes the path, which makes adding load easier. That convenience does not make the exercise harmless: with a heavy weight and the elbows behind you, the elbow takes a load that technique does not justify.

Protection comes from the load and the position, not the equipment. The [range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) guide helps you decide how much range the rep needs and when to shorten it.

## Sets, reps and rest

This page recommends 3 to 4 sets of 8 to 12 reps, with 60 to 90 seconds of rest between sets. Add load when the last set comes out clean, with the elbows in place and the torso not arching.

The cable allows a one or two second squeeze at the top without moving the elbow, so the set can end with control instead of chasing failure. The [triceps routines](/en/triceps-routines/) guide shows where this exercise fits in a full session.

## The lying versions

If the low pulley does not let you keep the elbows in front, the lying version changes the body angle and makes the position easier. The [lying EZ-bar extension](/en/lying-triceps-extension/) and the [lying dumbbell extension](/en/lying-dumbbell-triceps-extension/) cover that variation with the bench as support.
