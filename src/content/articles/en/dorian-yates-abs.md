Dorian Yates won Mr. Olympia six times in a row and trained his abs like any other muscle group: with load, sets and failure. His routine is not exotic at all, and that is exactly the argument.

## The abs are just another muscle

Yates starts from a simple idea. The rectus abdominis is a skeletal muscle, with fibres, tendons and a training response no different from the triceps or the biceps. If it does not grow from hundreds of reps without resistance, it will not grow in the abs either.

His comparison is direct: nobody would do three sets of a hundred bench press reps. With the abs, the habit is to add reps until you lose count. For him, that explains why so many people train abs for years without seeing any change in the shape of the muscle.

His routine goes the other way: little variety, slow execution, a hard contraction at the top and sets that end near failure, around twenty reps.

## Why the abs do not show

Two things get mixed in Yates's argument, and they are worth separating. The muscle develops from resisted work; whether it shows depends on the fat covering it. No abdominal exercise removes fat from the abdomen. Fat tissue comes down with a sustained calorie deficit, and strength training helps keep muscle while that deficit progresses.

Yates argues that cardio and diet are a complement and that the main factor is the right exercise. That stance has a useful part and a weak one. The useful part is development: flat abs with no tone do not improve by losing weight, and a strong midsection is built with load. The weak part is crediting abdominal work with removing the fat that covers it.

Put another way: train the muscle so it has something to show and adjust body fat so it shows. They are two separate jobs and neither replaces the other.

## One heavy basic in every session

His second idea is that the abs work in almost everything. In squats, deadlifts, bent rows, standing military presses and Olympic lifts, the trunk muscles brace to stabilise the spine and transfer force.

That is why he recommends at least one heavy free-weight basic in every session, whatever body part that day covers. On the bench press your back is supported and the margin is smaller, but the torso still needs tension when the load is high.

That work does not replace the specific routine. It complements it: it builds stability and teaches the abs to contract against a heavy resistance.

## Dorian Yates's ab routine

Three exercises, four or five sets each, and a target of twenty reps to failure. Execution rules: every rep goes down and up slowly, with a hard contraction at the point of peak tension.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roman-chair trunk raises | 4-5 | 20 |
| Floor crunches | 4-5 | 20 |
| Reverse crunches | 4-5 | 20 |

> **Note:** the twenty reps are the point of failure, not a fixed quota. If you reach twenty with room left, the set did not end where it should.

Yates also mentions hanging leg raises and high-resistance ab machines as variants he used to good effect. He leaves them out of the base routine because the three exercises above already cover the work.

The Roman chair allows trunk flexion with the hips fixed. Floor crunches and reverse crunches add two different angles: the first flexes the trunk from the floor, the second lifts the pelvis and legs. The rectus abdominis is one muscle and contracts as a whole in all three; what changes is which part of the range carries more load.

## Three sessions a week

The routine runs three times a week, at the end of the weight session. That is the part most people skip: leaving abs for last usually means doing them without enthusiasm or not doing them at all.

Yates accepts that other bodybuilders train abs at the start of the session. That works too, because it warms up the torso, improves blood flow and switches on the nervous system before heavy loads. What matters is that the work gets done and does not compete with the rest of the session for the energy you need.

## What to take from his approach

The routine is short and can be copied as it stands, but the thinking is what really transfers:

- Treat the abs as a muscle and give them load, sets and rest.
- Move slowly and chase failure around twenty reps, not a hundred loose reps.
- Include one heavy basic in every session and let the torso stabilise.
- Do not expect abdominal training to reveal the muscle: body fat decides that.
