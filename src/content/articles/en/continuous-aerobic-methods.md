They are the most suitable for improving cardiovascular capacity and health. They let you control work intensity and involve no big pace changes, which allows a continuous adjustment of the cardiovascular system. Each one has features that make it more useful for one goal or another.

## Continuous aerobic methods: uniform work

These are the ones where heart rate stays stable during exercise and intensity does not change significantly. Within that uniformity you can keep HR near the lower limit or raise it near the upper limit.

### Low intensity uniform work

It improves peripheral circulation and is ideal either for active recovery goals in trained people or for the first sessions of anyone starting a cardiovascular programme.

### High intensity uniform work

It runs near the HR limit of each person's training range and usually sits close to the anaerobic threshold. The goal is to raise the functional capacity of the cardiocirculatory system by increasing oxygen consumption.

It improves aerobic power and affects lactic anaerobic capacity. It improves glycogen use, increases glycogen stores, hypertrophies the heart muscle and improves capillarisation of skeletal muscle. Volume is lower than in low intensity methods, because holding a high HR for long periods is hard.

## Continuous aerobic methods: variable work

Here intensity changes during the session. Those changes can be organised in several ways.

### Progressive

You start at low intensity and raise it very gradually: pace, watts, cadence. The recommended duration ranges from 45 to 50 minutes. It suits people with some fitness level: it is the next step after stable low intensity continuous methods. Starting easy produces the cardiovascular adaptations you need and the body responds well to the gradual rise.

Begin with your pulse at the lower limit and raise the pace or resistance while watching HR climb in a progressive, controlled way. It is ideal for running: you start walking, move to long strides with arm swings, continue with a light jog and finish running. If you use a cardio machine, keep the work rate and raise the resistance level every 4 or 5 minutes.

### Fartlek

The main feature is that pace and intensity are random. That is what happens in an outdoor run or bike ride: the terrain forces pace changes. An indoor cycling session usually has that variable profile too.

That variable, random orientation stops the body from adapting and keeps producing new adaptations. The final load is higher than in continuous methods, and it is very useful for improving aerobic capacity and achieving high calorie expenditure.

For experienced people who can read their sensations, fartlek gives you freedom to change pace when it suits you. It is good to let your impulses loose now and then and not look at the heart rate monitor so much.

### Intervals

Also called interval training: it alternates periods of load with periods of active recovery in a systematic, structured way. It is used to raise the total training load. Pace changes are large, so it is indicated for advanced people.

It is a method for improving performance and is used on the track, treadmill or stationary bike, where you can control pace and intensity. The goal is to reach peaks that go beyond the upper limit of your working range, the anaerobic threshold, and come close to maximum HR. Only that way do you reach and exceed maximum oxygen consumption, with a functional improvement of the whole cardiocirculatory system. Load periods are followed by recovery down to the lower limit.

The reference is that recovery periods should always be shorter than load periods, in a 2/1 or 4/1 ratio. Some examples: 4 minutes of load and 2 of recovery, or 5 minutes of load and 1.5 of recovery.

## How to apply continuous methods

Choose the method by goal and by level. Low intensity uniform work is the entry point: it serves for the first sessions and for recovering between hard workouts. It follows the same logic as [LISS cardio](/en/liss-cardio-guide/), which sets intensity with the talk test.

Once you have a base, move to the 45 to 50 minute progressive session. Start at your lower limit and climb little by little, without jumps. On a machine, keep the pace and raise resistance every 4 or 5 minutes. Outdoors, running is the natural setting for this method.

Fartlek comes when you can read your sensations and do not need the monitor for every change. Let the terrain and your mood set the pace, and save this method for days when you want a high load without the structure of intervals.

Interval training is for advanced people and performance goals. Alternate load and active recovery in a 2/1 or 4/1 ratio, and work on the track, treadmill or stationary bike so you can control the pace. If you want to program intervals in more detail, the [HIIT training guide](/en/hiit-training-guide/) develops the structure.

No method is compulsory every week. Share out uniform work, progressive sessions and fartlek according to the goal of the block, and leave intervals for when performance is the priority.
