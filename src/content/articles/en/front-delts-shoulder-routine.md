The front head of the deltoid is the one that works hardest without being asked. Every bench press, every military press and every push-up loads it, so a real lag is unusual. When it shows up, the better move is to change the order and the angle of the work you already do, rather than adding raises at the end of the session.

## Why the barbell works the front head harder

With dumbbells, the weight tends to stay at the sides of the body. With a barbell, the path runs in front of the face and the chest, and the front head carries that resistance through the whole range. That is why the first change is swapping dumbbell presses for barbell presses, at least in one of every two shoulder sessions.

The barbell upright row completes the work: it travels up in front, with high elbows, and the front and middle heads share the load. If the bar bothers your wrist, the dumbbell version follows the same path with a freer grip.

## The key exercise: incline-bench front raise

The incline bench changes the angle of the front raise and keeps tension from the first rep to the last. It is the exercise you feel most in this area and the one you see least in standard routines.

The position: lying face up on an incline bench with two dumbbells and a pronated grip. The arms start straight over the thighs, without locking the elbow. Contract the front deltoid and raise the dumbbells until the arms are perpendicular to the floor, or close to it. Lower them to the starting point without resting them on the thighs between reps, so the set does not break.

Two details decide the exercise. The first is the elbow: it stays almost straight, but not rigid, and it does not bend to gain momentum. The second is the range: if the dumbbell rises past perpendicular, the traps join in and the deltoid stops being the star.

## The routine

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barbell military press or Smith machine press | 4 | 6-8 |
| Barbell or dumbbell upright row | 3 | 8-10 |
| Incline-bench dumbbell front raise | 3-4 | 10-12 |
| Rear-delt raises or reverse flyes | 3 | 10-12 |

## Shoulder balance

The routine ends with rear delts for a reason: the shoulder joint tolerates front work well and imbalance badly. If you add presses and front raises without compensating, posture closes and the shoulder loses range in the press.

Keep the last set as written. If you want more rear-delt work, the guide to [training the rear delts](/en/rear-delt-training/) explains which angles isolate it and how often it needs training.

## When this routine is not for you

Before you start, check whether the lag is real. Look at your numbers: if the military press climbs, the chest progresses and the front delt still does not show, it may be a definition problem rather than a muscle one. And if the bench press already leaves the deltoid sore the next day, adding front volume makes it worse.

This routine is for a visible lag after months of work, with back and chest in order. For a shoulder with no specific lag, the [complete shoulder workout](/en/complete-shoulder-workout/) spreads the work across the three heads and works as a starting point.

## How to progress

Run the routine for eight to ten weeks without changing the exercises. The 6 to 8 rep sets on the press call for more weight once the last rep is clean; the raises call for more reps before more kilos, because a straight arm has little mechanical advantage and momentum shows up fast.

On the incline raise, lower the weight if the elbow bends or if the dumbbell rests on the thigh between reps. One extra kilo through the full range is worth more than three kilos through half of it.
