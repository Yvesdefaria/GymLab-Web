## Mistake 1: the anabolic window

The idea that the shake has to go in during the first thirty minutes does not hold. Muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for hours after training, and the daily protein total and its distribution across the day decide the result. The post-workout shake is a convenient way to add protein at any point in the day. A shake is fast, not urgent.

For a strength trainee, the range is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day. Splitting that into three or four meals of 25 to 40 grams covers most of the day without watching the clock.

## Mistake 2: whey does not build more than food

Protein powder is food in powder form. Its advantage is logistical: it mixes fast, needs no cooking and fits in a backpack. Next to a chicken breast or a piece of fish, it has no special muscle-building properties.

The comparison matters when total protein falls short. If you reach the range with food, the tub adds nothing. If you do not, powder is the most convenient way to close the gap. The guide to [buying protein](/en/protein-powder-guide/) explains what to read on the label, and the [protein supplements](/en/protein-supplements/) guide sorts the types.

## Mistake 3: BCAAs when protein is already enough

Branched-chain amino acids are three of the nine essential amino acids. A complete protein, animal or soy, already brings them in sufficient amounts. Adding them separately to a diet that meets protein needs has shown no advantage in trials.

The exception is the usual one: if total protein is low, the problem is solved with complete protein, not with three loose amino acids. The body needs all nine to build muscle.

## Mistake 4: loading and cycling creatine

Creatine monohydrate works on a low, steady dose. Three to five grams a day keep stores at maximum; the loading phase is optional and only speeds up saturation. There is no need to cycle it, take a month off or add a carbohydrate transporter.

Loading, if used, is 20 grams a day split into four doses for five days. After that, return to the daily dose. In healthy adults, trials with 3 to 5 grams a day show no kidney damage.

## What is left

Three things survive the list: creatine monohydrate, protein when the total falls short and real food. The rest of the tubs compete for a gap the diet already fills.

Creatine has the most consistent evidence for strength and size. Protein powder is a convenience tool, and [whey](/en/whey-protein-production/) is the most common form of that tool. Food brings the rest: calories, fibre, micronutrients and satiety. A meal of real food does the same job as a scoop and brings more with it.

## Buy nothing

That is the uncomfortable part. If training does not progress, no tub fixes it: [progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) and [volume](/en/training-volume/) do the work, and the money is better spent on food. If body weight does not rise, the problem is calories, and the guide to [gaining weight](/en/how-to-gain-weight/) explains how to raise them without filling the cupboard with powders.

To lose fat without losing muscle, the [lose fat, gain muscle](/en/lose-fat-gain-muscle/) guide orders the process. Before buying, check the protein total and the calories; then decide whether anything is left to cover.
