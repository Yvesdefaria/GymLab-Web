The adaptation is slow, especially for the impatient. How does the body work when it tries to gain muscle mass and strength? And how should you train so it keeps adapting?

## What is homeostasis and how does it limit weight-training adaptation?

**Homeostasis** is the relatively constant equilibrium of the body's internal environment. It is maintained naturally by adaptive responses that preserve harmony and health.

If you do 20 push-ups in a single day you will not notice changes. If you do them every day, the body has to adapt to normalise the exercise, and the result is that you gain strength and muscle. That state is held by sensory, feedback and control mechanisms: heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature, electrolyte balance, breathing and glandular secretion.

The body looks for constancy in its parameters. A one-off stimulus triggers a chain of reactions to return to metabolic normality.

When you train with weights, the body perceives the effort as an aggression against its integrity and speeds up certain processes to minimise the repercussions. It is an anomalous situation it has to adapt to, and it has few long-term consequences if it is not repeated.

Moving a 50-kilo cement sack for one day does not make you stronger. If you move it every day, the body adapts to the new situation: the action stops being new, becomes habitual and your strength rises so that moving it does not alter homeostasis. In the end you move it with ease.

The body's priority is to keep equilibrium and make sure actions do not attack its integrity. The final goal is survival, and using 100% of the available energy resources is an alarm signal. Hence the adaptation of the muscles in strength and energy to repetitive effort.

## How long does the body take to adapt to weights?

Imagine you start doing **squats** and have never done them before. After the session your legs are empty of energy, with no glycogen, sore and with exhausted muscles. If you had to run from a predator at that moment, you could not.

After the session the body tries to recover strength and energy so that the same situation does not mean total exhaustion of the legs. That is where **supercompensation** appears: more glycogen is stored and the muscles hypertrophy, with more strength.

Once consistency has already caused adaptations, it is time to set new challenges to put the body on alert again. Over time, that session stops exhausting your legs and their strength and endurance shoot up. At that point, homeostasis has changed to treat squats as normal.

## Why you stop progressing when the load never rises

If training does not increase in difficulty and effort, its repercussions get smaller. Homeostasis tends to minimise any form of physical activity.

Someone who runs a kilometre every day improves performance in a short time. If duration and pace do not change, running the same route in the same time ends up reducing cardiac stimulation.

The same happens with weights. A set of squats with a given load improves the strength and size of the muscles involved, but if the difficulty stays the same, the effects fade and only a higher reaction threshold remains. Anything that does not force a change in homeostasis produces no gains in strength or size.

## What changes does weight training produce?

At first, improvements come mainly from **neuromuscular efficiency**: the brain becomes more effective at activating muscle fibres, which improves coordination and strength without visible changes in muscle size.

Over time, adaptation shows in **muscle hypertrophy**, the increase in fibre size. Adaptation happens when muscles are subjected to stress greater than they are used to, which causes microtears that, when repaired, leave bigger and stronger fibres.

Weight training also improves bone density, cardiovascular capacity and the resistance of tendons and ligaments. These changes belong to a continuous cycle: the body responds to stress with more capacity to handle greater loads.

## How to train so the body keeps adapting

Training must be planned and varied, intense and, above all, constant. If you repeat the same load and the same effort, the body has no reason to change.

[Progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) is how you set that greater stress without losing control. Adjust the load, the reps or the rest, and leave room for [recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/): adaptation needs adequate nutrition and enough rest.

If keeping to the plan is hard, the guide to [staying consistent with training](/en/how-to-stay-consistent-working-out/) and the guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) complete the practical side.

## In short

Adaptation to weight training is the body's response to repeated stress that breaks its equilibrium. First coordination improves at the neuromuscular level, then muscle grows. If the load never rises, homeostasis wins and progress stops.
