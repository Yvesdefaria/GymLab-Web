Abstaining before competition has famous defenders and an intuitive logic: if sexual effort costs energy, it might take something away from the match. The intuition falls apart when it is measured. Studies comparing athletes with and without sex the night before found no differences in strength, power or oxygen uptake.

## What was measured and what was not

Four studies on strength, aerobic capacity and VO2max found no advantage from abstinence or from sexual activity. The samples were small and the protocols differed, so the conclusion is the size it is: in what can be measured, the effect is indistinguishable from zero. The evidence is old and uneven, and none of the studies was designed to detect a small difference. That cuts both ways: it rules out a large effect and cannot rule out a tiny one.

Concentration has a nuance. In one study, sex the night before did not affect attention tests, while sex two hours before was associated with less attention. The reasonable margin is a few hours, not a week.

## Energy cost is not the problem

Sex uses around 100 kcal in an average person, a figure close to a half-hour walk. It does not empty glycogen or compete with a strength session. The idea that it drains energy for the match confuses the cost of an hour with the cost of a day and forgets that the body replaces what it spends.

## Orgasm and testosterone

After orgasm there is a brief rise in testosterone, and that finding became a promise of more muscle and more strength. The leap does not hold. The rise is short, does not change muscle mass and comes nowhere near the doses used in studies with exogenous testosterone.

The guide to [fat and testosterone](/en/fat-and-testosterone/) explains which factors really move the hormone. Sex occupies a small place on that list, behind sleep, available energy and genetics.

## The risk is in the night

The problem is rarely sexual activity. It is almost always what comes with it: a long dinner, alcohol, fewer hours of sleep and travel the next day. That combination does reduce performance, and it does so through known routes.

Alcohol fragments sleep and lowers protein synthesis, as the guide to [alcohol and training](/en/alcohol-and-training/) details. Sleeping four hours less weighs more than any orgasm. If the night comes before a competition, the priority is the bed and the water.

## The mind plays too

Someone who believes sex drains performance can feel it even when physiology does not explain it. That is the effect of expectation, and it works both ways: abstinence can also give a sense of control that helps some athletes. There is no reliable evidence that abstinence increases aggression either.

What does help is not trying anything new the night before a competition. Not a new dinner, not a new schedule, not an argument about abstinence. Routine protects sleep and removes variables.

## Women: the same lack of evidence

Studies on sex and performance in women are fewer and smaller, and none shows a drop in strength or aerobic capacity. Claims about a 24-hour analgesic effect do not have enough support to become a recommendation. For this question, the body does not need a different rule by sex.

## What to do before competing

Keep the night normal. Sleep 7 to 9 hours, avoid alcohol and leave a couple of hours between sexual activity and the event if attention matters. The guide to [how much sleep you need to progress](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) and the one on [active recovery](/en/active-recovery/) cover the rest of the rest.

Dinner counts too. The guide to [how to split meals](/en/how-to-split-meals/) places protein and carbohydrates without improvising, and the [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) page avoids looking in a jar for the energy that a full night of sleep provides.

## When to seek help

Loss of libido, persistent fatigue or erection problems can have medical causes and are not fixed by abstinence or supplements. Blood tests and a consultation are worth more than any theory about the night before.
