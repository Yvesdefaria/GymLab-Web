Calorie counting has a reputation for being obsessive and imprecise. Both are partly true, and neither makes the tool useless. Knowing how much you eat calibrates the eye; it is not a life sentence to an app.

## Atwater is the easy part

Atwater factors, the figures labels use, start from a stable amount of energy: 4 kilocalories per gram of protein, 4 per gram of carbohydrate and 9 per gram of fat. Alcohol adds 7 per gram and does not show up in most counts.

The label provides the grams, and multiplying is enough. A food with 15 grams of protein, 50 of carbohydrate and 20 of fat delivers 60 + 200 + 180, or 440 kilocalories. The operation is exact; the starting data are not.

Label figures refer to 100 grams or to a stated serving. If the pack holds 30-gram servings and the whole pack gets eaten, the count doubles. Checking servings per pack before grams avoids many mistakes.

## The big error is the portion

Estimating a portion by eye drifts by 20 to 30 percent on difficult foods. With oil, nuts or cheese, that margin decides the result. A handful of nuts can be 20 grams or 60, and the difference is about 250 kilocalories.

Visual references are for eating away from home, not for replacing the scale in your kitchen. They are a language, not an exact measure.

## One week with a scale

The best use of a kitchen scale is temporary. Weighing usual foods for a week, raw and cooked, teaches how much a real portion weighs. After that, estimates carry a reasonable margin, and the scale comes back if weight stalls.

Weight changes with cooking. A hundred grams of raw rice becomes 250 to 300 grams cooked because it absorbs water. Meat loses 20 to 25 percent of its weight, and dried legumes triple theirs after soaking and cooking.

What matters is choosing one convention and keeping it. Raw or cooked makes no difference if it is always the same. Mixing the two in the same week doubles figures for no reason.

## Labels and rounding

Labels round. Below 0.5 grams per 100 grams, a nutrient can be declared as zero, and calories are rounded. In a small product, several zeros can hide a portion that adds up.

The stated serving is not always the one eaten. A pack with two servings finished by one person delivers twice the table. Cereals, sauces and nuts are the usual cases.

Fibre and polyols deliver less energy than the rest of the carbohydrates, and automatic counts do not always apply that. For most people the difference is small; anyone counting every gram should read the fine print.

## Alcohol and liquid calories

Alcohol delivers 7 kilocalories per gram and does not fill you. A 330-millilitre beer is around 130 kilocalories, a 150-millilitre glass of wine about 110, and a 30-millilitre spirit about 70. Across a week, they add up to several meals.

The problem is not only the number. Alcohol lowers the decision barrier: dinner with two glasses often ends with bread, cheese or dessert that were not in the plan. Counting it without judging is more useful than banning it and compensating the next day.

## When to stop counting

Counting calories is for learning, not for ever. Tracking what you eat for four to six weeks with a log teaches accurate enough estimates, and after that the weight trend every two or three weeks is enough.

A kitchen scale, a label and a short list of visual references are enough. With a clinical goal, an eating disorder or kidney, liver or metabolic disease, tracking happens with a professional and different criteria.
