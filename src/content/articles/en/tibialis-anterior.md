The tibialis anterior sits at the front of the lower leg, against the outside of the tibia. You can feel it when you lift your toes and press just beside the bone. Its main job is dorsiflexion, which brings the top of the foot towards the shin, and it also inverts the foot.

## Where it starts and where it attaches

It starts at the lateral condyle and the body of the tibia, then runs down the front of the leg and across the ankle. It attaches to the first metatarsal and the medial cuneiform of the foot. That path explains why its tendon shows when you walk on your heels.

## What it does on every step

When the foot lands, the tibialis anterior slows the sole so it does not slap down. During stance it controls how far the arch drops, and as you lift the foot it pulls the toes up so they clear the ground. Running and sprinting repeat the same action, faster and with more load. An ankle that does not lift the foot in time compensates at the knee or the hip.

## The calf's antagonist

The gastrocnemius and the soleus push the foot down, into plantarflexion. The tibialis anterior does the opposite, and that pair keeps the ankle stable in both directions. If the calf is strong and the tibialis anterior is not, the foot loses control in the swing phase and the braking of each stride is shared worse. The [calf and soleus anatomy](/en/calf-anatomy/) explains the other side of the pair.

## Why the shin starts to hurt

When the dorsiflexors fatigue, the stride changes. The foot lands flatter, the arch gives way and the tibia takes more stress at each contact. In runners who add volume or train on hard surfaces, that accumulation shows up as pain along the inner edge of the shin.

The correct name is tibial periostitis, also called medial tibial stress syndrome. The original article used "pretibial periostitis", which is not the clinical term. The pain does not come from the tibialis anterior alone: the bone, the fascia and the deeper muscles are involved too.

## Three ways to train it

The original article describes three routes and gives no sets or reps. This guide keeps the three and does not invent a prescription.

**Banded dorsiflexion.** Sit with the band anchored in front of you and the foot inside the loop. Lift the toes against the resistance, hold for a second and lower slowly.

**Heel walking.** Lift the toes and walk on your heels. It is the simplest exercise and the one that most resembles the action you want to train. Do it barefoot on a smooth surface.

**The loaded option.** Specific equipment for dorsiflexion exists, but you do not need it. A cable machine with a foot strap or a dumbbell held on the instep offer the same resistance with more control. The ankle has to travel through its full arc and the lowering must not speed up.

## How to fit it into your week

Two or three short sessions are enough for a small muscle that already works whenever you walk. At the end of leg training or inside the warm-up are the two easiest slots.

If you run, frequency matters more than load. Ten minutes of specific work twice a week supports the ankle better than one long session. [Warm-up and stretching](/en/gym-warm-up/) explain how to fit it in without taking strength from the session.

To choose sets and reps, the guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/) works the same as for any small muscle group, and the guide to [calf growth](/en/calf-growth/) covers training the other side.
