Ronnie Coleman was working as a police officer in Arlington, Texas, when a colleague took him to Metroflex. The owner, Brian Dobson, offered him a lifetime free membership in exchange for one thing: letting him coach him for competition.

He accepted, and between 1998 and 2005 he won eight Mr. Olympias in a row, matching Lee Haney. On stage he weighed close to 135 kilos, and his shout of "Light Weight, Baby" became the soundtrack of a decade. The [list of Mr. Olympia winners](/en/mr-olympia-winners/) places those titles in context.

## The six-day routine

Coleman trained six days a week and hit every muscle group twice. He combined heavy basic movements with an isolation volume that is hard to imagine today, and sessions ran for hours.

This is the split his best years describe. He changed exercises and emphasis, but the structure stayed:

- **Day 1:** back, biceps and shoulders, with an emphasis on density. Heavy deadlift, barbell row, T-bar row, one-arm dumbbell row, barbell curl and dumbbell curl.
- **Day 2:** legs, with an emphasis on quads. Squats in sets that climbed to failure, leg press and leg extensions.
- **Day 3:** chest and triceps. Flat, incline and decline bench press, dumbbell press, triceps extensions and dips.
- **Day 4:** back, biceps and shoulders, with an emphasis on width. Pull-ups or pulldowns, seated row, military press and lateral raises.
- **Day 5:** legs, with an emphasis on hamstrings and calves. Romanian deadlift, lying and seated leg curl, calf raises.
- **Day 6:** chest and triceps with more isolation, sometimes arms or calves.
- **Day 7:** rest.

The split describes what he did. It is not a template. The guide to [training volume](/en/training-volume/) explains why that number of sets only holds up with years of base, food and rest.

## The weights he moved

The numbers attributed to him measure the man: a 363-kilo squat for two reps, a 363-kilo deadlift for two and a 226-kilo bench press for five. On the military press he used 80-kilo dumbbells in each hand.

The shouts of "Light Weight" and "Ain't nuttin' but a peanut" were not for the camera. They were how he convinced himself that the absurd weight in front of him was, in fact, light.

## The 5,500 to 6,000 calorie diet

To hold 135 kilos of mass and train the way he trained, he ate between 5,500 and 6,000 calories a day across six meals:

- **Breakfast:** a cup of grits with cheese, two cups of egg whites and coffee.
- **Pre-workout:** a protein shake.
- **Post-workout:** another protein shake.
- **First meal:** 450 g of grilled chicken breast, a cup and a half of brown rice, a cup of red beans and two pieces of cornbread.
- **Second meal:** another 450 g of chicken breast and a large baked potato.
- **Dinner:** 450 g of beef steak, a baked potato and salad.

The protein he declared sat between 500 and 600 grams a day. That figure runs far above the 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of bodyweight the hypertrophy literature works with, and it is recorded here as what he said, not as a recommendation.

Fast food showed up as an extra: pizza or burgers on top of the six meals, to add calories.

## The price of the career

In 1996, before his first Olympia, he suffered a severe disc herniation. He kept training heavy through the whole dynasty. In 2006 Jay Cutler took the title from him.

After retiring he went through more than thirteen major surgeries, mostly on his back and hips: spinal fusions, disc replacements, titanium screws and total hip replacements. Several failed or had complications. He has since been seen in a wheelchair or on crutches, living with chronic pain.

Asked whether he regrets training that heavy, he says no. His only regret, he has said, is not doing four reps on that 800-pound squat instead of two.

## What you can learn

Dedication is the part anyone can copy. He showed up for every workout, ate every meal on the plan and kept pushing when it no longer felt good. That discipline separates the lifter who progresses from the one who only tries.

The rest is a warning. Sharp pain in a joint or a nerve is not the burn of effort, and the line that sums up his story is this: heavy training is good, stupid training is not. [High-intensity training](/en/high-intensity-training/) and the guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explain where the useful limit sits.

His way of training came out of a neighbourhood gym in the nineties, the kind that has almost disappeared. The guide to the [old-school gym](/en/old-school-gym-training/) covers what that setting had and why it is hard to reproduce in a shopping-centre fitness club.
