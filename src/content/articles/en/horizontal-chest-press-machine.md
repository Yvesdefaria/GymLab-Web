## What separates it from the vertical press

On the [vertical press](/en/vertical-chest-press-machine/), the handles are pushed forward from shoulder height and resistance runs horizontally. That is why the chest stays loaded when the arms are almost extended: the machine does not take the work away at the end.

On the horizontal machine, the back lies flatter and the push goes forward with resistance running at an angle. Tension is higher halfway through and falls as the elbows approach extension. Each machine has its point on the curve and its use.

Each machine follows from that. The vertical press finishes the rep under tension and works with the chest high. The horizontal press accumulates volume in the middle range with a stable path and teaches you to press without thinking about balance.

## Setup: the seat at chest height

Sit with your back on the pad and set the seat so the handles sit at chest height, not at the shoulders or the neck. A seat that is too low leaves the shoulders above the handles and turns the press into a downward push. One that is too high leaves the handles next to the throat.

The feet stay flat on the floor, knees slightly bent. The shoulder blades come together and stay low, pinned to the pad, for the whole set. The head stays supported too.

The wrists stay stacked over the forearms. If the machine offers a grip choice, pronated is the most direct; neutral changes the shoulder angle and helps when the shoulder asks for another position.

## Execution

Push the handles forward until the elbows are extended but not locked. Locking at the end unloads the chest exactly when the machine already removes tension, so it adds nothing.

Return slowly, keeping the stack under control, until the handles reach chest height. Do not let the weight rest between reps: that rest turns the set into a run of loose reps.

The elbows stay at about 45 degrees from the torso. There is no need to flare them to 90 or tuck them against the sides. [Exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) explains why that middle angle shares the work between chest and triceps.

## The part about getting trapped

The line repeated about this machine is that you cannot get pinned under the weight. That is half true and worth saying whole: there is no bar holding you down, and if you let go of the handles the stack returns to its place. You can abandon the set whenever you want.

That has a cost of its own. With no physical consequence, abandoning a hard rep is easy, and a set of ten becomes a set of seven plus three loose pushes. The fix is to count reps and fix a target before you start, not improvise on feel.

What the machine does not remove is the load on the shoulder. The path is stable, but the shoulder still presses, and a badly set seat repeats the same forced position on every rep.

## Dose and progression

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. Pick a load that lets all three sets follow the same path without locking.

The normal progression is load, once 12 reps come out clean across all three sets. If progress stalls, add a set before adding weight: the extra volume usually unlocks the machine.

## Where it belongs in the session

It works as a first press for a beginner, because the guided path leaves the attention on the chest and not on balance. It also works at the end of the session, once the chest is worked and heavy load is no longer the point.

In a machine session it is the main press. In a session with a free bar, it is the second or third movement. The [machines vs free weights](/en/machines-vs-free-weights-chest/) comparison helps split volume between the two families.
