The hammer is the most repeated curl and the least explained. It gets credit for working the long head of the biceps, which it does not, and it misses the real point: it loads the brachialis and the forearm without asking the wrist to turn.

## What the neutral grip does

With the palms facing, the biceps loses the advantage of supinating and the brachialis takes a large share of the flexion. The brachialis starts on the lower half of the humerus and ends on the ulna: it crosses the elbow, flexes and does not turn the forearm. That is why it works the same with a neutral palm as with a palm facing up.

The brachioradialis, the muscle that shows on the thumb side, joins in: it flexes the elbow mostly when the hand is not facing up. Together they push the biceps from below and add size where the arm usually looks thin.

The biceps does not switch off. No elbow flexion leaves it out. What changes is the split, and that split makes the hammer the partner of the [barbell curl](/en/barbell-curl/), not its replacement.

## The position

Stand with the feet at hip width and the knees unlocked. A dumbbell in each hand, palms facing and arms hanging. The elbows stay pinned to the torso.

Grip the middle of the handle with the thumb closed around it. The dumbbell stays vertical, one end up and one end down, through the whole set.

Chest up, back straight. The first rep already shows whether the weight is too heavy: if the torso leans back to start, the load is past the limit of the arm.

## The range

The dumbbell rises in a short arc to chest height with the elbow as the only hinge. At the top, squeeze for a second without shrugging. Lower under control for two or three seconds to full extension.

The bottom is the part most often cut. Without lowering to a straight arm, the brachialis loses the part of the range where it works most. Reaching the end is not locking the elbow back: it is finishing the range without dropping the dumbbell.

The wrist does not turn. If the palm ends facing up on the last rep, the exercise became a regular curl and the brachialis stopped doing most of the work.

## It is not a long-head exercise

Many descriptions file the hammer under the long head of the biceps. The long head crosses the shoulder and the short head does not, and the angle of the arm relative to the torso decides which one arrives stretched. In a standing hammer the arm hangs at the side, exactly as in a standing supinated curl.

Changing the palm changes the muscle that flexes, not the head that stretches. For the long head the shoulder has to sit behind the body, and the incline bench provides that position. The [biceps and forearm anatomy](/en/biceps-forearm-anatomy/) shows the path of both heads and why the grip does not separate them.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The number is this page's recommendation; the original gives none.

Add weight when all three sets reach 12 reps without swinging, with the elbows in place and the dumbbell still vertical. Each exercise is compared with itself: the weight of the hammer does not carry over to the supinated curl.

## The mistakes

- **Swinging the torso.** Momentum lifts the dumbbell and moves the work to the lower back.
- **Letting the elbows spread.** Once the elbow leaves your side, the front delt starts pushing.
- **Cutting the descent.** Without reaching extension, the brachialis loses its long position.
- **Turning the wrist.** A palm that ends facing up turns the hammer into a curl.
- **Shrugging.** When the traps rise, the elbow drops and the range gets shorter.

## Where it fits

The hammer goes after a heavy curl and before forearm work. [Grip strength training](/en/grip-strength-training/) explains why the forearm needs its own work, and [forearm exercises](/en/forearm-exercises/) order the four actions of the wrist. The [forearm routine](/en/forearm-routine/) places this curl next to the rest of the grip work.

The [biceps routines](/en/biceps-routines/) spread the variants across the week and [bigger biceps](/en/bigger-biceps/) shows how to alternate grips without repeating the same stimulus. The hammer asks less of the elbow when the bar starts to bother, and that is a comfort argument, not a rehab one.
