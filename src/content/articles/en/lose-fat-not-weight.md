The doubt shows up in the first week of a diet: weight drops two kilos, then rises one, and the quick conclusion is that something is failing. It is almost never the plan. It is the tool used to measure it.

## What the scale adds up

The number on the scale is the sum of body fat, muscle mass, liver and muscle glycogen, water and the contents of the digestive tract. Only the first is the target.

Glycogen carries water: each stored gram holds about three. A dinner with more carbohydrate and salt than usual can add a kilo by the next morning without a single gram of fat gained. A long strength session inflames muscle and holds fluid for a day or two. A stretch of constipation adds its share.

That is why daily weight works as data, not as a verdict. The trend over three or four weeks says far more than any single morning. A bioimpedance scale does not solve it either: its body-fat percentage shifts with hydration and with the last meal, so it works for comparing trends, not for reading an exact figure.

## What to measure instead

The waist is the best home indicator of abdominal fat. Measure with the tape at the navel, without pulling tight, at the end of a normal exhale. Always at the same time, ideally in the morning before eating.

Photos every four weeks, with the same light, the same posture and the same distance, show changes the scale hides. Clothes that fit differently count too, and so does strength: if weight holds but more kilos move on the same lifts, the process is working.

## The deficit that holds

A deficit of 300 to 500 calories a day allows a quarter to half a kilo of fat loss per week. At the start the scale drops more because of water and glycogen, and less as weight falls. More aggressive cuts speed up the first week and usually end in hunger, binges or quitting.

Protein stays high, between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day, to preserve muscle and keep hunger down. Carbohydrate covers training, around 3 to 5 grams per kilo for anyone lifting weights, and fat fills the rest of the calories. Fibre, between 25 and 35 grams, helps the plate satisfy.

The deficit can be split many ways: three meals, four, two, or an eight-hour window. No distribution changes the result when calories and protein are the same.

## Strength and steps, in that order

Strength training is what tells the body to keep muscle while the deficit lasts. Two to four sessions a week, with compound lifts and a simple progression, are enough for most people.

Walking adds expenditure without taxing recovery. A target of 7,000 to 10,000 steps a day covers many people; structured cardio is optional and can be added in two or three sessions of 20 to 30 minutes. [Strength training to lose fat](/en/weight-training-for-fat-loss/) develops the plan in more detail.

## Why weight rises on any given Tuesday

Sudden increases almost always have an explanation. A salty meal retains water. A high-carb day fills glycogen. A new or harder session leaves muscle inflamed. The menstrual cycle moves fluid for several days. Creatine, 3 to 5 grams a day, increases water inside the muscle and can add a kilo at the start.

None of those gains is fat. If the waist does not move and clothes fit the same, the scale is telling another story.

## When to change the calories

Wait three or four weeks before changing anything. If the average weight and the waist have not moved in that time, cut 100 or 200 calories or add 1,000 or 2,000 steps a day. Then wait again.

Reacting to every morning is the fastest route to frustration. The body does not negotiate with impatience.

## What does not change the result

Sit-ups do not burn the fat that covers them, and no spot exercise does. [Spot reduction](/en/spot-reduction-myth/) is an old myth.

The thyroid only explains a plateau when there is a diagnosis and a blood test to confirm it. Cellulite creams do not change adipose tissue. And fat burners that promise results without diet or training have shown nothing against a well-run deficit.

## A pace you can keep

Losing between 0.5% and 1% of body weight per week is possible at the start for someone with plenty of fat to lose. After that, a quarter to half a kilo a week is realistic with a 300 to 500 calorie deficit. Anyone starting from a low body-fat percentage moves more slowly, and that is normal too.
