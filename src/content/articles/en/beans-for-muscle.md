Rice and chicken works, and there is no need to change it. Beans enter the same diet by another route: they cost little, fill you up and bring fibre and minerals that a chicken breast does not have. The key is knowing what they provide and how to cook them so they do not take their revenge.

All of them sit around one gram of fat per cup, so the energy comes mostly from starch and fibre. That combination explains why they satisfy so much and why they are so cheap per calorie.

## Incomplete protein, still useful

A cup of beans carries more protein than two large eggs. The difference is the amino acid profile: legumes are low in methionine and cereals are low in lysine. Put them together across the day and the protein from both goes further.

That does not make beans a second-class protein. In a diet with eggs, fish or meat, legumes add to the total without a problem. If the diet is vegan, pairing them with rice, corn or bread stops being a detail and becomes the rule.

## Fibre: the benefit and the limit

One cup covers a good part of the fibre recommended for the day. That fibre feeds the bacteria in the colon, helps transit and blunts the rise in glucose after a meal. It is also the cause of the gas.

The oligosaccharides in beans are not digested in the small intestine and reach the colon, where bacteria ferment them and produce gas. Gas is the normal result of that fermentation, not a defect in the food.

It can be reduced. Soaking dry beans for eight to twelve hours and throwing the water away removes part of the compounds. Cooking them properly, until they mash against the tongue, finishes the job. Red kidney beans also need a hard boil for ten minutes before the heat goes down, because raw or undercooked they are toxic. Starting with half a cup for a few weeks gives the gut time to adapt.

## The sodium in canned beans

Canned beans solve a meal in two minutes and keep almost all their nutrients. The point to watch is salt: some varieties carry a lot. Draining and rinsing them with water lowers the sodium on the surface, and choosing the no-salt-added version lowers it further.

If the rest of the day is well planned, half a cup of canned beans will not ruin a diet. If hypertension is in play, the total salt for the day is what counts.

## There is no problem after training

For years people repeated that the fibre in beans made them a bad post-workout food because it slows digestion. The reasoning has one true part and a wrong conclusion. Fibre does slow absorption, but a plate of beans with rice still delivers carbohydrate and protein, and the window to refill does not close at thirty minutes.

If you train twice a day and need fast energy between sessions, a shake or a piece of fruit gets there sooner. In every other case, beans are as valid a recovery meal as any other.

## How to fit them into the week

The easiest route is the can: rinse them and add them to a salad, a stew or a plate of rice. The cheapest is dry: soak the night before and simmer, or use a pressure cooker if you are in a hurry.

A reasonable serving is one cooked cup per meal, with rice or bread to complete the amino acid profile. The guide to [legumes in your diet](/en/legumes-in-your-diet/) covers the other varieties, and the guide to [lentils](/en/lentils-in-your-diet/) covers the legume that cooks fastest.

To match the serving to the rest of the day, the guide to [portion sizes](/en/portion-sizes/) gives hand and plate references. And if you want a fat to go with them, [nuts](/en/nuts-nutrition/) are the densest option in the cupboard.
