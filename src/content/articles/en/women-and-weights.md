The fear of weights usually comes from the wrong image: the body you think you will end up with. What happens under load is more concrete. Muscle adapts, bone gets stronger and daily tasks get easier.

## What strength does to muscle

Hypertrophy is the response to repeated mechanical tension above what the muscle already handles. It arrives with progressive overload, enough protein and calories that support the work. In the first weeks strength rises through coordination; size takes months to show. The rep range matters less than the effort: six to twenty reps all work if the set ends near technical failure.

A woman's testosterone sits in the order of one tenth of a man's. That lowers the ceiling for muscle mass, not the ability to respond to training. Gaining several kilos of muscle takes years of high volume and a calorie surplus, so it is not a side effect of two sessions a week. The guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) details the process.

## Bone: the part you cannot see

Bone responds to load like muscle: put it under tension and it reinforces itself. Peak bone mass is reached around thirty and then declines. Menopause speeds the loss through falling oestrogen, and at that point strength training stops being aesthetics and becomes prevention. Impact counts too: walking, stairs or jumping supply the load bone needs beyond the bar, and a bone that has to last decades needs both. The guide to [weights and cardiovascular health](/en/weights-and-cardiovascular-health/) covers the other side of the system.

In your twenties you build, in your forties you maintain, at menopause you protect bone and at sixty function leads. At every stage the exercise is the same: carry, push, pull and hold. You do not need a different exercise for each decade, only a load and a volume that match the moment. The guide to [bodybuilding and ageing](/en/bodybuilding-and-aging/) develops the age side.

## Pregnancy and postpartum

If the pregnancy has no complications, training with your doctor's approval is safe and helps. After birth, the pelvic floor leads: with urine leaks, pressure or a feeling of heaviness, get an assessment from a specialised physiotherapist before loading again. Strength returns progressively, and there is no six-week deadline to meet.

## Why weight goes up when you start

The first weeks of training add one or two kilos on the scale that are not fat. Muscle stores more glycogen and, with it, water. It is a temporary, normal change, and it explains why weight alone does not measure progress. The scale can stall while the mirror changes; waist, photos or bar strength tell the story better.

## Spot reduction does not exist

Crunches do not burn belly fat; pulldowns do not remove arm fat. Fat is mobilised systemically when energy balance is negative, and where it shows first depends on genetics. Local work builds the muscle underneath, which is a different thing and matters too. Training a muscle and burning the fat over it are two separate jobs, and both have their place. The guide on [losing belly fat with crunches](/en/spot-reduction-myth/) takes the promise apart in more detail.

To start, two days a week with five or six exercises and loads that leave two reps in reserve. Progression matters more than the exercise selection.
