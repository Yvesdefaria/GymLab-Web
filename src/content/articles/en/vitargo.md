## What it is and which category it belongs to

Vitargo is the commercial name of a carbohydrate made from waxy maize starch, processed so the stomach empties it quickly. The category it represents is intra-workout carbohydrate: powders dissolved in water and taken during the session.

Chemically it is starch, like the starch in a potato or a slice of bread. The difference sits in the processing, in gastric emptying speed and in the price per gram.

## Under 60 minutes, water is enough

For a 45 to 75 minute weights session, muscle glycogen covers the work and water covers the need. Adding carbohydrate improves neither strength nor hypertrophy in that range, and it does add calories that count towards the day.

The exception is someone who arrives without having eaten or trains fasted in the morning. There a little sugar before the session can stop it collapsing, and a banana solves the problem without powder.

## When the session gets long

From 75 to 90 minutes, especially in the heat, carbohydrate starts to earn its place. The reference is 30 to 60 g per hour, with the aim of keeping glucose available without filling the stomach.

The table is a guide, not a recipe. Gastric tolerance decides: some stomachs accept solid food at 90 minutes and others only tolerate liquid.

## The figures that come from no trial

The packaging ships with a comparison graph and a list of measured advantages: more glycogen, faster gastric emptying, more performance and less protein breakdown. None of those figures appears in a published trial with a described method; they come from a manufacturer's comparison against other carbohydrates it does not identify.

The protein argument is the easiest to take apart. The body does not burn muscle in a 90 minute session if it ate that day. Protein sparing is measured during prolonged fasting, not during a normal workout, and a carbohydrate shake does not change that account.

## Food that does the same

A banana supplies about 25 g of carbohydrate. Two dates, around 15. A sports drink carries 30 to 60 g per 500 ml. Cold rice, bread with honey or a boiled potato also work, with the advantage of bringing fibre, minerals and some protein.

The powder wins on convenience and on how fast it mixes. Nothing else. If the stomach tolerates food during the session, food costs less and covers the same ground.

## Osmolality and cramps

The packaging also promises low osmolality that does not pull water from the body and reduces cramps. A solution with fewer particles per litre is absorbed somewhat faster, but the practical effect is small once the fluid volume is adequate.

Cramps do not come from a lack of carbohydrate: they are linked to fatigue, dehydration and sodium loss. A starch powder does not fix any of the three on its own.

## Where it fits and where it does not

It makes sense in a long race, in a double session, in a hot workout or for someone who cannot eat solid food while moving. Outside those cases, the money is better spent elsewhere.

Performance also depends on what comes before: [caffeine](/en/caffeine-supplement/) has more support than any starch powder. Heat changes fluid needs, as the guide to [training in hot weather](/en/training-in-hot-weather/) explains.

If the goal is fat loss, adding carbohydrate during the session runs against the day's account, and the guide to [training for fat loss](/en/fat-loss-superset-workout/) sets that priority.
