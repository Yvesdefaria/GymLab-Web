Standing and seated calf machines are convenient, but not essential. A barbell and a pair of dumbbells let you load the calf in the two positions that matter, and free weights add a stabilising demand the machine does not ask for. The only condition is respecting the range.

## The rule that orders everything

The knee decides which muscle works. With the leg straight, the gastrocnemius takes the work; with the knee bent, the soleus takes over. The [calf and soleus anatomy](/en/calf-anatomy/) explains why: one crosses the knee and the other does not. That is why the routine has a standing exercise and a seated one, and they are not interchangeable.

## Standing barbell calf raises

Set the bar as for a squat, with the balls of your feet on a step or a plate and your heels off it. Lower until you feel the stretch and rise to the top. That is four sets of 15, 15, 12 and 12 reps.

The free weight adds balance and forces you to hold the bar, something the standing machine does not ask for. It also limits the load: if you can move a lot of weight but cannot hold it, the exercise becomes uncomfortable before it becomes useful. In that case, add reps and lengthen the pause at the top.

## Seated dumbbell calf raises

Sit on a bench with your feet on the step and the dumbbells on your thighs, close to the knee. Raise your heels and lower into the stretch. That is four sets of 20 reps.

Here the weight tends to run short: the dumbbells you can hold on your thigh are not the ones you would move on a seated machine. It does not matter. The pause at the top and the full range make up for the load.

## The range: stretch at the bottom, pause at the top

The calf responds to two things weight does not fix: dropping the heel below the level of the foot and holding for one or two seconds at the top. Without the first, the bottom of the rep disappears; without the second, the bounce does the work.

That is why you do not need to load much. A raise with a pause and a full range stimulates more than three fast sets with twice the weight. If the heel does not drop, the rep stays half done.

## Where to place it in the week

Two or three sessions a week are enough, and each one takes eight to ten minutes. You can do them at the end of leg day or in a separate gap. If you do both positions in the same session, start standing and finish seated: the gastrocnemius fatigues first and the soleus handles the end better.

If you only have time for one, alternate: one week standing, one week seated. Frequency makes up for the lack of variety.

## When to move to machines

Machines allow more load and keep the work on the ankle, especially in the seated version. When free weights run short, the guide to [standing versus seated raises](/en/standing-vs-seated-calf-raises/) explains how to split the two positions, and the guide to [calf growth](/en/calf-growth/) orders the sets per week. Neither is required to start.
