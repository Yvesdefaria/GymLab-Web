Legumes swing between the reputation of poor people's food and that of a superfood. Neither label helps decide what goes on the plate. They are a cheap food, with protein and fibre, worth knowing how to cook and how to spread across the week.

## What legumes provide

Dry, 100 grams of lentils, chickpeas or beans provide around 20 grams of protein, about 60 grams of carbohydrate and very little fat. Fibre runs from 10 to 15 grams in that same amount, more than most refined grains. When cooked they absorb water and double or triple in weight, so a 70 or 80 gram dry portion fills a plate.

The calorie density of the dish is close to that of rice or pasta, with more protein and more fibre. The accompaniment makes the difference: cured sausage, black pudding or a generous pour of oil add more calories than the legume itself.

## Plant protein: how to complete it

Legume protein is limited in methionine and cysteine, two amino acids that grains provide in good quantity. Grains, in turn, are low in lysine, which legumes have in abundance. That is why lentils with rice, chickpeas with couscous or beans with bread form a complete combination.

The idea that the combination must happen in the same bite is stricter than necessary. The body keeps a pool of amino acids and the daily sum also counts. Even so, the classic dish works, tastes good and covers the whole meal.

Soy is the exception in the group: it provides about 36 grams of protein per 100 grams dry and a more complete profile. Soy does not contain vitamin B12. No plant does, and anyone on a vegan diet needs a reliable source, fortified or supplemented.

## Fibre, satiety and gas

Legume fibre adds bulk, retains water and feeds the gut microbiota. The daily reference is 25 to 35 grams, and a plate of legumes covers an important part without effort.

Gas comes from oligosaccharides that the intestine does not digest and that gut bacteria ferment. It eases with soaking for 8 to 12 hours, discarding the soaking water, cooking uncovered and raising the amount slowly. Lentils and split peas usually sit better than beans.

## How much to eat and how to cook them

A reasonable portion is 60 to 80 grams dry per person, two or three times a week. Canned legumes are already cooked: rinse them to lower the sodium and add them at the end of the stew.

Salt can go in at the start without fear: it does not harden the legume appreciably. What is worth avoiding during cooking is acid, because it slows softening. Lemon or vinegar go in at the end.

## Soy and phytoestrogens without alarm

Soy contains isoflavones, compounds with weak oestrogenic activity. In humans, usual food intake has not shown effects on testosterone or male fertility in the available studies. The evidence covers food consumption, not high doses of isolated supplements.

Tofu, tempeh, edamame and soy drink are practical ways to add protein. The guide to [soy protein versus milk protein](/en/soy-vs-milk-protein/) compares the two options, and the one on [vegan protein](/en/vegan-protein/) covers blends when food falls short.

## How legumes fit into a training diet

Legumes do not sit apart from your goals. They provide slow-digesting carbohydrate, protein and fibre, and they fit a cutting diet if the daily total is counted. The guide to the [ideal diet for weight loss](/en/ideal-diet-for-weight-loss/) and the one on [calories for muscle gain](/en/calories-for-muscle-gain/) show how to balance the plate.

A plate of lentils with rice and a little chicken or egg covers quality protein, carbohydrate and fibre. The page on [juicy chicken](/en/juicy-chicken/) explains how to cook it without drying it out. Anyone raising protein without relying on meat finds half the work in the legume and the other half in the grain.

The usual mistake is serving them with cured sausage and plenty of bread, or saving them for the heavy winter stew. In summer they work in salad, in a cold soup or sautéed with vegetables. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) clarifies how much training requires, and [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) remind you that protein powder does not replace a plate of legumes.
