Cutting a fruit opens the door to two processes that were already there: oxygen reaches the flesh and enzymes start working. The visible result is a browning apple or banana; the invisible one is a slow loss of vitamins. The good news is that the loss is smaller than the appearance suggests.

## What happens when you cut fruit

The cut breaks cells and releases enzymes that oxidise phenolic compounds. Those compounds turn into brown pigments, and the colour changes within minutes. Vitamin C also oxidises with light and air, though more slowly than the browning.

Cold slows both reactions. At 5 °C the enzymes work slowly and nutrient loss stretches over time. At room temperature the same fruit spoils in hours.

## How much vitamin C is lost

A team at CEBAS-CSIC studied melon, kiwi, mango, pineapple, strawberry and watermelon, cut and stored at 5 °C. They measured vitamin C, carotenoids and polyphenols over nine days. At six days, vitamin C loss depended on the fruit.

The overall range lands between 5 and 25 % for vitamin C and between 10 and 25 % for carotenoids. Melon is the most sensitive fruit on the list, and kiwi holds its pigments best.

## Carotenoids and polyphenols

Carotenoids are the pigments that colour watermelon, melon and mango. In the study they were lost somewhat faster than vitamin C, except in kiwi, which barely changed.

Polyphenols stayed the same in every fruit across the nine days. That is the reassuring part: most of the phenolic compounds are still there even when the colour fades.

## Looks change before nutrition

From day six, most of the fruit in the study no longer looked appetising, except mango and watermelon. The analysis, however, still showed an antioxidant content very close to the starting point.

That gap between what you see and what is measured explains why fruit cut the day before is not a problem. If it smells fine, is not slimy and was kept cold, it is still fruit with almost all its nutrients.

## Storing it at home

The first measure is cold. Cut fruit goes into the fridge in a sealed container, not on the counter. The second is air: the less empty space left, the slower the browning.

A squeeze of lemon helps with the colour of apple and avocado, because acid slows the enzyme that darkens them. It does not restore lost vitamin C, and it is not needed for fruit that already stores well.

Cutting large pieces helps too. Fruit in big chunks exposes less surface to oxygen than the same fruit diced small, and it keeps better until the next day.

## Cut fruit from the shop

Minimally processed fruit goes through a wash, a dry and a cold pack. The container blocks light and limits oxygen, and the cold chain does the rest. That is why a well-kept tray can arrive in good nutritional shape.

Check the packing date and the temperature of the display. A tray that has spent hours out of the fridge loses texture and flavour, and the food safety risk rises even if the colour holds.

## More vitamin C than an orange, with caveats

Strawberries beat oranges in vitamin C per 100 grams. The comparison changes when you look at a whole piece: an orange weighs far more than a strawberry and wins on the total per serving.

Kiwi also sits high in vitamin C and delivers less sugar per 100 grams than many sweet fruits. None of these figures makes one fruit compulsory: the one you actually eat is the one that counts.
