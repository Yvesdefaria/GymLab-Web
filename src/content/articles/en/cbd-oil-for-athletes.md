## What is inside the bottle

Cannabidiol is one of the cannabinoids in *Cannabis sativa*, the same plant that produces THC. Industrial hemp, the source of almost all the CBD on sale, is subject to a legal THC limit around 0.3%. That limit is not zero. A product labelled as hemp can still carry traces of the psychoactive compound, and that is the first fact an athlete needs to understand.

The oil is not a single molecule in practice either. It carries CBD diluted in a carrier oil and, depending on the extraction, more or fewer cannabinoids, terpenes and THC residue. Two bottles with the same number on the label can hold different things.

## What the evidence supports

Prescription CBD, at high doses and under medical supervision, reduces seizure frequency in two rare childhood epilepsies, Dravet syndrome and Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. That is where the molecule is best studied and where the trials are controlled.

Anxiety, sleep and pain look different. The trials are small, last a few weeks and use doses that do not match between studies. Some find mild improvements, others find none, and the whole does not support a claim that CBD does anything consistent in a healthy person who sleeps badly or trains hard.

For muscle and recovery there is no data. No controlled trial shows more strength, more muscle mass or less muscle damage with CBD, and no known mechanism explains it either. Pages that present it as a recovery aid describe general properties of the compound, not results measured in athletes.

## What changed in 2018

The World Anti-Doping Agency removed CBD from its prohibited list in 2018. The decision covers cannabidiol, not the whole family: THC and the other cannabinoids remain banned in competition.

That is the detail that decides whether a bottle works for anyone subject to testing. Labs look for THC metabolites, not CBD, and a full-spectrum oil keeps traces of the former. The amount per dose is small, but it repeats daily and the margin depends on the batch.

## The sum the label does not do

A 10 ml bottle with 1,000 mg of CBD holds 100 mg per millilitre. If the dropper releases 0.05 ml, each drop carries 5 mg. The sum is simple and almost no package does it for you: the label shows the bottle total or the milligrams per dose.

The spectrum matters more than the number. Isolate means CBD and nothing else. Broad spectrum means CBD with other cannabinoids and no detectable THC. Full spectrum means the whole plant, THC traces included. For anyone who competes, the third one is the one that needs a batch certificate.

## The batch certificate

The certificate is the only proof that answers the right question. It has to match the batch in your hand, not the brand in general, and list the cannabinoid profile with the THC figure, plus pesticides, heavy metals, solvents and microbiology. Independent analyses of commercial products have found gaps between the CBD declared and the CBD inside; without a certificate, that gap is invisible to the buyer.

## Interactions and groups that ask first

CBD competes with liver enzymes that process other drugs, specifically CYP3A4 and CYP2C19. It can raise the concentration of anticoagulants such as warfarin, of some anti-epileptics and of immunosuppressants, and it adds drowsiness when combined with sedatives.

High prescription doses have raised liver enzymes in some patients, especially alongside valproate. At over-the-counter doses the risk looks smaller, and information on long-term use in healthy people is still missing.

It is worth asking a doctor before starting during pregnancy or breastfeeding, under 18, with diagnosed liver disease, or on any long-term medication.

## How to decide

For better sleep, for next-day muscle soreness and for performance, CBD has no support. For anyone subject to testing, the THC risk is real and avoidable. A reasonable decision comes down to two options: leave it off the list, or buy only with a batch certificate, a calculated dose per drop and a spectrum without THC.
