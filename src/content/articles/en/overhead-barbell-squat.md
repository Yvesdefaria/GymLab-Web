A bar overhead leaves no room. Any compensation a back squat hides shows up on the first rep: the bar drifts forward, the chest drops or the heel lifts. That is why the lift works so well as a screen.

## The elbows lock

The position starts with the arms. A fully extended elbow and a shoulder held in active external rotation keep the bar overhead. With a soft elbow, the bar drifts forward, the torso leans and the squat turns into a balancing act.

The lock is not arm tension. It is the position that keeps the bar above the centre of gravity while the hips drop. External rotation comes from a wide grip and the shoulder taken back and down, not from lifting the chest.

## The bar over the midfoot

With the bar in the air, the vertical reference is the midfoot. The ribs stay down, the abdomen is firm and the gaze is forward. The feet sit slightly wider than the hips, toes turned out a little.

The descent is that of a normal squat, heels down and knees tracking over the toes. The difference is that any drift of the bar is immediate, because there is no back to hold it.

## Depth is set by control

No knee angle works as a rule. The squat goes as low as the position holds: bar over the foot, elbows locked, ribs down. When one of the three breaks, the descent is over.

The 90-degree reference that appears in many manuals is a teaching approximation, not a destination. A lifter with good mobility goes lower; another stays higher and works the range patiently.

## The limit is overhead

The quadriceps is rarely what fails. The brake shows up in shoulder flexion, thoracic extension and ankle dorsiflexion. That is why the lift shows in one rep what a back squat can hide for months.

[Shoulder routines](/en/shoulder-routines/) cover the mobility and strength work that holds the position. [Exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) covers general bar control, and [range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) explains why you earn the range before you load it.

## The screen and the dose

The most useful version is one rep with a light bar, filmed from the side. Note the depth, where the bar sits relative to the foot and the first point where compensation appears. Repeating it every few weeks measures mobility progress without adding load.

As an exercise, this page's recommendation is two or three sets of 3 to 5 reps with a light load and 2 to 3 minutes of rest. The bar stays light on purpose: if the weight goes up, the position stops being the limit and the screen loses its point.

## Common mistakes

The first is extending the arms without locking the elbows and mistaking the position for a press. The second is opening the ribs and arching the lower back to get lower. The third is watching the bar instead of the front.

The fourth is loading the bar as if it were a back squat. The fifth is assuming today's depth is the permanent one; range changes with the day, the warm-up and fatigue.

## Where it fits

It belongs in the warm-up of a leg or shoulder day and in a mobility block. The dumbbell version, the [overhead dumbbell squat](/en/overhead-dumbbell-squat/), works one side at a time and exposes differences between shoulders; the bar splits the weight and demands symmetry from both.

The barbell pattern on the back is in the [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/), and neck control is in [head position in the squat](/en/squat-head-position/). The [squat safety guide](/en/squat-safety/) covers the whole pattern and its limits. For the legs, the [deep squat](/en/deep-squat/) and the [jump squat](/en/jump-squat/) complete the block.
