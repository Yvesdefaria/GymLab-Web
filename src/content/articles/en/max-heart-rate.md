From a practical point of view, heart rate is the most interesting parameter, at least for someone who practises physical activity.

Heart rate (HR) is the number of beats the heart performs in a minute and an excellent indicator of exercise intensity. It responds very quickly to any change in intensity, and a heart rate monitor gives you constant information, adjusted moment by moment.

## Factors that affect your maximum heart rate

The cardiac muscle does not only respond to changes in exercise intensity and blood flow demand. Other factors interfere with heart rate and you should keep them in mind.

### Age

As you get older, heart rate tends to drop steadily for efforts of the same intensity. Maximum heart rate also falls as age advances.

### Fitness level

The heart rate response to exercise depends on chronic adaptation to training. At the same intensity, a trained athlete has a lower heart rate than an untrained person, and recovers faster.

### Sex

A woman's heart is smaller because her body structures are smaller. Relative to body weight, the result is also lower: it is between 85 and 90% of a man's. With smaller heart chambers, it ejects less blood and its stroke volume is lower for the same oxygen demand.

A woman's heart is forced to work harder: because its capacity is smaller, it compensates by raising heart rate. That is why women, all else being equal, always have slightly higher heart rates, between 5 and 8 beats per minute more. These factors are known in advance and are taken into account in the formulas.

## Other factors that change your pulse

Some factors affect HR intermittently or occasionally depending on the conditions of each situation. In most cases they influence heart rhythm and make the monitor's information less reliable.

**Body position.** Lying down, gravity does not affect circulation through the vascular tree, venous return is favoured and heart rate falls.

**Ambient temperature.** Heat and cold cause vasodilation and vasoconstriction respectively, so heart rate changes: it rises in warm environments and drops in cold ones.

**Altitude.** As altitude increases, barometric pressure falls and so does the partial pressure of oxygen in the air. Relative hypoxia appears with a drop in arterial oxygen tension, which raises sympathetic activity and increases heart rate at rest and in response to exercise.

**Time of day.** Heart rate varies through the day because of nervous system stimulation: it is usually lower in the morning and tends to rise as the day goes on. That is why you should take your baseline HR in the morning.

**Health status.** Medical conditions, stress, sleep and fatigue alter the nervous and endocrine systems and cause variations in heart rate at rest and during exercise. In some conditions, such as anaemia, valve disease or recovery from infectious illness, heart rate during effort is higher than in healthy people at the same intensity.

**Digestion.** When you eat, blood flow is redistributed towards the small intestine for absorption. If you exercise in that state, blood demand rises and HR is higher.

**Tobacco, drugs, stimulants and medication.** Beta-blockers inhibit the cardiac response. Stimulants such as caffeine or ephedrine activate the sympathetic central nervous system and raise HR.

**Previous exercise.** High-intensity exercise, such as strength training, stimulates the sympathetic nervous system. Catecholamines are released and the cardiac muscle is stimulated, so HR is slightly higher after a weights session.

## What maximum heart rate is for

From HRmax you can calculate the heart rate you need for the work you want to do. If your maximum heart rate is 190 and you want to work in the middle aerobic zone, from 60% to 70%, you need to be at 115 to 130 beats per minute.

Several equations let you estimate HRmax without testing it through maximal overexertion. They range from the simplest, which need no equipment, to the most reliable, which require monitored exercise tests.

## General formula: 220 minus your age

The general formula (Fox and Haskell) is the most used and the least reliable. Subtract your age in years from the fixed figure of 220. For example: 220 - 30 years = a maximum heart rate of 190.

It has several drawbacks. It ignores your starting point: having 70 resting beats is not the same as 50 when you set your working heart rate. It also ignores sex: women have higher average heart rates, so their pulses for a given workload should be slightly higher.

- Subject A's pulse for work at 60% = 220 - 30 = 190 at 60% = **114**.
- Subject A's pulse for work at 90% = 220 - 30 = 190 at 90% = **171**.

Even so, the formula works with a few adjustments. Women should subtract a figure around 225 or 228 from their age, and depending on the activity should add between 10 and 20 pulses to the working figure.

For example: in the previous case, 220 - 30 = 190 at 60% = 114, add 15 for being a woman and you get **129**. And 220 - 30 = 190 at 90% = 171, add 15 and you get **186**.

Anyone who has played sport knows that this second calculation is closer to real work.

## Other formulas for calculating HRmax

These equations come from different American universities, and the last one is presented by Pedro Ángel López Miñarro in his book.

- HRmax = 205.8 - (0.685 × age in years)
- HRmax = 206.3 - (0.771 × age in years)
- HRmax = 217 - (0.85 × age in years)
- HRmax = 208 - (0.7 × age in years)

None of them separates women from men, although some propose a difference of 5 pulses either way when calculating intensities from the equation result.

There is another formula by sex that requires knowing the subject's weight.

- Men: HRmax = ((210 - (0.5 × age in years)) - 1% of weight) + 4
- Women: HRmax = (210 - (0.5 × age in years)) - 1% of weight

With HRmax known, you can set the pulse ranges for each intensity through a direct percentage or with the Karvonen method.

## Direct calculation of maximum heart rate

Warm up well, stretch for a few minutes and then sprint, meaning a run at your maximum speed for two and a half to three minutes. Take your pulse for six seconds at the end of the test, wait about five seconds and take it again.

This is the most direct and simplest way to find your maximum pulse, but it is not advisable at all for people with little or no fitness. If that is your case, try a sprint of thirty or forty seconds.

You will finish with a very high pulse, so counting it over just six seconds leaves a large margin of error. If you count over fifteen seconds, that is enough time for the body to start recovering and the value will not be exact either. Using a heart rate monitor makes the whole thing much easier.

## Assessing heart rate after training

When your cardiovascular session ends, the monitor keeps giving you very useful information that you often ignore. Get used to watching how your pulse recovers and how many calories you spent by the end of the session.

The higher your fitness level, the faster heart rate recovers after effort. The cardiac recovery index in the second minute after effort seems to be a useful marker to assess aerobic training status.

An acceptable level of recovery occurs when your HR is below the lower threshold. Watching how long it takes you to reach that recovery HR is very useful.

It is worth assessing the kinetics of your HR against the same exercise every so often. By watching how it evolves you get valuable information about whether you are improving cardiovascularly and whether your body is producing the adaptations needed to keep progressing.

If you are a beginner, you will see big changes. As your fitness improves, the changes become less significant: at the start the goal is to improve, while in trained states the goal is to maintain.

If over several weeks or months you take less time to recover, you are improving. It is a clear sign that your cardiovascular adaptations and fitness are growing. If in recent sessions you take longer than usual to reach that recovery HR, you may be overtrained and not recovering properly. It may be time to give your body a rest.
