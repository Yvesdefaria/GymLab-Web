Almost everywhere there is a race or a cross-country event to sign up for. The ideal move is to pick a couple of events between 5 and 10 km to get a taste for the bib, and close the year at one of the many San Silvestre races, which mix sport and leisure in a festive atmosphere.

Those end of year races are the debut as runners for hundreds of people. The most common mistake when preparing them is wanting to recover your best form in a few weeks: that only brings fatigue, a likely injury and a mental rejection of training.

With two or three months ahead, the key is to raise the load very slowly, adding minutes to your runs or intensity to the pace little by little.

The goal of this guide is to finish the event, not to chase a time or a place. That last part needs a personalised plan that you will not find here.

## How many days a week to train for a popular race

Three days a week is enough, and they should alternate. Monday, Wednesday and Friday or Saturday works, and so does Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday or Sunday.

That split leaves a rest day between sessions, which is what the body needs to absorb the work. If you come from another sport or want to combine running with weights, the guide to [training planning](/en/training-planning/) helps you fit everything into the week.

## How long each session should be

35 or 40 minutes of continuous jogging is enough for the first two weeks. From there, add five minutes each week until you can run for an hour without stopping.

If you are completely sedentary, the starting point is different: first manage 30 continuous minutes of running. Without that base, talking about intervals or paces is skipping stages.

## The right pace

Intensity should be very easy at first. After the first month, when minutes and kilometres come easily, pick one day to run at a livelier pace or to add pace changes.

A simple example: jog 20 easy minutes as a warm-up, run the next 15 at a lively pace that is not your limit but does demand effort, and finish with another 10 or 15 very easy minutes to cool down.

You can also do it progressively: 20 easy minutes, 10 or 15 at a medium pace and the last 10 or 15 harder. Or try a fartlek after the warm-up, with sets of 3 hard minutes followed by 2 easy ones and so on, without stopping, to keep working aerobically. Either way, finish with 10 or 15 minutes of easy jogging to bring things down.

Pace changes draw on anaerobic power. If you want to know how that capacity is measured, the [anaerobic power test](/en/anaerobic-power-test/) explains it. To add intervals without overdoing it, the [HIIT guide](/en/hiit-training-guide/) sets ratios and progressions.

## Strength, upper body and flexibility

While you add intensity and duration to your running, do not forget basic elasticity and flexibility work, especially after a run. That way the muscles recover, you avoid injuries and you slow the atrophy caused by a movement as repetitive as the running stride.

It also pays to keep general tone and a light strengthening of the groups that work less when you run. For the trunk and arms, dips, push-ups and pull-ups are the handiest exercises, and you can do them anywhere.

If you are willing to pick up weights, a pair of dumbbells at home is enough to strengthen biceps, triceps, chest and shoulders. Use light weight at first, whatever you can handle with confidence, and put technical execution above everything. Anyone who wants to go further with strength work should get help from a specialist: in any gym, the floor coach teaches you to do things properly.

Stretching after the run deserves its own chapter. The guide to [stretching](/en/stretching/) sorts out which ones to do and when.

## How to train your abs

Working the rectus abdominis should be sacred for any athlete, and for a runner even more so. A strong pelvic girdle prevents a lot of back trouble, the area that causes the most sick leave in the modern world.

The ideal is to get advice from a coach or a sports technician. One of the most common mistakes is anchoring your feet on a wall bar, a piece of furniture or under the sofa.

The legs stay bent, but the feet do not rest on anything. When lifting the trunk towards the knees, raising it about 30 degrees is enough. No bringing the chest to the knees: past that angle other muscles join in and the work can backfire, because the abs only work on their own up to that degree of lift.

## What to wear when it is cold

When the cold bites you need to wrap up and, at the same time, take off wet clothing, especially the first layers, as soon as the session ends.

If temperatures are very low, cover your throat with a scarf or something similar. Keeping your hands warm with gloves also reduces the feeling of cold.

## What to look for in your gear

When it comes to training and racing kit, the range of brands, tiers and prices is enormous. What you should never give up is a minimum of textile quality on race day, so you do not end up with chafing.

That includes socks, where it does not pay to skimp, and above all shoes, which should be a priority to avoid injuries.

The big sports brands make good running shoes. Look for cushioning and stability ahead of lightness, which is what competition shoes prioritise. For the vast majority of people, a pair of training or mixed shoes is enough to enjoy a race.

Anyone chasing more performance, with no physical problems or a slim build, can explore competition shoes, which are thinner, lighter and have barely any protection.

## A practical plan to finish your race

To sum up: pick an event between 5 and 10 km, set aside two or three months and run three alternate days. Start with 35 or 40 minutes of continuous jogging and add five minutes a week until you can run for an hour.

Add one day of pace changes after your first month, work the strength of your trunk and arms, look after your abs with the technique described and check your shoes before race day.

With those ingredients, finishing the event and enjoying the road stop being a lottery.
