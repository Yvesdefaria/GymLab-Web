## How to set it up

Place an adjustable bench at about 30 degrees between two low pulleys, the same distance from each. Set the bench slightly in front of the line between the pulleys so the cables stay loaded even when the hands meet at the top.

Clip a handle to each pulley and sit with your back on the pad. The shoulders should line up with the pulleys, not above or below them. Take both handles with the palms facing each other and reach out over the chest with a small bend in the elbows.

That bend stays fixed for the whole set. The elbow neither opens nor closes to press: it is a lever that transmits what the shoulder does.

## The line of pull: why low pulleys

With a dumbbell on an incline bench, resistance pulls towards the floor through the whole range. The top of the movement loses tension, which is exactly where the chest contracts fully.

With two low pulleys, resistance pulls up and back. The vector changes along the range and tension holds at the top too. That is the real difference between the incline cable fly and a dumbbell fly.

The work concentrates on the clavicular portion, the upper chest, the same region the incline press targets. The two heads are described in [chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/), and the general case for cables is in the [cable exercise benefits](/en/cable-exercise-benefits/) page.

## Execution: fixed elbows, hands that meet

From the extended position, open the arms out to the sides along a wide arc until the chest stretches. The elbow keeps the same bend and the wrists do not rotate.

On the way back, trace the same arc and bring the hands together in front of the chest. Do not cross the wrists or pass one hand in front of the other: crossing adds internal rotation at the shoulder and does nothing for the chest.

Squeeze for a second at the point of peak contraction, hands together and elbows slightly below shoulder line. Return slowly, keeping the cable under control.

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. This is an isolation movement: pick a load that lets every rep follow the same arc.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **Using a flat bench.** The setup collapses: with a horizontal torso the low pulleys pull from behind and the arc changes. Without the incline it is another exercise.
- **Crossing the hands.** It adds rotation at the shoulder and shortens the useful range.
- **Turning it into a press.** If the elbow extends on the way up, the triceps join and the chest loses the arc.
- **Shrugging the shoulders.** The shoulder blades stay down and against the bench.
- **Letting the stack drop on the way back.** The eccentric is half the exercise.
- **Too much weight.** With too much load the arc shortens and the movement becomes a press.

## Where it belongs in the session

After the presses, once the chest is worked and heavy load is no longer the point. The fly finishes the job with constant tension and does not compete with the press for weight.

It also works as a first movement in a chest session with little pressing volume, or as a partner to the [Smith machine incline press](/en/smith-machine-incline-press/) when the upper chest gets two different stimuli.

What it does not do is change which fibres of the chest work. The muscle has two heads and both contract on every rep. Position changes the angle the tension comes from and the range it travels, not an isolated part of the muscle.

## Progression and variations

The normal progression is load: two more kilos on each pulley once 15 reps hold the same arc and the full pause. Then tempo: three seconds down and one second of contraction.

The single-arm version allows more range and more torso rotation, and asks for more control. The standing version with high pulleys changes the line of pull and works another part of the range.
