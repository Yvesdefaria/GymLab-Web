Understanding the differences and knowing when to apply each grip improves your training and your overall performance in the gym. It also helps you choose variations with a clear purpose when you plan your routine.

## Differences between the pronated and supinated grip

The **pronated grip**, also called a pronation grip, holds the bar or the dumbbells with the palms facing down. The **supinated grip**, or supination grip, is performed with the palms facing up.

A trick to remember the term: imagine you want to eat soup with your hand. Soup, supinated. Think about how you place your hand to hold that soup and you have the supinated grip. The pronated grip is the opposite.

These variations change more than hand position. They also alter the mechanics of the muscles involved and the way the weight is distributed through the exercise. If you want to compare more variations, see the guide to [lat pulldown grips](/en/lat-pulldown-grips/).

## Pronated grip: muscles and stability

- **Muscles worked:** the upper back dominates, with the traps and rhomboids, plus the forearm muscles.
- **Stability:** it offers greater stability in pulling exercises such as rows and pull-ups, thanks to the more natural wrist position.

## Supinated grip: muscles and stability

- **Muscles worked:** it emphasises the biceps and the lower back, which makes it ideal for exercises such as the biceps curl and some row variations.
- **Stability:** it is less stable in pulling exercises, but it allows a wider range of motion in pushing exercises.

## Benefits of the pronated grip

The pronated grip is used in a wide range of exercises because of its many benefits:

1. **Better muscle activation:** it activates the upper and middle back muscles more effectively, which makes it ideal for rows and pull-ups.
2. **Greater wrist stability:** the pronated position is more ergonomic for the wrists and reduces the risk of injury during heavy lifts.
3. **Better posture:** by working the postural muscles hard, it helps correct imbalances and improve overall posture.

## Benefits of the supinated grip

The supinated grip, though less common in some exercises, offers specific advantages you can use in certain routines:

1. **Greater biceps activation:** it places more emphasis on the biceps, which makes it ideal for exercises that aim to develop this muscle group.
2. **Better grip strength:** by involving more of the forearm and hand muscles, it can noticeably improve grip strength.
3. **Exercise versatility:** it allows a wider variety of movement in pushing and pulling exercises, with more options for customising your routines.

## Exercises with a pronated grip

- **Pronated pull-ups:** they focus more on the lats and rhomboids.
- **Barbell row:** it increases activation of the upper and middle back.
- **Pronated-grip deadlift:** it improves stability and reduces tension in the wrists.

Technique matters more than strength. Before you load the bar, review how to perform [pull-ups](/en/pull-ups/) and the [barbell row](/en/barbell-row/).

## Exercises with a supinated grip

- **Barbell biceps curl:** it maximises biceps activation.
- **Inverted row:** it improves biceps activation and works the lower back.
- **Supinated pull-ups:** they increase range of motion and biceps activation.

The [inverted row](/en/inverted-row/) and the [reverse-grip barbell row](/en/reverse-grip-barbell-row/) are two simple ways to bring the supinated grip into a back day.

## How to add both grips to your routine

Including both grips can give you a more balanced and complete training programme, and it helps avoid overuse of certain muscles and muscle imbalances. Some tips for integrating them:

1. **Alternate grips:** in exercises such as rows and pull-ups, switch between the pronated and supinated grip across different sessions.
2. **Supersets:** combine pronated and supinated exercises in supersets to save time and raise the intensity.
3. **Listen to your body:** if you feel discomfort or pain with a particular grip, adjust your technique or reduce the weight until you feel comfortable.

## Neutral and alternating grip: when to use them

### Neutral (hammer) grip

The hands are placed with the palms facing each other. It is also called the hammer grip. It is typical of the hammer curl and some row exercises.

This grip is recommended in exercises such as the dumbbell bench press for people with a shoulder injury or discomfort, because it avoids internal rotation of the humerus and keeps the subacromial space open.

### Alternating grip

One hand holds the bar with a supinated grip and the other with a pronated grip. It is common in the deadlift because it lets you handle a much heavier load. Some studies question its effectiveness and recommend the pronated or supinated grip.

## How to choose the best grip for you

The choice between the pronated and supinated grip depends on your training goals and on how your body responds to each technique. Including both in your routine gives you variety, from better muscle activation to greater injury prevention.

Listen to your body and adjust your training so you move towards your goals safely and effectively.
