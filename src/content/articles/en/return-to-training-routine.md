Coming back after a layoff is not solved by repeating the old routine with less weight. Joints and tendons tolerate less than muscle, and a session that runs too long leaves damage that shows up two days later. This plan changes the unit of measure: instead of counting reps, it counts seconds.

The timer limits the set without calculating percentages to the decimal. It also blocks the classic comeback delusion: when the clock says rest, the set is over, even if two more reps were there.

## Who this routine is for

It works if you have finished rehab, if you are coming back from a resolved injury, or if months of little activity have left your fitness lower. If you have not trained for six months or more, this is not the routine: start with a beginner plan and raise the volume little by little. If recovery is still ongoing, the physiotherapist decides.

## The four-week ladder

Each set lasts one minute, split between work and rest. Week 1 is almost all work; week 4 is almost all rest and the load goes up. That is the progression: less time under load and more load.

Sets in the first three weeks are straight: the same load on every set. Week 4 uses ascending sets, each one heavier than the last.

## How the load is chosen

Week 1 stays below 50 % of the 1RM you had before the break, and 25 to 30 % is often the right place to start. From there, add 10 to 20 % per week. If your week 4 goes well past the 78 % of the old 1RM in the example, you started too high.

An example with a machine chest press and an old 1RM of 90 kg: 30 kg in week 1, 45 kg in week 2 and 60 kg in week 3. Week 4 climbs inside the session itself, from 40 to 70 kg, because those are six ascending sets. The example rises by more than that 10-20 % between weeks because it starts very low; with your real weight, respect the percentage.

The timer rules. Five seconds either way changes the set, so use a real one.

## The week-4 example

| Exercise | Sets | Load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Machine chest press | 6 | 40, 50, 55, 60, 65 and 70 kg |
| Cable flyes | 6 | ascending ladder |
| Seated cable row | 6 | ascending ladder |

Each set is 5 seconds of work and 55 of rest. Before the press, a warm-up of 8 reps with 30 kg. The final 70 kg is 78 % of the old 1RM, so the ceiling holds. Each exercise runs its own ladder, calculated from its week-3 weight.

## If you prefer counting reps

The system works the same without a stopwatch, with a rough equivalence: 22 reps in week 1, 15 in week 2, 7 in week 3 and 2 in week 4. Keep the rests and the load progression. Exercises with a long range of motion will produce fewer reps and short ones more.

## The three example sessions

Exercises run from largest to smallest and use machines or cables, which is what fits best when an area has to be rebuilt. Pick one session and repeat it two or three times a week, or alternate upper and lower body.

**Upper body.** Machine chest press, cable flyes, seated cable row, high-cable pullover, dumbbell lateral raises, dumbbell shrugs, EZ-bar cable curl and triceps pushdowns.

**Lower body and abs.** Leg press, seated leg curl, Romanian deadlift, standing calf raises, reverse crunch and weighted sit-ups.

**Full body.** Incline bench press, wide-grip cable row, overhead press, reverse flyes, dumbbell curl, rope triceps pushdowns, leg press or hack squat and goblet squat.

## What to watch in the first weeks

A session closes in an hour or less and ends by stretching the area you trained, so full range of motion returns before the load goes up. If sharp pain appears, the joint swells or the movement loses range, drop the load and get it checked. The diffuse muscle soreness of the next day belongs in the plan; the stab during the set does not.

Technique matters more than the number. A rep that bounces on the machine does not count as work. The [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) and [full range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) guides cover what is taken for granted here.

If you want to keep going after week 4, repeat the cycle with 10-20 % more load and start the ladder again at week 1. When the plan feels short, you are ready for a normal routine: the [volume routines](/en/volume-routines/) index has the next steps. The [progressive overload principle](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) explains why the load has to rise, and the [overtraining](/en/overtraining/) guide helps tell normal fatigue from the kind that asks for an easy week.
