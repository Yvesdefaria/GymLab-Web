The calf looks like one block and inside it are two muscles with different jobs. One crosses the knee; the other stays at the ankle. The knee position decides which of the two takes the set, which is why the standing or seated question has an anatomical answer.

## The knee decides the muscle

The gastrocnemius crosses the knee and the ankle. The soleus crosses only the ankle. That difference separates the two positions: bend the knee and the gastrocnemius shortens from above and stops contributing; keep the leg straight and it works through its full range.

The practical rule follows: straight leg for the gastrocnemius, bent knee for the soleus. Straight-leg work is mainly gastrocnemius; bent-knee work is mainly soleus. Neither position trains both equally.

## Standing: the gastrocnemius

Standing raises, donkey raises and the press variation with the knee extended load the gastrocnemius. It is the visible muscle, the one that draws the diamond shape behind the knee, and the one that answers straight-leg work.

The position needs a free ankle so the heel can drop below the level of the foot. Without that range, the bottom of the rep is lost and the set runs short.

## Seated: the soleus

On the seated machine the knee stays bent at about ninety degrees. The gastrocnemius relaxes and the soleus takes over plantar flexion, the movement that lifts the heel.

The soleus is the larger of the two by mass and sits under the gastrocnemius, so it stays out of sight. Its development adds density to the calf in profile and mass low down, where the gastrocnemius stops.

## Order and load

If you train both positions in the same session, start standing and finish seated. The gastrocnemius is the one that tires most under load; the soleus, used to holding the body up all day, handles the end of the session better.

On reps, the calf tolerates moderate work with heavy resistance, in the six to ten range. Sets of twenty also count, but the stimulus arrives when the load is high and the range is complete.

Hold the contraction for one or two seconds at the top and do not let the heel drop uncontrolled at the bottom. Constant tension beats speed. If you want to squeeze the last sets, the guide on [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains how to get close to the limit without breaking technique.

## The mistakes that cost sets

Bouncing at the bottom, doing half reps and letting the weight fall on the way down turn a calf set into a formality. The ankle is a small joint and the full range is already short; shorten it further and little is left to train.

The other mistake is fear of load. The calf takes kilos and needs them: it is a postural muscle that handles plenty of volume, and many routines train it too light for too long.

## How much frequency it tolerates

The soleus holds the body up on every step, so it tolerates frequent work. The gastrocnemius also recovers quickly for most lifters, which is why many plans put calves in two or three times a week.

That tolerance invites excess. If sets pile up without any weight increase for weeks, the problem usually sits in how the volume is spread rather than in the choice of machine. [Calf anatomy](/en/calf-anatomy/) explains why each muscle responds differently, and the guide on [calf growth](/en/calf-growth/) spreads the sets across the session.

## How to fit it into your routine

A complete leg plan includes both positions and does not treat them as interchangeable. If you train on machines, the guide to the [best machine exercises](/en/best-machine-exercises/) covers the equipment in turn.

The sum is simple: one standing raise and one seated raise cover both muscles. Picking only one leaves half the calf without direct stimulus, however much weight you move on the one you chose.
