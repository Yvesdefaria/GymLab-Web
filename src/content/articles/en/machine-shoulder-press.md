## The machine chooses the path

In a dumbbell press, the hand finds its own path and the shoulder adjusts the angle on every rep. On a plate machine, the handles travel the arc the mechanism imposes. The body adapts to the machine.

That rigidity is the main difference from a free-weight press, before any muscle argument. The machine picks the plane, the distance between the handles and the point where the weight feels heaviest.

## The cam profile

Plate machines with a cam change the resistance arm through the range. The cam is an off-centre piece that turns with the lever: at mid-range the line of pull sits further from the pivot and resistance grows; near lockout the arm shortens and the load lightens.

Not every plate machine has a cam. Some use a fixed pivot and the profile sits closer to a dumbbell. This page covers the plate and cam machine, the one that usually has a reclined seat and two independent arms.

## Where it wins

The stable base is the first advantage. The back stays supported, the feet are planted and the torso does not have to brake the weight. That stability lets a hard set finish with the position intact.

The second advantage is mid-range. The cam concentrates resistance where the deltoid has the most leverage, and lockout stays lighter. For anyone chasing accumulated work in the deltoid, that distribution fits.

The third is the ease of changing plates and repeating the same position. Across a set of 10 to 12 reps, the machine keeps the effort in the shoulder.

## Where it robs you

Lockout loses load. When the arms extend, the lever arm shortens and the weight stops demanding. The machine does not follow the end of the range like a dumbbell, which still weighs at the top.

The stabilisers work less. The rotator cuff and the muscles that control the scapula do not have to correct the path; a free-weight press asks them to keep the head of the humerus and the shoulder in place. That difference matters when the goal includes control, not only pushing.

The arc belongs to the machine. If the seat sits low or high, the handles miss the shoulder's range and the exercise loses travel. The machine does not adapt to each person; it has to be adjusted.

## The execution

Set the seat so the handles sit at shoulder height. Put the back against the pad and the feet on the floor. Hold the handles with the palms forward and the wrists in line with the forearms.

Press up without locking the elbows hard. Lower under control to shoulder height. If the shoulders climb towards the ears at the end of the range, the seat is too low or the weight is too heavy.

## Dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The machine allows a set close to failure from a stable position; leave one rep in reserve when the goal is volume rather than testing the limit. ## Where it fits

It works as the main press on a machine day or as the second movement after a free-weight press. It also fits at the end, when the shoulder is already tired and a stable base is welcome.
