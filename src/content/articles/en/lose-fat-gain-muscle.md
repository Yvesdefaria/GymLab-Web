The goal is to find the exact point: the most muscle you can gain with the least fat. The less fat you add during a bulk, the less you have to lose when you cut, and the more muscle you keep when the diet gets strict.

Anyone who has spent time in a gym has heard about bulking and cutting phases. Gaining some fat while chasing muscle is normal. What you can control is how much fat comes with every kilo you add.

## Training is the first step to gain muscle and lose fat

Anabolism is the state in which the body builds muscle tissue. Catabolism does the opposite: it breaks muscle down for energy. Your job is to spend as much time as possible in the first state.

Weight training is anabolic on its own. It still needs support: proper nutrition and enough rest. Weights, food and sleep push the balance towards building muscle.

Too much cardio works against you. A long endurance session puts the body in a catabolic state that favours muscle breakdown. That is why marathon runners have defined, small legs instead of big, powerful ones, and why many bodybuilders add aerobic work only when they want to drop body fat.

The first thing you need is basic, heavy training. After a session, the body moves the calories you eat into muscle cells, so they are less likely to end up as fat. If you never force the body to work harder than it is used to, it has no reason to change. The guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) covers that bulk plan in more detail. To add expenditure without taxing recovery, the [HIIT guide](/en/hiit-training-guide/) explains how to program the intervals.

## Rest: where muscle is built

You can follow the best programme in the world and eat well, but without enough rest everything else is wasted. Nutrients the body cannot use are excreted or stored as fat tissue.

Sleeping little changes the environment: what was anabolic turns catabolic. The body needs those hours to digest and use what you ate. Without them, recovery takes longer and the next session goes worse.

A deload week at the right time stops fatigue from piling up. The [deload week](/en/deload-week/) guide explains when to cut volume without losing what you gained.

## Nutrition: carbs, calories and meal count

Protein matters, but the detail that decides this phase is carbohydrate, especially what you eat right after training. A session burns glycogen, the sugar stored in muscle and liver. Keep it very low and the body breaks down muscle protein to rebuild it.

### How many meals and how many carbs

A bodybuilder usually eats five meals a day, and six when motivated. Protein, carbs and fat are split fairly evenly across them, with one exception: the meal after training.

The classic example: 40 grams of carbs per meal and six meals make 240 grams a day. The post-training meal needs double, 80 grams. To keep the total the same, take 20 grams from the meal before training and 20 from the last meal of the day. You eat the same amount, but at the moment you need it most.

### Glycaemic index: when to use each type

The glycaemic index measures how fast the body absorbs a carbohydrate. Above 70 is high, between 55 and 69 is medium, and below 55 is low.

In every meal except the one after training, choose low GI carbs. They burn slowly, keep energy steady and stop glucose and insulin from spiking, which is what favours fat storage. Good sources are sweet potato, brown rice, green leafy vegetables, pumpkin, oats, unprocessed bran and legumes.

After training you want the opposite. High GI carbs enter fast and refill glycogen while the muscle is most receptive. Bread and pasta made with processed flour work, as do white potatoes and white rice.

### How many extra calories to add

You need more calories than usual to switch on the anabolic environment. Half a kilo of muscle equals about 3500 calories. Spread that amount across the week and the body has what it needs to gain half a kilo per week.

With six meals a day the maths is simple: about 83 extra calories per meal (83.3 times 6 meals times 7 days is 3500). Keep those calories as clean as you can, because you are already eating above your normal intake.

Avoid the shortcut of 8000 calories a day. The metabolism does not adapt to that figure and the result is usually fat. If you prefer low-impact cardio, the [swimming routine](/en/swimming-workout-routine/) spreads three sessions across the week.

## How to keep body fat to a minimum

The body adjusts its ability to grow or lose weight according to the calories it usually receives. Raise or lower them and the metabolism speeds up or slows down.

The strategy is the rollercoaster. Say your average is 3500 calories a day to gain mass. On days without weights you drop to 3350 or 3250: you eat just enough to hold size and the metabolism slows a little. On training days you add the same amount, up to 3650 or 3750, and the body has more calories than it needs.

By the end of the week the average is still 3500 a day. The weekly total stays the same; what changes is how you spread it.

On days without weights, add some cardio. Two or three sessions of 30 to 45 minutes at moderate intensity are enough to keep fat away, and they improve blood flow, which helps recovery. Do not chase records: that would take recovery capacity away from your weight sessions.

## Realistic expectations and mistakes that stall progress

What you want is quality weight, which means muscle before kilos on the scale. Losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time is not easy, and it does not have to be: what you can control is how much fat comes with every kilo of muscle.

The most common mistakes are eating extra to be safe, sleeping little and making cardio the centre of the plan. Fix those three and recomposition moves forward.

With heavy training, rest and meals in order, the body has everything it needs to build muscle without filling you with fat. Time to work.
