The use of a **belt** has always been a controversial topic. It has its advantages, but at the same time it should not be worn systematically.

## What a weightlifting belt does

A **weightlifting belt** stabilises the lumbar spine in exercises where you handle high intensities and the back takes very heavy loads, for example during the squat.

In those cases it is justified to take load off the lumbar area and gain more stability. What is not beneficial is wearing it systematically.

## The risk of wearing it every session

The claim that a **lumbar belt** prevents lower-back overload is widely misunderstood. It is true that the belt increases intra-abdominal pressure and stabilises the spine, protecting it from the loads that travel through it.

However, it is a double-edged sword. The more you use it, the weaker your musculature becomes and the greater the risk of **injury** in later sessions. If you want to avoid muscle imbalances and back pain, use it as little as possible.

Your body has a **natural belt** that works like a corset: the **core**, the deep abdominal musculature (transverse abdominis and internal oblique). The spine is also surrounded by muscles whose main job is to provide stability.

Every time you put on a girdle, a corset or a **lifting belt**, you replace the action of those muscles with an external element. You stop letting them take load and condition themselves to protect the joint structures naturally.

The core becomes weaker and an imbalance appears that can end in **injury**, because it cannot stabilise the spine under heavy loads.

That is why the belt should only be used in specific situations, when you lift a lot of weight, such as the last sets of squats when you work maximum strength. Let your stabilisers work and take part in the exercise, and you will not depend on the belt for every effort.

## Advantages of a lifting belt

**Training belts** compress the spine by increasing abdominal pressure and wrapping the torso. They provide lumbar support.

They can also reduce the risk of lower-back injury and improve proprioception by increasing the perception of stability in a given lift.

There is also psychological help, a placebo effect on top of the extra proprioceptive information. That information makes you aware of your spine position, lumbar curve and abdominal tone.

## Drawbacks and risks of a lifting belt

You risk cancelling out the work of the **natural lumbar belt**. Abuse leads to atrophy of the spinal musculature and weakness in the midsection, with consequences in other sports too.

As for **injury risk**, an athlete who wears a lifting belt while injured risks a more serious injury. Believing you can lift more kilos because of it can also lead to serious injuries.

Belts can raise blood pressure. The increase in intra-abdominal and thoracic pressure during the **Valsalva manoeuvre** produces arrhythmias from reduced cardiac flow and compromises venous return.

They can also change your lifting style.

## How to choose a weightlifting belt

When you look for a belt, it is key to know the options and which one fits your needs. There are **powerlifting** belts, models for **CrossFit** and for **weightlifting**, and each type has its own characteristics.

Consider the material, thickness, closure type and level of support. A good belt improves performance, and it also protects your lumbar area during heavy lifts.

## How to know whether you need a belt

If you are wondering whether to use one, ask yourself this first:

- **Are you going to use heavy weights?** If the answer is no, you have no reason to use it. If it is yes, you may need it.
- **Do your exercises challenge the lumbar area?** If they do not, do not use it. If the area is challenged, you probably should.
- **Can you generate force with your lumbar area and the muscles of your abdominal wall?** If yes, you may not need the belt.

If you are going to use heavy loads and your exercises challenge the lumbar area, think about the following:

- Have you trained your abdominal wall and your paravertebral and lumbar musculature for long enough?
- Do you have any injury?
- Have you worked the right progression to reach those weights?
- Have you worked the technique of the exercise you are going to do with a belt?

If any answer is **no** or you are unsure, it is worth reviewing your training. You will probably look for other work options before risking that exercise that demanded the belt.

Before you use it, follow these steps:

- Work the technique of the exercises that will require the belt.
- Strengthen your midsection, lumbar area and paravertebral musculature. Activate the transverse abdominis, a muscle often forgotten in training.
- Follow the load progression correctly in the exercises that demand the belt.

And even if at this point you think you can use it and lift the heaviest loads of your life, one detail remains: you must introduce the **belt** gradually. You cannot wear it from day one and pull like an animal.

Remember that it can compromise blood flow, raise blood pressure and make breathing harder. Using it also requires planning a progression to reach optimal training.

With all of this, you now have the arguments to decide whether to use it in your programme.
