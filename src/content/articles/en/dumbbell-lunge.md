The lunge is the easiest unilateral pattern to set up and one of the easiest to ruin. With a dumbbell in each hand, the exercise comes down to a step, a descent and a push. Three details separate a useful rep from wasted time: the direction of the step, the position of the torso and how far you go down.

## The real advantage

Loading one leg without a rack has a cost and a benefit. The cost is that dumbbells cap the weight long before a barbell does. The benefit is that the spine does not take the compression of a load resting on the traps, and the exercise fits at home with two weights and a hallway.

Dumbbells also force you to stabilise the load at your sides, so the grip and the trunk work differently than with a bar. That extra demand shows up in the first month and fades as the body adapts.

## How to do it

1. Stand with the feet hip-width apart and a dumbbell in each hand.
2. Step forward with one leg. The heel lands first and the foot settles flat.
3. Lower by bending the front knee until the thigh is close to parallel. The knee travels over the foot, in line with the second toe, and the torso stays upright.
4. The rear knee moves towards the floor without touching it; the rear foot rests on the ball.
5. Push through the front foot to return, without jumping. Switch legs.

The step is measured by the result: if the front knee shoots far past the foot on the way down or the heel lifts, the step is too short or the ankle is out of range.

## The limit of the range

Ninety degrees describes a common position, not a safety rule. The useful reference is the pelvis: go down as far as it stays stable and the torso stays upright. For many people that lands at thigh parallel; for others, slightly below.

Going past 90 degrees under control does not break anything by itself. What ruins the rep is reaching the bottom with a rounded back, a lifted heel or a knee caving inward. When any of those three signals appears, the range is over.

## Common errors

The first is leaning the torso. The chest drops forward on the way down and the weight shifts to the front foot, so the quadriceps lose part of the work and the lower back gains it. Keep the ribs down and the eyes forward.

The second is a short step. The front knee travels far ahead and the lunge becomes a narrow squat with one leg in front.

The third is letting the knee fall inward. It loses the line with the foot and the work is shared badly; push in the direction of the second toe and keep the arch of the foot.

The fourth is pushing with the rear leg. The rear heel does the work and the front quadriceps stops halfway.

The fifth is dropping the weight on the way down. The eccentric half is half the exercise, and letting it fall turns a set into half a set.

With a bar you chase load; with dumbbells, control and access. Neither is better in absolute terms, and a plan can use both in different weeks.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose is three sets of eight to ten reps per leg, with ninety seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this recommendation is the guide's and matches the rest of the unilateral leg work.

Alternate legs within the set or finish one side and switch, depending on what lets you keep the technique. Alternating keeps the heart rate up; going side by side lets you correct position without rushing.

## Where it fits

The dumbbell lunge works as unilateral work after the squat or the leg press, and as the main lift at home. It also works as a second movement on leg day when no bar is available.
