> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. If the pain is sharp, arrives after a snap at the back of the ankle or you cannot rise onto your toes, get advice before you keep training.

The Achilles tendon is the cord you can feel behind the ankle, just above the heel. It joins the gastrocnemius and the soleus to the calcaneus and passes the force you produce when you walk, run or jump down to the foot. In a jump, that transmission multiplies your body weight several times over.

## What Achilles tendinopathy is

It is tendon pain that shows up when load grows faster than the tissue can handle. The tendon thickens, loses elasticity and protests in the movements that stretch it under tension: pushing off when you walk, the running stride, the heel raise.

It does not take a dramatic cause. Add running volume over a few weeks, change the surface, break in a very stiff shoe or put jump sets into a routine that was already stretched thin.

## Why it appears

The original article points to three factors that keep repeating:

- **Repeated overload.** Running or jumping more than you were doing. Tendons tolerate a familiar stimulus well and a sudden jump badly.
- **Tight calves.** A shortened calf keeps more tension on the tendon with every stride.
- **Worn or too stiff footwear.** A shoe that has lost its cushioning changes how force is shared across the foot.

You see it in the gym in anyone who combines running and weights without counting the volume of both. The leg does not tell a squat set apart from one more kilometre.

## How it feels

The usual signs are these:

- Stiffness and pain on the first steps of the morning, which ease once you move around.
- Pain at the back of the ankle, between two and six centimetres above the heel.
- Discomfort when you push off: stairs, rising onto your toes, taking off in a run.
- A tendon that feels thicker to the touch, sometimes with a small nodule.

As it progresses, it hurts to walk. In severe cases a rupture can follow, and that consult does not wait.

## How long recovery takes

Longer than you would like. The tendon works on every step, so there is no position where it rests completely, and recovery is measured in weeks or months rather than days.

Most cases resolve by adjusting activity and building strength. A small share ends up in surgery, especially with a rupture or when the problem becomes chronic. That decision is medical and comes after everything else has been tried.

## What to do while it hurts

The first step is to reduce load, not remove it. Cut running volume, shorten the stride, switch to the bike or the pool for a few days and keep the strength work that does not spike the pain.

The treatment with the evidence behind it is progressive loading: calf and soleus strength work, starting from comfortable positions with small increases. The full protocol, with its phases and its numbers, lives in the guide to [common gym injuries](/en/common-gym-injuries/). This page does not repeat it on purpose.

Anti-inflammatories relieve the symptom and do not repair the tendon. They are for getting through a bad day, not for a plan.

Ice, if you use it, is for comfort: a few minutes on the area after a hard session or at the peak of a flare. It does not speed up repair and it does not replace loading work.

Crutches are left for severe cases and they are a medical decision, not something you decide on your own.

## How to prevent it

- **Warm up before you run or jump.** A few minutes of easy cardio and ankle mobility are enough to get the tendon warm. It is covered in [warm-up and stretching](/en/gym-warm-up/).
- **Raise volume by weeks.** If you add kilometres or jumps, add them one at a time.
- **Train the calf.** Standing heel raises with a straight knee for the gastrocnemius; seated, with a bent knee, for the soleus.
- **Stretch without bouncing.** The [stretch](/en/stretching/) for the calf helps if you keep it gentle and held.

## When to get advice

Ask for an assessment if the pain does not improve in two or three weeks, if it grows even though you lowered the load, if a clear lump appears on the tendon or if you cannot rise onto your toes. The guide on [when to see a sports doctor](/en/when-to-see-a-sports-doctor/) covers what to expect from the appointment.

Heel pain has a frequent neighbour: [plantar fasciitis](/en/plantar-fasciitis/). They share the area and little else; that page explains why.
