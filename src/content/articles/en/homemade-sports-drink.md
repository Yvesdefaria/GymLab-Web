Water covers almost every session. The mix of water, sugar and salt comes into play when training runs long, the heat is high or you sweat for hours. Telling one case from the other saves you from buying powders you do not need and from mixing a drink saltier than your body asks for.

## When water is enough

Up to 60 or 90 minutes, water. Strength training is intermittent, with rest between sets, and fluid loss rarely reaches the point of compromising the session. A normal meal and water afterwards replace what was used.

Heat and duration change that. Losing 1 to 2 % of body weight in fluid is enough to notice less strength and worse endurance, and anyone training in the sun or stacking two sessions a day reaches that point sooner. The guide to [hydration for training](/en/hydration-for-training/) explains how to estimate the loss by weighing yourself before and after.

## What sweat takes with it

Sweat is water with sodium, and smaller amounts of potassium and magnesium. Sodium concentration varies a lot from one person to the next and shifts with acclimatisation, so there is no universal figure.

For a long, hot session, the practical reference is 300 to 600 milligrams of sodium per hour. Sodium helps retain the water you drink and is part of the body's fluid balance. There is a limit on the other side too: drinking several litres of plain water over many hours can dilute blood sodium, especially in very slow efforts. The guide to [limiting sodium](/en/how-to-limit-sodium/) explains where the balance sits for the rest of the day.

## The base recipe, for one litre

- 1 litre of water
- 20 to 30 grams of sugar, about two level tablespoons
- a quarter teaspoon of salt, about 1.5 grams
- the juice of half a lemon

Dissolve the sugar and salt in a little warm water, add the rest of the cold water and the lemon, and keep the bottle in the fridge. The result sits around 2 to 3 % sugar and provides about 600 milligrams of sodium per litre, within the 300 to 600 milligram per hour range.

The lemon adds flavour and makes the drink something you want to reach for. If the mix tastes sweet, dilute it with another 500 millilitres of water. On a humid, hot day that extra dilution helps.

## How much carbohydrate and sodium per hour

Past 75 or 90 minutes, the body welcomes a steady supply. The range that works for most people is 30 to 60 grams of carbohydrate and 300 to 600 milligrams of sodium per hour.

At 30 grams of sugar per litre you cover the low end of that range. Reaching 60 grams per hour means doubling the concentration or drinking more volume, and it is worth testing in training before using it in an event.

## Mistakes that keep coming back

The tablespoon of salt shows up in many recipes. One level tablespoon weighs about 18 grams and provides close to 7 grams of sodium, more than three times the daily reference for an adult. That much salt does not improve hydration and throws the rest of the day out of balance.

Guarana is another common addition. It supplies caffeine, not electrolytes, and its role in a hydration drink is questionable. If you want caffeine for performance, it makes more sense to treat it as a separate supplement and check dose and timing, as the guide to [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) explains.

The rule of drinking every 15 minutes also falls short as a standard. It ignores thirst, temperature and each person's sweat rate, and drinking large volumes at once causes stomach discomfort. Sipping at intervals and adjusting to how you feel works better.

## When you do not need one

A 45 to 60 minute weights session does not need a sports drink. Neither does anyone who finishes and eats within the next two hours. In those cases, water and a plate with carbohydrate, protein and salt cover the same ground.

The drink earns its place in continuous efforts longer than ninety minutes, in heat and in sports with hours of sweating. For very long or high-intensity sessions, [concentrated carbohydrate drinks](/en/vitargo/) are another option, and that guide explains when they have an edge. If training happens in high temperatures, the guide to [training in hot weather](/en/training-in-hot-weather/) covers timing and volume.

## Fitting it into the day

Mix the bottle the night before, keep it cold and take small sips before and during the session. When you finish, water and your usual meal. The sports drink is a tool for one specific session, not an all-day beverage and not a substitute for eating.
