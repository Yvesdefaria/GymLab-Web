The general theory of adaptation says that an organism placed under a stimulus adapts to that stimulus. The Bulgarian method takes that idea into weightlifting and pushes it: train with high frequency and high intensity, cut the exercises down to the ones that matter and adjust the load without scheduled rest phases.

## What the Bulgarian method is

The Bulgarian method is the system used by Bulgaria's national weightlifting team during its dominant era. It is defined by very high frequency, a high volume of work at percentages close to maximum and extreme specificity: few exercises, chosen for their relationship to the competition lifts.

It was not a static system of rules and fixed numbers. Abadjiev adjusted training constantly to make it work better, and the exercise menu shrank over time. That capacity to adjust is as much part of the method as the intensity.

## Origin in the Bulgarian school

In the 1970s and 1980s, the Bulgarian national team was one of the most dominant in weightlifting. No other team produced medals and world champions with such consistency.

Bulgaria had a population of 8 million, far below the United States, the Soviet Union or China. Bulgaria had a much smaller talent base and still dominated the podium and placed its lifters at the top of the world rankings every year.

Ivan Abadjiev was the head coach. His methods were unheard of at the time: athletes trained twice a day, six days a week. There were no light days, and a very high percentage of weekly sessions demanded a limit weight: 95% of the best snatch, 97% of the best clean and jerk and 97% of the best front squat. The reps at those weights were decided by the coach in the training hall.

The athletes competed far more often than their rivals, and those competitions were an integral part of the training cycle. There was also no end to the competitive period with a corresponding off-season, which was common in the rest of athletics.

The methodology was radical and different enough to become the Bulgarian method. Specificity was the compass: only the content with the highest correlation to the competition lifts was trained. That included front and back squats, high pulls from the snatch and the clean, and the snatch and clean and jerk themselves.

## The traditional method

The traditional view holds that a muscle needs 48 to 72 hours of rest between two training sessions. The theory behind it is that muscle fibres are damaged in training and take that time to repair and grow stronger.

It is also accepted that, when the neuromuscular system is pushed too hard with constant heavy training, the central nervous system inhibits motor unit function to protect the body from injury caused by excessive fatigue. That neuromuscular inhibition is a protective mechanism: it prevents damage to connective tissue and joints when tension is produced too often. The combination of physiological and psychological distress signals with reduced neuromuscular function is known as overtraining syndrome.

The accepted training model was based on periodisation. The idea is that the body can be pushed through stimuli and that, through progressive periods of variation and increased load, the organism reaches a higher level of performance. The general traditional model starts with a phase of increasing volume at low intensity, then raises intensity and lowers volume. Loads are selected by phase according to the quality being trained, whether muscular endurance, power or strength, and a recovery or inactivity period follows competition.

## Abadjiev's method

Traditional periodised training produces improvements and remains effective. Abadjiev's system, by contrast, contradicts the accepted principles of periodisation in almost every respect.

The Bulgarian coach read adaptation differently: he recognised the human body's capacity to adapt to a stressor. In the 1960s, a group of proteins was observed in animals recovering from a period of famine, proteins that were crucial in muscle gene expression. The same proteins appeared in animals subjected to physical stress.

On that basis, Abadjiev set his principle: constantly stress the body and mind up to a point where the organism can adapt. That raises the ceiling of the athlete's maximum capacity and forces the body to adapt. The aim is to push the body into a state of tension and response that lifts it to a new adapted state.

That state is reached with intense and frequent training, in large amounts, and with competitions built into the cycle as a psychological stressor. Abadjiev believed that the proteins generated in response to tension in hungry or exhausted animals accelerated muscle function, and that inducing a similar state in humans through training that was both more intense and more voluminous would allow adaptation to that new state.

A more recent study at Mid West state university trained experienced weightlifters once or twice a day, six days a week, at high percentages of their maximums, very similar to Abadjiev's system. In the first week, the lifters showed the expected effects: muscle soreness, an initial drop in performance and a general feeling of fatigue. After that first week, they no longer experienced the pronounced soreness or physical fatigue, and performance improved noticeably: they lifted higher maximal weights and described heavy attempts as calmer.

After six weeks, the lifters were taken out of high-intensity training and trained at very light percentages or not at all. One week later, when they returned, they experienced fatigue and a drop in performance. Two observations follow. First, the lifters could not perform well under the frequent, heavy stimulus until their bodies changed in a tangible way, that is, until they adapted. Second, the state adapted to high frequency and intensity lasted while the stimulus continued or increased.

Abadjiev summed it up with an image. When a rabbit is chased by a wolf, the wolf has no preparation phase before acting: it may hide in the bushes, but it is ready to chase at full speed at any moment. For him, reaching exceptional results through hard work and then stopping to return to a lower level made no sense.

His idea of recovery was different too. Allowing the body to recover meant, in his view, letting all physiological functions reset, which leads to a state where the muscles and neuromuscular function are not ready to react to a stimulus. Removing the intense stimulus takes the athlete back to a lower level of performance.

The system influenced weightlifting methodology around the world. Athletes at the Colorado Springs Olympic training centre often describe training with a modified Bulgarian system. The successful Turkish national team trains with a very similar system, with special emphasis on specificity: its most experienced lifters switched from back squats to front squats because of their higher correlation with the clean and jerk. Several Turkish athletes, including Halil Mutlu and Naim Suleymanoglu, were Bulgarian citizens and competed for Bulgaria until they defected to their ethnic homeland.

## How to apply the Bulgarian method

Copying the volume of a national team makes no sense for a regular lifter. What you can take are the principles, and only with enough of a base.

- **Specificity:** cut the exercise menu down to the ones that resemble your goal. In weightlifting, the snatch, the clean and jerk and the squats lead; in strength training, the big lifts and their variants.
- **High frequency:** the method repeats the same pattern many times a week. That frequency demands stable technique and a body that can handle the work.
- **High intensity:** a significant share of sessions use percentages close to maximum. It is not a plan of light sets with high reps.
- **Constant adjustment:** there is no fixed number of sets and reps for the whole cycle. The coach adjusted the load according to the athlete's response, and you can do the same with a record of weights and how you feel.
- **Compete or test:** adding competitions or tests to the cycle introduces a psychological stressor and shows whether adaptation has happened.
- **Removing the stimulus has a cost:** adaptation lasts while training continues or increases. Stop completely and performance falls.

That last point explains why the method does not fit a long off-season. It also explains why a maximum-strength plan needs continuity and why you should review your [training volume](/en/training-volume/) before increasing frequency.

## Who it suits and who it does not

The method was applied to experienced athletes with an adequate background in the sport, who had started training as children and had developed the basic strength and structural resilience of muscles and connective tissue, plus the psychological toughness to handle Abadjiev's demands.

It is not a starting point. A beginner needs to learn technique, build a base and tolerate work before considering the frequency and intensity of the Bulgarian national team. It is also a poor fit for anyone who cannot train twice a day, sleep and eat accordingly, or who arrives with accumulated injuries.

The criticism is well known. Abadjiev was called the butcher. The Bulgarian team's injury rate was high compared with other teams, and its athletes had the shortest competitive careers: many were removed after an injury or an accumulation of injuries. The human body adapts to an environment or a set of stimuli, but only up to a point.

Many critics consider it physiologically impossible to survive that tension without anabolic steroids or other banned substances. It is well known that world-class weightlifters are supported with supplements and recovery aids by coaches, nutritionists and pharmacologists, and that national teams have access to massage, contrast baths, acupuncture and other recovery resources. That context is part of the method's history and cannot be separated from it.

Even so, the principles of Abadjiev's Bulgarian method are undeniable as a training resource. They are perhaps best understood not as the most effective method, but as an effective method. Its importance today lies in its implications and applications.

There will never be a perfect training method, because human body function will never be fully understood.
