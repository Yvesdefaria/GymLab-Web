A Sunday grill and a Tuesday pan are not the same. Neither are fresh chicken and a cured sausage. Separating those two questions avoids two common mistakes: fearing chicken and treating sausages as if they were breast.

## Where they form

Heterocyclic amines, known as HCAs, form when amino acids in meat react with creatine and creatinine at high temperatures. They appear mostly on the browned surface, the crust that is sought for flavour.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, or PAHs, have another origin. Fat and juices drip onto the flame, burn incompletely and the smoke deposits the compounds on the meat. The closer the piece sits to the fire and the more fat drips, the higher the exposure.

Neither forms in the juicy interior of a properly cooked cut. The risk sits on the surface and in what burns, not at the centre of the meat.

## Processed and fresh are not the same

The International Agency for Research on Cancer placed processed meat in group 1 for its link to colorectal cancer. That classification describes the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk.

Red meat sits in group 2A, probably carcinogenic. Fresh chicken is not classified as a carcinogen. A study that finds HCAs in roasted chicken does not turn chicken into a processed product or roasting into a banned practice.

The figure usually quoted is an 18 percent increase in relative colorectal cancer risk for every 50 grams of processed meat a day. Absolute risk remains low and depends on the whole diet and the rest of a life.

A steamed breast and a burnt breast are the same food with a different surface.

## What reduces formation

Lowering the temperature has the largest effect. Cooking over medium heat, turning the piece often and taking it off when golden rather than black reduces the crust and the compounds with it.

Heating meat for two minutes in the microwave before grilling and discarding the liquid cuts a good share of HCAs, because most of the reaction no longer finds its substrate on the surface. It is a cheap, unglamorous step.

Marinating with acidic ingredients, herbs and spices reduces formation on the surface. No exotic recipe is needed: lemon, garlic, rosemary or vinegar work.

Trimming visible fat keeps it from dripping onto the flame and making smoke. Cooking on a cooler zone of the grill, with the lid down and no flare-ups, also helps. Piercing the meat with a fork while it cooks releases juices that feed the fire.

Minced meat deserves separate attention: when it is turned, the inner surface becomes outer and can stay undercooked. Burgers need an internal temperature that guarantees safety without reaching charcoal.

## What is not needed

Cherries do not eliminate HCAs from a burnt piece, casein does not sequester them in any relevant way, and a beer does not neutralise them. Nor is there a reliable individual risk figure like the one circulating in some articles.

Vegetables and fibre are good for many reasons and go well with meat. They do not turn a charred surface into a safe one. Garlic, tea or red wine supply phenolic compounds, and none replaces control of the fire.

Studies on antioxidants and HCAs are interesting in the laboratory and hard to translate to a real meal. The practical lever remains cooking.

## Frequency and context

Population guidance limits red meat to about three portions a week, 350 to 500 grams of cooked weight, and asks for less processed meat. Chicken, fish, eggs and legumes share the protein without loading the same dish every time.

There is no need to swear off a barbecue forever. One meal is weighed within the week and the year, not as a verdict on its own.
