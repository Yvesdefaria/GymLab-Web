Maximum aerobic capacity falls by 8 to 10 percent per decade from age 30, and the rate speeds up after 60. Strength and muscle mass follow a similar path. Part of that decline is biological and part is disuse, and the second part can be reversed.

That is why stairs cost more at 70 than at 40. Inactivity accelerates the process: someone who sits all day loses muscle faster than someone who walks and carries weight several times a week.

Nobody returns to 30. What is achieved is reaching 80 with more strength, better balance and less dependence.

## Strength works at 70 and at 86

A classic trial with institutionalised people aged 86 to 96 showed how much room remains at that age. For eight weeks they did leg presses and knee extensions with progressively rising loads, under supervision. Leg strength rose by 174 percent, thigh mass grew by 9 percent and mobility improved in walking tests.

The participants had osteoarthritis, coronary disease, hypertension or previous fractures, and almost all completed the programme. The lesson is that old muscle responds when the dose and the supervision are right.

## Before starting: screening and approval

At 70 a review before the first session is wise, especially with cardiovascular disease, poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes with complications, osteoporosis, a hip or knee replacement, or recent falls. The doctor assesses the effort allowed and any medication adjustments.

Beta blockers blunt heart rate, diuretics change hydration and anticoagulants raise the risk of bruising. None of them prevents training, but all of them change how the session is monitored. Chest pain, dizziness or disproportionate breathlessness during effort call for a consultation, not endurance.

## The first eight weeks

The opening plan targets technique and consistency, not fatigue. Two sessions a week, six exercises and one or two sets of 8 to 12 reps with a load that allows finishing without straining. Perceived effort stays at 5 or 6 out of 10.

The exercises cover the basic patterns: standing up from a chair, leg press, chest press, seated row, a light hip hinge, calf raises and a short carry. Machines are useful at first to learn the path; free weights arrive when technique holds.

## How to progress without breaking

The rule is simple: when 12 reps feel easy in two sessions in a row, add 2 to 5 percent. That small jump avoids the pain that comes from doubling the load at once. Every four to six weeks, a week with less volume helps the body absorb the work.

Muscle soreness in the next 24 to 48 hours is normal. Sharp pain or joint pain is not, and it calls for stopping and reviewing the movement. Technique matters more than load.

## Balance, falls and bone

Strength training improves balance and reduces the number of falls, the most common cause of fracture at this age. Two or three times a week, with support nearby, is enough: heel-to-toe walking, standing on one foot for a few seconds and getting up from a chair without hands.

Bone responds to impact and load. Walking, stairs and weights stimulate it; swimming does not, because the body floats. With diagnosed osteoporosis, avoid forced spinal flexion and loaded twists.

## Protein and vitamin D

After 70, muscle protein synthesis responds less to food, and the reference rises to 1.0 or 1.2 grams per kilo of body weight a day. Spreading it across meals helps more than concentrating it at dinner.

Vitamin D supports bone and helps absorb calcium. The reference after 70 is 800 IU a day, and the [vitamin D foods guide](/en/vitamin-d-foods/) explains what food supplies and when a test is needed.

## What to avoid

Maximum lifts, cold starts and the idea that pain means progress all belong elsewhere. Nor is running wise when knees already hurt, and holding the breath on every rep spikes blood pressure. The goal is to add training weeks, not to win a bet.
