The word light reads like a health promise and, in most cases, it only describes a subtraction. A product can be light and still be ultra-processed, low in fibre, high in sodium and more expensive. The label reports one nutrient; marketing supplies the rest of the package.

## What light products are

In the European Union, light is a comparative claim. To use it, the product must cut at least 30% of the energy or the nutrient it refers to, and say so on the package: "30% less sugar than the original". Without that sentence, the word means nothing concrete. A 200 kcal product drops to 140 at most to call itself light, and it still provides calories.

The rule also requires the comparison to be against a similar product, not an invented category. If no normal version of the same food exists, the claim loses its meaning. And if the reduction concerns fat, the manufacturer must state the reference value.

## Compare per 100 grams, not per serving

Serving size is the first trick. A package can shrink the portion from 30 to 20 grams and look lighter without changing the recipe. The honest comparison is per 100 grams or per 100 millilitres, between products in the same category. That shows whether the saving is real or just presentation.

The second trap is rounding. A product with 3.4 grams of fat per 100 grams cannot be called "low fat", but it can be presented as "reduced" if it meets the 30% cut against a similar product. The absolute figure matters more than the adjective.

## The classic swap: fat for sugar

When fat is removed, flavour collapses and is rebuilt with sugar, starch or salt. When sugar is removed, a sweetener appears and sometimes more fat. The result can have fewer calories and a worse nutrient profile. The ingredient list ranks by amount: if sugar or syrup appears in the first three, the product is not a light alternative even if the wrapper says so.

Low-fat dairy is a good example. A fat-free yogurt can carry more sugar than the full-fat one so that it tastes of something. The calorie gap between them is usually small, and the full-fat version is more filling. The guide to [dietary fats](/en/dietary-fats/) explains why fat is not the enemy it was sold as.

## Sweeteners: approved, not harmless

Aspartame, sucralose and acesulfame K are authorised and have an acceptable daily intake. That does not turn them into water. In 2023 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified aspartame as a possible carcinogen, with limited evidence in humans. Usual doses stay far from the limit, but a light label is no excuse to drink soft drinks all day. Water remains the default drink.

## Where light does save calories

Some categories make sense in a reduced version: plain yogurt, sugary drinks, sauces and some dairy. In a calorie deficit, swapping a soft drink for its sugar-free version saves about 100 kcal per can without touching food. The guide to the [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/) explains why that saving only works if the daily total holds, and the one on [how to build a diet](/en/how-to-build-a-diet/) shows where to place it.

## What the label does not say

A light product can be ultra-processed, low in fibre and protein, and less filling than the original food. The label reports one nutrient, not overall quality. Nor does the comparison with the normal version say whether the base product was a good idea.

The ingredient list fills part of that gap. Partially hydrogenated oils and interesterified fats show up there, and the guide to [trans fats](/en/trans-fats/) explains how to read them. In a bakery product, that line says more than the badge on the front.

## How to decide in the shop

Five seconds of reading: compare per 100 grams, look at the first three ingredients, check fibre and protein, and distrust a much higher price for a small saving. If the product has no normal version to compare against, the word light adds nothing.

The [body recomposition guide](/en/body-recomposition-guide/) places these decisions inside a plan, and the [healthy pizza](/en/healthy-pizza/) guide applies the same exercise to a dish with no label. In both cases the question is the same: what does it add to the day's total.

## What the wrapper cannot fix

No light product makes up for a disordered diet. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) have a close relative in label mistakes: chasing a shortcut in one nutrient and losing sight of the whole. Smart shopping looks more like filling the basket with recognisable food than choosing the pack with fewer red numbers.
