The frequency question has no single answer. It depends on intensity, volume, genetics, diet, rest and even the atmospheric pressure where you live. That last one is a joke, unless you live somewhere with pressures as unusual as Bolivia's.

## Which variables change frequency

The damage from a session drives everything else. A light session leaves little trace; a session with sets to failure, negatives and forced reps may need several days.

Genetics, food and sleep change repair speed. Two people on the same routine can need different rest periods.

## The rule: return when the muscle has recovered

Weight training exposes the muscle to a stimulus it is not used to. That stimulus causes microtrauma and, with proper recovery, leads to growth or hypertrophy.

Think about how people get a tan. You lie in the sun for 15 minutes; if you are not pink the next day, you go back for 20 or 25 minutes. If you burn, you wait for the pink to turn brown and then try 30 minutes. The skin adapts with melanin and is better equipped for the next stimulus.

Muscle works the same way. You train it, it repairs and it comes back better prepared for next time.

## What happens if you train too soon

If you return before repair is finished, you add microtears to fibres that have not recovered. The result is more fatigue, not more stimulus.

That is one road to overtraining. The muscle loses size, hardness and density, and becomes more prone to injury.

## What happens if you wait too long

Once the muscle has fully recovered and you keep waiting, it starts to atrophy. That is the opposite of hypertrophy: it loses size and hardness as the days pass.

Atrophy begins a few days after full recovery. If you had to choose between training too much and resting too much, resting too much is the smarter option: you will progress less than you could, but you will progress. Overtraining sends you backwards.

## How to work out your rest days

Frequency depends directly on the damage from the last session. Two light sets of 10 reps let you return the next day. Twenty sets of 15 reps to failure, with forced reps at the end, need several days.

Negative or eccentric reps and forced reps cause much more myofibrillar damage. If you use them, extend the rest.

As a practical reference, if the muscle is sore and congested for the next 24 to 48 hours, the intensity was right. Wait at least one day after the soreness disappears before repeating that group.

Example: you train chest on Monday, you are sore on Tuesday and Wednesday, and the soreness is gone on Thursday. Under this rule, you go back on Friday. That gives a five-day frequency, which is optimal for most people chasing muscle or tone over the medium term.

Some muscles, such as the biceps, shoulders and abs, are resistant and hard to damage with normal intensity. If you have gone a long time without feeling anything, raise intensity: shorten rest between sets or add reps. Increasing volume is the last resort.

No soreness does not mean the session was wasted. Do not obsess over damaging the muscle in every workout either.

## Two cases: Yates and Coleman

Dorian Yates, six-time Mr. Olympia, trained each muscle group every 7 or 8 days. He used exaggerated loads, forced reps and negatives, and his muscles took at least 6 or 7 days to recover.

Ronnie Coleman, eight-time Mr. Olympia, trained each group twice a week, with 3 or 4 days of rest between sessions. Maybe his training was not as hard, maybe he recovered faster, or maybe both.

Both approaches work because both leave just enough time for the muscle to recover before it is trained again.

## Frequency for maintenance

Not everyone trains to gain muscle. People who want to stay fit as the years pass often use lighter weights three times a week, without producing the muscle damage described above.

For that goal, training the whole body on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, every two days, is a good solution. The plan changes because the goal changes.

## Conclusion

Review your current routine and ask whether you train at the right frequency, whether you do too much or too little. Try adjustments and watch how your body responds.

Do not copy a routine just because it works for an acquaintance or because a gym instructor gave it to you. Every body is different, and the wrong frequency can cost you months of sacrifice.
