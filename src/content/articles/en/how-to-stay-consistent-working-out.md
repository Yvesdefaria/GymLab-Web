Few people remember **Bernarr Macfadden** today, one of the pioneers of physical culture about a century ago. His story explains why consistency weighs so much.

A self-proclaimed fitness expert, he ran a publishing empire and owned several health-food hotels and restaurants. His magazine, Physical Culture, was the forerunner of today's fitness magazines, with a circulation of half a million copies at the end of the First World War.

His philosophy rested on two principles: a vegetarian diet and large doses of daily exercise. You do not have to go vegetarian to keep reading.

Macfadden believed in daily training, without exceptions. He was said to have an exercise for every muscle, eyelids included, and even an ideal routine for hair.

His followers took different approaches, with one exception: for a programme to work, the person had to train consistently.

## Why consistency matters

A poorly organised routine followed consistently delivers more strength and size than an ideal routine followed sporadically.

The main reason is that any good programme rests on a **rhythm**, and the exercises are **interconnected**. What you do on Friday depends on what you will do on Monday and the other way round. Skip one of the days and progress suffers the following week.

Many people tire of training because they set themselves unrealistic programmes. Most copy a well-known athlete's plan to the letter without thinking that the athlete does nothing but train. Anyone with work and family obligations should adapt the routine to their reality.

If they do not, they soon find they cannot get to the gym four or five times a week. They see no results, lose heart and end up quitting. Then they promise to start again when they have more time, and that time never comes.

## Build a routine that fits you

The first step is to **set a programme that fits your daily life**, one you can adjust at different times of the year.

For most people, three days a week is best because it offers some flexibility. If you have to miss a session, you can make it up the next day or another day of the week.

The most important consideration is knowing how much exercise you can do in a week. That is why routines that train all the major muscle groups in every session beat split routines.

If you miss a day on a split routine and then try to train that muscle group twice as hard the next day, you will not get much. The **heavy, light, medium** principle helps for the same reason.

If you miss Monday, your heavy day, you can do the same routine on Tuesday and follow with a light day on Wednesday. That way it is not so exhausting. If you have to skip Wednesday or Friday, move the sessions to Thursday or Saturday.

There is always someone who asks: "What if I cannot train Monday, Wednesday or Friday?" Then do Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday. It is not ideal, but it beats skipping training. It lets you meet your obligations and, more importantly, it helps you mentally through the satisfaction of staying consistent.

Once you are convinced, you will train regularly for the rest of your life. If a person takes training seriously, they find a way to complete every session.

In my case, I learned quickly that consistency was essential. If I missed a session, I needed at least a month to get back to my previous level. So even when I finished work at eleven at night, I went to train at that hour to keep the rhythm.

## Schedules, travel and plan B

Some people skip a session simply because they have no plan in their diary. It is common among younger lifters balancing study and training, who tend to skip the Friday session. More experienced ones move Friday's session to Thursday and arrive on Monday in full form.

The same works when you know work will keep you busy: adjust your schedule to fit both. Travel complicates consistency more than any other cause.

Again, planning helps. If you go on holiday somewhere, check whether there is a gym you can use. It may not have the equipment you need, but keeping the routine maintains the habit.

**Any training session is better than nothing.** I know a man who always kept 10 kg dumbbells in the car when he travelled and did a full session in the hotel room: squats, lunges, deadlifts, press, biceps, front and lateral raises, and ab work. He always ended up sore.

He also claimed he could do a decent session without the dumbbells, using push-ups, lunges, chair dips and a variety of ab exercises.

Cardio fits anywhere. I met someone who was stuck in a hotel by a storm and started running up and down the building's stairs for 20 minutes.

## The habit that is hard to break

Skipping regular training costs tone, muscle size and strength. It also leaves you with a habit that is easy to adopt and hard to break. Before long, any excuse will do to avoid training.

Anyone who takes training seriously because they want to gain muscle and strength finds the time and the way to exercise without missing a session.

> No excuses. If you are consistent for a year, you will keep the discipline for the rest of your life.
