The seated position and the braced elbow turn the curl into a genuinely isolated rep, in the useful sense of the word: one joint, one arm and a range you can watch up close. It is also the best place to find out that one arm works differently from the other.

## The position

Sit on a flat bench or a chair with the feet on the floor and apart. One dumbbell in the working hand. The free hand rests on the opposite thigh to support the torso.

Lean forward until the chest is close to the thigh, with the back straight and the chest open. The working arm hangs between the legs, extended, and the elbow rests against the inner thigh on the same side. The palm faces up through the whole range.

From there, the dumbbell rises to near the shoulder, the biceps squeezes for a second and the descent is controlled to full extension. The elbow does not leave the thigh: the only working joint is the elbow.

## What the brace fixes

The braced elbow fixes the humerus and removes swinging. That is all. It does not choose which biceps head works.

The head that arrives stretched depends on the shoulder angle: with the arm behind the body the long head stretches, with the arm in front the short head works. In the concentration curl the shoulder stays neutral and the arm hangs vertically, so both heads work from a middle position. The [anatomy of the biceps and forearm](/en/biceps-forearm-anatomy/) shows each one's path.

The neutral-grip version, with the palm facing in, is a hammer curl with the elbow braced, and it shifts work to the brachialis and brachioradialis. It changes the muscle doing most of the flexion.

## The strict test

With one arm and a braced elbow, the load cannot be stolen. If the dumbbell rises, it rises because the biceps can lift it. That honesty has two uses.

The first is measurement. A set of 10 strict reps says more about the biceps than a set of 12 with swing and more weight. The second is comparing sides: almost everyone has a stronger arm, and here the difference appears without the body compensating.

The concentration curl is also a way to work near failure without losing position. If the rep stalls halfway, the dumbbell goes down and the elbow stays on the thigh. There is nothing to drop and no balance to chase.

## The mistakes

- **Moving the torso.** The back can stay inclined, but it does not turn or swing to push the dumbbell.
- **Lifting the elbow off the thigh.** Without the brace, the exercise loses what makes it distinct and the shoulder starts helping.
- **Using too much weight.** A heavy load forces the torso to turn or the elbow to lift, and both cancel the position.
- **Cutting the descent.** Full extension is where the biceps reaches its long position; dropping the dumbbell early leaves half a rep.
- **Bending the wrist.** The hand breaks inward at the end and the set finishes in the forearm.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest, a one-second pause at the top and a two to three second descent. The dose is a recommendation from this page; the original gives no numbers.

It is a finishing exercise. It goes at the end of the session, when the biceps is already tired and the weight matters less than the position. It also works as the only variation on a short day: three sets per arm and the work is done.
