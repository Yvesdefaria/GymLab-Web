If you have been training for a while, you have heard that some exercises are useless, some are sacred and some routines work forever. You have also heard that overtraining does not exist if you eat enough.

These claims sound good, but in more than a few cases, following them leaves you stuck.

## Isolation exercises do not build size

For decades, people said single-joint exercises only shaped the muscle and added detail, without real hypertrophy. Volume, according to that idea, belonged to multi-joint lifts: squat, leg press, bench press, overhead press and every kind of row.

Leg extensions, lateral raises, flyes and concentration curls were left out. Because they use less weight, they supposedly could not produce substantial growth.

> When a muscle receives enough tension, it adapts by growing, whatever the exercise.

The overload placed on the muscle decides productivity. Make the concentration curl your main exercise, with heavy weight and low reps, and it becomes an excellent builder of big, strong biceps.

Mike Mentzer used it as his main exercise with heavy loads, and his biceps were exceptional. If you treat it as a basic lift instead of leaving it for the end with light weight, the biceps responds.

The same applies to flyes, lateral raises or leg extensions. Stimulation decides, ahead of the exercise label.

## The squat, bench press and overhead press are sacred

Whole books are dedicated to the squat, presented as the best exercise for general growth and for the legs. It is an exceptional movement for strength and volume, especially in the legs, but it does not work the same for everyone.

People with long legs tend to lean too far forward and struggle to keep the back straight. With moderate weight the problem stays controlled; as the load rises, so does the risk.

Something similar happens with the overhead press or the bench press. They may be perfect for one person and may not suit another person's structure.

> No exercise is the best for everyone: bone length and joint levers differ.

Each person has different bones, levers and tendon insertions. Even neurological wiring affects how well an exercise fits. You have to test in the gym and keep what works for you.

## Find the perfect routine and never change it

This myth runs deep. No champion, present or past, has followed the same routine for years.

On paper, if you try movements until you find the best for each group, you could build the perfect routine and stop searching. In practice it does not work. The body adapts to almost everything, and a repeated stimulus stops being a challenge.

> The body has an enormous capacity to adapt, which is why no routine works forever.

Bodybuilders change their routines fairly often. Periodisation exists to organise phases with different training loads and prevent stagnation. No routine delivers results indefinitely.

## Overtraining is just under-eating

Some gurus claim overtraining does not exist if you match calories to output. If that were true, a bodybuilder could train all day and grow as long as they ate enough. That is absurd.

Good food restores energy and helps recovery, but exercise drains both physical and nervous energy. There is an individual tolerance point that, once crossed, reverses progress.

> You can train hard or long, but not both at once.

Every set stimulates growth and also drains energy. At some point an extra set only lengthens recovery. However many calories you eat, the body needs rest, especially to restore nervous energy.

## Training more than three or four times a week is useless

Another stubborn myth. It rests on the idea that a trained muscle needs seven days to regenerate, which is not true for everyone: small muscles can recover in 24 hours.

For decades, many bodybuilders trained each group twice a week and grew without problems. Some went to three sessions per group. The difference lies in how much work each group and the system receive.

> One of the most effective ways out of a plateau is frequent, very short sessions.

Arthur Jones's Nautilus system, the precursor to Heavy Duty, used one exercise per muscle group and trained the whole body in a single session, from legs to arms, three times a week. Each group was worked three times.

Many professionals and advanced amateurs train several times a day in short, specific sessions. They dissect each group and work it in isolation. It is not the best system for everyone or forever, but it renews growth while the stimulus lasts.

## Eat big and you will get big

People repeat that the only way to gain mass is to eat a lot of calories and a lot of protein. The line "we are what we eat" is taken as fact, and bodybuilding sometimes claims diet accounts for up to 80% of success.

Eating well matters: without nutrients there is no progress. But everything you ingest goes through digestion before absorption, and absorption is what decides.

> The body is built from what it absorbs.

What matters is what the body can absorb. That is why spreading protein across several meals works better than concentrating it in one.
