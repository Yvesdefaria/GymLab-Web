The 1RM is used in powerlifting and weightlifting competitions to establish a person's strength limit. It also tells you what weight to use across the different rep ranges in your training.

## What the one-rep max is

In weight training, the one-rep max is the maximum amount of weight someone can load on a given exercise and lift for a single rep. It is written 1RM and represents 100% of your strength in that movement.

There are two ways to get it. The first is to test it directly: warm up, add weight and make single attempts until you can no longer lift. The second is to estimate it from a set with more reps, which is what the calculator does. The estimate is reliable but approximate, and the fewer reps you use, the closer it gets to reality.

The 1RM changes from exercise to exercise. Your bench press max does not carry over to the squat or the deadlift, because each movement has its own limit. It also changes over time: it rises when you train and falls when you stop.

## How to calculate your 1RM step by step

The direct test follows a simple sequence. You need a good warm-up and long rests between attempts.

1. Do a light warm-up of 5 to 10 reps at 40-60% of the maximum you think you have.
2. Rest for a minute, do some gentle stretching and perform 3 to 5 reps at 60-80% of your perceived maximum.
3. Add weight and attempt a single rep. If it goes, rest for 3 to 5 minutes, add more weight and try again. Repeat until you cannot lift the weight.
4. Your 1RM is the last completed lift.

A maximal attempt is a considerable effort with an injury risk. Save it for when you genuinely need the number, and remember that estimating from a lighter set is often enough.

## 1RM calculator

The [1RM calculator](/en/one-rep-max-calculator/) applies the Brzycki formula. You enter the weight lifted and the number of reps, and it returns the estimated 1RM. The fewer reps you did with the heaviest possible weight, the more reliable the result.

The formula the calculator uses is weight × 36 / (37 − reps). With 80 kilos and 5 reps, the estimate is 90 kilos. That is consistent with the 1RM you would get from a direct test, though it does not replace one.

## Example with 5 reps at 75%

Imagine your routine calls for 12 bench press reps at 75% of your 1RM. To find the weight, follow these steps:

1. Pick a weight you think will take you to failure at 4 or 5 reps.
2. You load 80 kilos on the bar and reach failure on the fifth rep.
3. In the calculator you enter 80 as the weight lifted and 5 as the number of reps.
4. The calculator estimates your 1RM. From that number, 75% comes out at around 68 kilos, the weight you will use for the set of 12.

The result is an approximate reference. If 12 reps at 68 kilos feel impossible, lower the weight a little and adjust. If you have reps left, add weight. The estimated 1RM helps you get close faster and adjust as you go.

To understand why 12 reps and not 5, read the guide to the [optimal rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/). If the set ends near the limit, the guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains how to recognise it without overshooting.

## When testing it is worth it

The one-rep max is useful in weightlifting and in specific maximum-strength routines, where the working weight is calculated as a percentage of the 1RM. It also tracks strength progress on a given exercise across a cycle.

In most other cases, an estimate is enough. If you train for hypertrophy and work between 8 and 12 reps, you do not need to test your 1RM every week. A set of 5 reps near the limit and the calculator are enough.

Testing the 1RM makes most sense at the start of a strength plan, to set the loads, and at the end, to check the result. Doing it every few days adds fatigue and risk without new information.

## Risks and mistakes

The most common mistake is testing the 1RM without enough warm-up or with technique that breaks down on the attempt. The second is repeating the test too often: every maximal attempt leaves fatigue, and the next session feels it.

Avoid these errors too:

- Using a set of 10 or 12 reps to estimate the 1RM and treating it as exact. The more reps, the less reliable the estimate.
- Forgetting that the 1RM is specific to each exercise and each moment. It does not carry over from one movement to another.
- Starting with too much weight on the first attempt. It is better to reach the limit through several small jumps.
- Confusing the estimated 1RM with a target you must hit in every session. It is a reference for calculating loads and adjusting each session.

If you work with percentages, remember that the 1RM changes with training. Read the guide to [progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) to adjust loads when your 1RM rises, and do not force a percentage that no longer matches your level.
