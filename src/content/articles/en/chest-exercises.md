A chest trained only with presses receives load at one end of its range. With presses and flies it receives it at both. Exercise choice matters less than order and progression, but six movements cover the whole muscle.

## The six exercises that hold a routine together

### Barbell bench press

Three to five sets of 5 to 10 reps. It is the exercise that moves the most weight and the one that sets upper-body strength. The chest pushes, the front deltoid helps and the triceps closes the movement, so it also counts as arm work.

The comparison between [dumbbells and a barbell](/en/dumbbell-vs-barbell-bench-press/) explains what changes when each arm carries its own weight.

### Incline dumbbell press

Three or four sets of 8 to 12 reps. The backrest stays at around 30 degrees. With dumbbells each arm follows its own path and the chest stretches further at the bottom, at the cost of moving less weight than a bar.

The angle and how to check it on your own bench are covered in the [incline chest exercises](/en/incline-chest-exercises/) guide.

### Machine flies

Three sets of 10 to 15 reps. The machine keeps tension through the whole range, including the point where a dumbbell loses it. That constant tension lets you work close to failure with little weight and no balance demands.

### Smith machine incline press

Three or four sets of 8 to 12 reps. The fixed path removes the stabilisation work and lets you focus on the chest. It is useful for the negative phase and for the last sets, when the free press is no longer under control.

### Pullover

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps. It changes the line of pull and stretches the chest from a position the presses do not reach. It also spreads the work without asking the shoulder for heavy loads, which makes it useful at the end of a session full of presses.

### High-cable crossover

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps. The pulleys keep tension through the whole arc and let you bring the hands together with a light load. It is the usual close of a chest session, after the presses and the flies.

## The bench angle and the upper chest

The chest has two heads that share one tendon, and no hand position disconnects one to leave the other working alone. What the incline changes is the line of the arm, which comes closer to the direction of the clavicular fibres. That effect holds up to around 30 degrees; above that, the front deltoid takes a growing share of the work.

The angle changes the split of a lever that has two heads, not three. The [chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/) guide develops the full map.

## The order of the session

Presses go first, when you can move weight and control technique. Then comes the fly or the crossover, when fatigue no longer allows loading and the goal is to accumulate tension with little weight.

One rule prevents the most common mistake: do not stack inclines. An incline press with a bar, another with dumbbells and another on a machine in the same session work a similar angle and punish the shoulder without adding stimulus.

## Chest training at home

The chest can be trained at home with bodyweight and a pair of dumbbells. Push-ups cover the horizontal press, the dumbbell press adds load and resistance bands cover the fly gesture when there are no pulleys.

The guide to [dumbbells for chest training](/en/dumbbells-for-chest/) orders that equipment and explains what can be replaced and what cannot.

## Machines or free weights

Machines let you take a muscle to failure with less risk and less time between sets. Free weights demand stabilisation and spread the work across more muscles. Neither is better in the abstract: the decision depends on experience, available equipment and the point of the session.

The comparison of [machines and free weights](/en/machines-vs-free-weights-chest/) develops the differences, and the guide to [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) covers the details that repeat in every pushing exercise.
