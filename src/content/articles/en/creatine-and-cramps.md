The suspicion makes sense. If creatine pulls water into the muscle cell, it was thought to disturb the electrolyte balance and trigger cramps. The hypothesis sounded reasonable and was repeated for years without being tested. When it was measured, the result was different.

## Where the idea comes from

Creatine is osmotically active and raises the water content inside the cell. That water is not missing from the rest of the body, because plasma volume is maintained and general hydration does not get worse at the usual doses. The imbalance people feared does not appear in the blood work.

The reputation survives because cramps are common in sports with repeated effort, and anyone who starts creatine in pre-season can coincide with them. The coincidence became a cause.

## What the trials show

The most cited paper is a trial with 38 American football players at college level training in summer. Cramps appeared in 27% of the creatine group and in 47% of the group that did not take it. The study was designed to measure injuries and cramps, and the result favoured creatine.

There are two reasons not to read it as proof of prevention. The first is size, with 38 players split into two groups that leave little statistical room. The second is context: American football is played in full kit under the sun, and dehydration weighs more than any supplement.

The second paper is a small cohort of people on haemodialysis, a group with very frequent cramps. On creatine, the incidence fell over four weeks. That is interesting for that population and does not transfer to a healthy adult who trains.

What is clear is the absence of harm. No trial has found more cramps in people who take creatine, and the honest reading is that creatine is neither culprit nor cure.

## Why cramps happen

A cramp is an involuntary, painful contraction that will not relax. It appears when the fatigued muscle loses fine control of the contraction, and the factors that weigh most are fatigue, heat, sodium loss through sweat and a load you are not used to.

Acute dehydration contributes too, especially if you sweat a lot and replace it with water alone. In a two hour session in the heat you lose sodium and fluid together, and drinking without salt dilutes the sodium that is left.

## Water and sodium: how much and when

Weighing yourself before and after training is the simplest way to know how much you sweat. Every kilo lost equals a litre of fluid, and useful replacement is about a litre and a half per kilo to cover urine as well.

For sessions under an hour, water is enough and thirst is a reasonable guide. Beyond that, a drink with sodium helps retain the fluid and replace what is lost. A pinch of salt in the bottle or a sports drink does the same job.

Sodium matters as much as the water. Someone who sweats heavily and drinks only water for hours can end up with cramps and a headache, and the problem is solved by adding salt to the drink. The guide to [training in hot weather](/en/training-in-hot-weather/) expands the protocol.

## The creatine dose

Three to five grams a day, rest days included. That amount keeps the tank full and does not demand more water than the body asks for. If you sweat a lot, hydration is adjusted by thirst and by weight lost, not by creatine.

If a large dose upsets your stomach, split the 5 grams into two servings. The problem is usually the volume of powder at once, not the molecule.

## Who should ask first

Creatine is safe in healthy adults at the usual doses, but some groups should ask a doctor before starting, such as people with a diagnosed kidney condition, pregnant or breastfeeding women, minors, and anyone on drugs that affect the kidney.

The guide to [creatine and the kidney](/en/creatine-and-kidneys/) explains why creatinine rises in blood tests and how it is read. If a cramp appears at rest, lasts more than a few minutes or repeats without any link to training, the question is medical rather than nutritional.

## Where it fits

Cramps are part of the normal fatigue of training, like soreness. The guide to [delayed onset muscle soreness](/en/delayed-onset-muscle-soreness/) separates late pain from an injury, and the one on [muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) organises rest between sessions.

For the dose and the general context, the [creatine](/en/creatine-supplement/) page gathers the essentials, and the [evidence](/en/creatine-evidence/) page reviews the myths still going around. Creatine does not fix a cramp; water with salt and a progressive load do help.
