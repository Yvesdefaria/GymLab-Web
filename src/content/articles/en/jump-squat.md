A loaded jump is judged by the speed it produces, not by the weight it moves. That is the difference from a squat: the goal is not to beat a big load, but to push the floor as fast as possible with a load that allows it.

## The load has a cap

The bar does not take the weight of a heavy squat. The useful range runs from zero to 40% of your one-rep max, and 20-30% is the usual working zone. Above that ceiling the body prioritises moving the bar and the jump turns into a slow push.

The percentage is calculated from your squat one-rep max, not from the weight of the last jump you did.

## Intent leads

Height is not the scoreboard. At a given load, every rep is executed with the same intent to leave the floor fast. The nervous system sets the ceiling before the muscle does, and a jump that loses height rep after rep is a fatigue signal.

The dip is short: a fast quarter squat with no pause at the bottom. The bar sits on the traps and is pulled down through the whole flight so it does not leave the shoulders.

The bar comes out of the rack on the traps, as in a back squat. Before the first rep, check the clear space above your head and where the feet are planted.

## The landing

The fall is where control is lost. Land with the hips back, the knees tracking over the toes and the weight spread across the whole foot. Absorption is progressive: ankle, knee and hip flex in the same order as in a squat.

After the landing you stand up and reset. Chaining jumps without recovering turns the set into endurance work. A landing you can repeat matters more than a silent one.

## Sets and reps

This page's recommendation: three to five sets of 3 to 5 reps with full recovery, 2 to 3 minutes between sets. The set ends when height drops or the landing stops being clean.

Power work is programmed by quality, not by volume. If the jump does not rise, the power session ends earlier than a strength session would. [Ballistic training](/en/ballistic-training/) explains why it is measured by acceleration, [jump rope](/en/jump-rope-training/) is the cheapest entry into jumping, and the [progressive overload principle](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) sets out when to add load and when not to.

## Common mistakes

The first is chasing height with a bar that will not leave the floor. The second is landing with the knees caving in or the torso ahead of the feet. The third is chaining reps without recovery and calling it power when it is already endurance.

The fourth is starting with load before owning the jump with bodyweight. The fifth is using the jump squat as a substitute for the heavy squat: it covers something else.

## Where it fits

It goes at the start of the session, after the warm-up and before heavy work, while the nervous system is fresh. Two or three sets are enough for the day; the rest of the session stays with strength. Keep it away from the end of a session, when the landing is the first thing to degrade.

The [bodyweight squat](/en/bodyweight-squat/) works as the warm-up, the [box squat heights](/en/box-squat-heights/) guide sets the depth of the dip and the [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/) is still the strength base. The [squat safety guide](/en/squat-safety/) covers the loaded pattern and its limits. The [deep squat](/en/deep-squat/) and the [overhead barbell squat](/en/overhead-barbell-squat/) complete the barbell squat block.

Stop the set when jump height drops, not when the lungs burn.
