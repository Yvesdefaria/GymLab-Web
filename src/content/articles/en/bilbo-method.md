Classic progression adds kilos until the lift stalls. The Bilbo method offers another way through that plateau: lower the load, move it as fast as possible and measure progress in reps and speed rather than in the weight on the bar. It is not an effort-free system or a law of physics. It is a way to accumulate quality work with a submaximal load.

## What the Bilbo method is and where it comes from

The method circulates in Spanish-speaking forums and its authorship is not documented. The attribution to a specific person in some texts comes without a source: it is a popular routine with no verified origin.

The central idea is concrete. You do one main set at 50-70 percent of your 1RM, for 15 to 40 reps, driving the concentric phase at the highest speed you can sustain. The descent is controlled, without letting the bar drop. The set ends when speed falls clearly and the last reps turn into a slow struggle. That point is the stimulus; going further only adds fatigue.

This is speed work, not a demonstration that mass times acceleration replaces load. Moving 50 kilos fast requires producing force, and that is why the method can help maintain or improve the ability to apply power. It does not turn a light load into a maximal one or remove joint wear by magic.

## The warm-up and the main set

The warm-up is short and progressive. Empty bar for 10 reps, 30 percent of your 1RM for 5 and 40 percent for 3. The goal is to switch the movement on, not to arrive tired for the set that matters.

Then comes the Bilbo set. With a 100 kilo bench press 1RM, the working load is around 50 kilos and the target is to beat 30 reps with clean technique. If the bar starts to slow on rep 24, the set ends there. Log the weight, the reps and how the last two felt. One more rep than last week is progress; if the number does not rise but the bar moves more smoothly, that counts too.

Technique comes before the record. A bounce off the chest, a shorter range or an arch that turns the lift into a different exercise make the mark a false data point. The set stops before mechanical failure, when the next rep would force the movement out of shape. The guide to [muscular failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) explains why that limit matters in a strength scheme.

## How to programme a cycle

Pick a load you can move for 30 to 40 comfortable reps. That is the reference for the first day, not your 1RM. From there, the order of progression is simple: first add a rep, then a little weight. If the set drops below 15 reps, the cycle is nearing its end. This page sets that finish line at 10-15 reps; some versions of the method mention 8-10, but 10-15 leaves room to keep speed and technique.

Do one or two weekly sessions per lift. More frequency does not fit if every set chases top speed and the rest of the session still has accessory work. A well-planned cycle lasts from about eight weeks to several months, and ends when reps fall below the threshold or when two or three sessions in a row fail to beat the mark.

## A complete bench press session

The main set comes first, after the ramp-up sets. The rest of the session works the muscles that support the press in hypertrophy ranges.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Incline dumbbell press | 3 | 8-12 |
| High-cable crossover | 3 | 12-15 |
| Cable triceps extension | 4 | 10-15 |
| Lateral raise | 4 | 12-20 |

The main set needs full rest before it starts, and accessories tolerate 60-90 seconds. That distribution is reasonable guidance, not a rule of the method. The page on [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) helps adjust it by exercise.

## Mistakes that break the cycle

The first is sacrificing technique to add a rep. The second is training with accumulated fatigue: speed is the first quality lost when you sleep badly or come from a hard session. The third is skipping accessory work, because the mass that supports the press also has to be trained.

The fourth mistake is jumping the load too far. If the weight rises all at once, reps fall and the cycle shortens. The fifth is starting without a base: a beginner needs to consolidate technique before moving weights at top speed under fatigue. In the deadlift and the squat, the method demands even more care, because technical collapse at high reps carries more risk than in a bench press.

## What to expect from the method

The Bilbo method can be useful to break a plateau, to keep speed in a strength block or to train with less load when the joints ask for a break. It does not replace heavy work forever: maximum strength is built with heavy loads, and speed is a complement.
