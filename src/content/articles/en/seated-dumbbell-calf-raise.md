A seated machine is not required to train the soleus. A bench, a block five to ten centimetres high and a pair of dumbbells reproduce the position and the stimulus. The difference from the machine is the weight, and that difference is solved with time and range.

## Why the bent knee changes the muscle

The soleus starts at the tibia and the fibula and ends at the Achilles tendon. It does not cross the knee, so its length depends only on the ankle. When you sit and bend the knee about ninety degrees, the gastrocnemius shortens from above and stops contributing; the soleus takes over plantar flexion.

That is why the seated version is not a comfortable alternative to the standing one. The [anatomy of the calf and soleus](/en/calf-anatomy/) explains the split, and the [standing machine](/en/standing-calf-raise-machine/) covers the other muscle with the knee extended. If you do both, the soleus goes last: it is used to holding the body up all day and handles fatigue better.

## Block height

The block measures five to ten centimetres. At that height the balls of the feet rest on top and the heels hang free below. A taller block adds range and also adds the risk of the foot sliding; a lower one leaves the rep half done.

The edge of the bench stays behind the knees, with the back straight and the torso upright. Sit too far back and the feet push diagonally, so the ankle loses its axis. The heels move vertically, not forward.

## Dumbbells and the load limit

The dumbbells rest on the lower thigh, near the knee. There the weight sits on bone rather than tendon, and the hand only steadies it. One dumbbell per leg spreads the load more comfortably than a single one across both.

The limit is obvious: the dumbbells you can hold on your thigh are far lighter than the weight of a seated machine. That is not a flaw in the exercise, it is its condition. Load is compensated with a longer pause and a full range, and if the weight runs short, a bar across both thighs takes more plates.

## The pause that pays for the weight

At the top you hold for a second, with the ankle in full plantar flexion and the weight on the base of the toes. That second turns a light set into a useful one: the soleus spends more time under tension and the stimulus does not depend on kilos alone.

At the bottom you lower into the stretch without letting the heel drop. Full range and the pause are the part of the exercise that more weight cannot replace, and the part most often lost when the set gets fast.

## How much weight and how many sets

The dose this page proposes is three sets of 15 to 20 reps with 60 seconds of rest. It is a recommendation from this article, not a number inherited from the machine.

Add weight when all three sets reach 20 reps with the pause intact. If the pause disappears or the heel stops dropping, the dumbbell is already too heavy. If the weight runs short for weeks, switch to the bar across the thighs or move to the [seated machine](/en/seated-calf-raise-machine/).

## Mistakes that cost range

- **Feet that slide.** The ball of the foot drifts forward and the ankle loses its base. A block with a rough surface prevents it.
- **Bouncing at the bottom.** The heel touches the floor and rises on momentum, with no pause and no control.
- **Leaning the torso.** If the body tips forward, the drive comes from the quadriceps instead of the ankle.
- **One dumbbell badly placed.** The weight pulls to one side and the hip twists.

## Where it fits

The dumbbell version is the easiest to set up at home and the one that fits a leg session best. After the seated work, a standing variation covers the gastrocnemius: the [standing machine](/en/standing-calf-raise-machine/) or the [donkey raise](/en/donkey-calf-raise/) keep the knee extended. The guide to [standing versus seated raises](/en/standing-vs-seated-calf-raises/) orders both positions across the week.
