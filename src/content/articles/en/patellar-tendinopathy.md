> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. If the pain appears suddenly after a jump, you cannot straighten the knee or the joint swells, get advice before you keep training.

Pain in the patellar tendon rarely arrives overnight. It settles in over weeks and reps, and recognising it early saves months of discomfort.

## What patellar tendinopathy is

The patellar tendon is the final portion of the quadriceps. It runs from the lower pole of the kneecap to the tibial tuberosity, is 3 to 5 centimetres long and about 2 centimetres wide, and forms part of the knee's extensor mechanism. Every time you straighten the leg, the force of the quadriceps travels through it. The quad also absorbs the impact of every landing, which is where the extra load in jumping sports comes from.

It is known as jumper's knee because it shows up in jumping sports and in repeated movements with a bent knee, and also in the weights room, where squats and leg presses add reps through the same joint.

## Why it appears

The usual mechanism is overload from repeated microtrauma. Jumping on hard surfaces, running, plyometrics or piling up squat volume without raising load gradually. The tendon works hardest when the knee goes past 60 degrees of flexion with body weight on that leg.

It also shows up in cyclists, and in anyone who changes shoes or surfaces without giving the tendon time to adapt.

The factors that bring it on keep repeating:

- **A worn shoe or a change of footing.** A small shift in the running axes changes the tension that reaches the tendon.
- **A weak quadriceps.** A quad with little strength leaves the tendon doing work that is not its job.
- **Tight hamstrings.** They limit movement and change how load is shared at the knee.
- **A high or unstable kneecap.** That is a professional's ground, not something an exercise corrects.

## How it feels

The pain sits at the front of the knee, over the tendon, and appears in specific movements:

- Walking down stairs.
- Squatting.
- Standing up after sitting for a while with the knee bent.
- Jumping or landing.

Pressing the lower pole of the kneecap hurts. At first the discomfort arrives at the end of the session or eases as you warm up; as it progresses, it shows up at rest. Intensity varies too: there are days of discomfort you can work around and days when walking down a staircase is a chore.

Swelling is not on the list, and it is worth explaining why. Patellar tendinopathy answers to load and to degeneration of the tendon, not to classic inflammation, so the knee does not usually swell or turn red. If there is an effusion, the conversation is a different one: meniscus, cartilage or something that needs an assessment.

## Loading is the treatment

The first step is to dose the load: keep the work that does not spike the pain and cut the work that does. Less volume, less range, another variation.

The protocol with support behind it starts with isometric quadriceps work and moves on to slow, heavy resistance. The numbers, with their phases and tempos, are in the guide to [common gym injuries](/en/common-gym-injuries/). This page does not repeat them on purpose.

Anti-inflammatories relieve the symptom and do not change the tendon's capacity to carry load. Passive therapies, such as laser or ultrasound, relieve for a while at most.

The squat is not banned. Change the range, slow the descent and adjust the weight. [Squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) has the execution that protects the knee.

The [warm-up](/en/gym-warm-up/) and [stretching](/en/stretching/) help you tolerate the session. They do not cure the tendon, but they make strength work feel better.

## The strap, the ice and the return

The circular strap sits below the kneecap and compresses half of the tendon. It shares out the tension and that is why it relieves, but it is a load modifier: you can train with it and feel less discomfort, and the tendon does not get stronger by wearing it.

Ice, about 20 minutes after the session, is for the discomfort. It does not speed up repair and it does not replace loading work.

The return is a ramp. Less volume, less range and small increases, with the same logic you use for any tissue that complains. For the first month, stay cautious even when the knee looks recovered: the tendon tolerates one session before it tolerates one week.

## When to get advice

Ask for an assessment if the pain has not improved in several weeks, if it appears at rest or at night, if you feel a lump or a dip in the tendon, or if after a jump there is sharp pain and you cannot straighten the knee.

Ultrasound and MRI are medical decisions, not tests to order on your own. Around 10% of cases end in surgery, according to the original article. Most resolve without an operating room; the rest go through a specialist.

The knee rarely travels alone. If the root of the problem is further up, [training with back problems](/en/training-with-back-pain/) explains how exercise selection changes when the spine already has a pronounced curve.
