The hip thrust is also called hip lift or hip push. You rest your upper back on a bench and drive your hips up with a barbell, a dumbbell or a machine over the pelvis. There are floor versions, bench versions, box versions and a dedicated machine.

## Which muscles work

The driver is the **gluteus maximus**, which extends the hip. The hamstrings help, since they also cross the hip, and the adductors stabilise the pelvis as it rises. The quadriceps keeps the knee in place, especially when the feet are further forward.

The psoas is not on that list. It is a hip flexor: its job is to bring the thigh toward the trunk, not to push the pelvis up. When the glute extends the hip, the psoas lengthens and yields.

## The benefits of the hip thrust

**It loads the glute in its short range.** At the top of the movement the hip is extended and the glute works near its shortest position. The squat recruits it too, but with the hip more flexed and with much more quadriceps involved.

**It builds hip strength with little load on the back.** The bar rests on the pelvis, not on the spine, so axial compression is lower than in a barbell squat. For anyone who tolerates shoulder loading poorly, that makes it a useful alternative.

**It teaches the extension pattern.** Many lifters push with the lower back because they cannot feel the glute. The hip thrust forces the two apart: the pelvis rises through the hip and the lumbar spine stays still.

**It adapts easily.** Free bar, Smith machine, dumbbell, band or machine. The machine version allows heavy loading with less demand on stability, and the band version works for learning the movement at home.

**It transfers to sport.** Powerful hip extension sits behind running, jumping and the first step. That is the practical argument for including it in a strength plan, always as one piece among others.

## Hip thrust and squat complement each other

The original article claimed the hip thrust activates the glute more than the squat. With nuance, the idea holds: through much of the range, the hip thrust loads the glute more than the squat. Leaping from there to replacing the squat is a long jump, because each exercise covers a different part of the pattern.

The squat trains the full pattern with an upright torso, loads the quadriceps and demands whole-body stability. The hip thrust works hip extension from another position. A serious leg plan usually needs both, plus a hinge movement.

That hinge is the stiff-leg deadlift, which appears in the [hamstring routine](/en/jay-cutler-hamstring-routine/) and has its own guide in the [stiff-leg deadlift with a barbell](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-barbell/). The squat, for its part, is covered in the [squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) guide.

## How to perform it

1. Sit on the floor with your upper back against the edge of the bench, level with the shoulder blades.
2. Plant your feet at hip width, knees bent and soles fully down.
3. Place the bar over the pelvis, with a pad if the weight digs in.
4. Drop the ribs, brace the abdomen and look forward without jutting the chin.
5. Push through the heels and lift the hips until torso and thighs form a line.
6. Squeeze the glute at the top, without arching the lower back to gain height.
7. Lower under control to the floor or to the starting position.

The classic mistake is chasing height with the back. If the lumbar spine arches at the end, the hip is already extended and what rises is the spine. Pushing through the toes is also common: the pressure should spread across the whole foot.

## Two ways to fit it into your week

The first is inside leg day. If you train legs once a week, add two glute-focused exercises after the main lifts, for example hip thrust and dumbbell lunges.

The second is an extra day. If your week allows it, give a session to hips and glutes, or add those exercises at the end of back day: hip thrust, lunges, abductors, adductors and back extensions.

Both options work as long as total volume does not explode. The [training volume](/en/training-volume/) guide explains how to spread sets without piling on work.

## How many sets and reps

The original article gives no sets, reps or frequency. It describes the exercise, its benefits and two ways to include it, and stops there. This guide does not invent a prescription.

As a starting reference, a hip exercise like this fits at the end of the session, with moderate sets and reps that let you control the position. The exact number depends on your plan and the point of the season.

## Mistakes and precautions

- **Arching the lower back at the top.** Extension ends at the hip. If the back arches, the set is already over.
- **Placing the feet too far away.** The further out they are, the more hamstrings and the less glute. Adjust the distance until the glute does the work.
- **Bouncing off the floor.** The descent is half the exercise.
- **Starting with a loaded bar.** Learn the movement with bodyweight and a band before adding plates.

The hip thrust does not fix posture and does not replace the squat. It is a hip extension exercise that adds to the plan when the glute needs direct work. To arrive at the session with the hips ready, the [glute warm-up](/en/glute-workout-warm-up/) guide proposes a short circuit with no equipment.
