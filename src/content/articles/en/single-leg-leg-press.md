The 45-degree press shares the work between two legs, and that sharing hides something: if one side pushes less, the other makes up for it and the set ends without anyone noticing. On one leg there is nowhere to hide. The weak side shows up in the first set and the strong side sets the ceiling for the session.

## What the single-leg press works

The movement is the same as in the bilateral version. The knee and the hip flex together, the platform moves away and the torso stays on the pad. The quadriceps extends the knee, the glute and the hamstrings extend the hip, and the foot sends the force into the carriage.

The difference is the split. With two legs, the body picks the comfortable path and the strong leg does more than it seems. With one, each side does its own work and the result shows in the rep count.

## How to set up

One foot in the middle of the platform, pointing straight or slightly out. The pelvis and lower back stay in contact with the pad, and the head rests on it in a neutral position. The archive asked for the opposite, telling you not to rest the head; what you do not do is lift it to watch the platform.

The knee tracks in line with the foot through the whole range. If it collapses inward at the bottom, the weight is too high, the foot is misplaced or the ankle is out of range.

## The symmetry a bilateral press hides

Nobody is symmetrical. One side pushes a little more, one ankle has more range, one hip holds the position better. On a two-leg press those differences blur. On one leg they become the main subject of the set.

The protocol is simple. Always start with the weak side, match the reps of the strong side and add weight only when both sides hit the same number with the same technique. One rep of difference is normal; five is a signal. If the weak side stalls for weeks, check the load on the strong side: the plan is often built around the leg that was already ahead.

A gap corrected on the press tends to show up in the squat and the lunge. The [vertical leg press](/en/vertical-leg-press/) is the other variant in the family and follows the same logic of range.

## Range and foot height

The range does not change because you use one leg: go down as far as the pelvis stays on the pad, exactly as on the bilateral press. Foot height is the lever that decides the split. In the middle, the work is shared between knee and hip; higher up, the hip flexes more and the glute and hamstrings join in; lower down, the hip range shortens and the quadriceps takes more.

Platform depth decides nothing. The archive blamed the glute emphasis on the platform travelling further down; the lever that explains it is foot height, as the guide to [foot position on the leg press](/en/leg-press-foot-position/) sets out.

## The whole foot and the tempo

You push through the whole foot with the heel down. If the heel lifts, the foot is too low or the ankle is out of range.

The descent takes two or three seconds. On a machine with a carriage, the bounce returns part of the work and the knee absorbs the rest.

## Common errors

The first is lifting the pelvis to gain range. The lower back peels off the pad and the load goes exactly where the pad was meant to protect.

The second is twisting the torso toward the pushing side. The hip opens and the foot drifts off the middle of the platform.

The third is pushing with the toes. The whole foot pushes; the toes alone shorten the range and load the ankle.

The fourth is treating the single-leg press as a max-load lift. Its place is control and symmetry.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose is three sets of eight to twelve reps per leg, with ninety to a hundred and twenty seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this is the guide's recommendation. Start with the weak side and bring the strong side down to what it produces.

## Where it fits

It goes at the end of leg day, after the squat or the bilateral press, when maximum load is no longer the goal and attention can go to one side.
