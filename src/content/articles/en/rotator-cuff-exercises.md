The supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis wrap the joint and pull the head of the humerus towards its centre while the arm moves. Their job is stability, and they are trained that way: long sets, small loads and control.

## The four muscles and what each one does

The supraspinatus is an abductor: it helps move the arm away from the body in the first part of the range. The infraspinatus and teres minor rotate the arm outwards. The subscapularis rotates it inwards.

None of the four moves the arm with the strength of a deltoid or a pectoral. What they do is keep the head of the humerus centred so those larger muscles can work without the joint drifting.

## How much, when and with what load

Five to ten minutes, two or three days a week. Two or three exercises of 2 or 3 sets of 15 to 20 reps, with dumbbells of 2.5 to 5 kilos or an equivalent resistance on a cable or band.

The elbow stays at 90 degrees and close to the side through the whole rep. If the elbow drifts away, the movement stops being a rotation and becomes something else.

There is no mandatory order in the session. Training it last is no safer than training it first; what changes is the fatigue it arrives with, and at this load fatigue is not an issue.

## External rotation at zero degrees

Lie on your side on the floor or on a flat bench with a light dumbbell in the top hand. The elbow stays against the side and the forearm crosses the abdomen.

From there, turn the forearm up until it is perpendicular to the floor and lower under control. Two or three sets of 15 to 20 reps per side. This is the position that works the infraspinatus and teres minor most.

On a cable, the handle is set at abdomen height and you step away from the stack so there is tension from the start. The range is the same.

## External rotation at 45 and 90 degrees

Rotation at zero degrees covers one part of the range. The arm also rotates with the shoulder abducted, and those positions show up in every press.

**At 45 degrees.** Standing with the arm about 45 degrees from the torso and the elbow at 90. Turn the forearm up without moving the elbow. Two sets of 15 to 20 reps.

**At 90 degrees.** The arm out to the side, the elbow at shoulder height and the forearm pointing forward. The rotation lifts the forearm up. Here the weight drops further: two sets of 15 reps with the lightest dumbbell you have.

## Internal rotation

The subscapularis is the one almost nobody trains. Lie on your side with the dumbbell in the bottom hand, the elbow against the side and the forearm parallel to the floor. Raise the hand towards the abdomen and return.

Two or three sets of 15 to 20 reps with 2.5 to 5 kilos. On a cable, the handle is at abdomen height and the body stands side-on, with the same range.

## Raise in the scapular plane

The prone horizontal abduction with external rotation was done on a bench set at about 30 degrees to target the supraspinatus with the arm horizontal. The supraspinatus is an abductor, and the position that makes it work is the raise in the scapular plane.

Standing with a light dumbbell in each hand, raise the arms about 30 degrees in front of the frontal plane, with the thumb up. Two or three sets of 12 to 15 reps. The thumb-down version also exists, but it leaves the shoulder in a less comfortable position.

## Face pull and the rest of the shoulder

The [face pull](/en/face-pull/) combines horizontal extension, abduction and external rotation, so it covers part of this work in a single movement. It works as a finisher on a shoulder or back day.

None of this replaces the press. The [shoulder routines](/en/shoulder-routines/) and the [chest routines](/en/chest-routines/) order the heavy work, and the cuff comes along. Training it does not change your anatomy or guarantee that an injury will not happen; it leaves the joint better prepared for what you already do.
