> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. If the pain stops you from putting weight on the foot, appears after a blow or comes with numbness or loss of strength, get advice before you train.

The plantar fascia is a band of tissue that runs from the heel to the toes along the sole of the foot. When you load it beyond what it tolerates, the heel area hurts, especially on the first steps of the day.

## What plantar fasciitis is

It is irritation of the fascia at its insertion into the calcaneus. The repeated force of running and jumping produces small tears near the bone, and that is the area that hurts when you step down.

Although the name speaks of inflammation, what is usually found is tissue worn down by repeated load.

## Why it appears

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons lists several factors that increase the force on the fascia:

- Unsuitable footwear.
- Differences in leg length.
- Tight calves.
- Very high or very low arches.
- Too little recovery time between runs.
- Long hours standing or walking on hard surfaces.
- A high body weight.

None of them is a sentence. They are conditions that change how load is shared, and almost all of them can be modified.

## How it feels and how long it lasts

The pain starts at the heel bone or in the arch, is mild at first and grows over time. The most characteristic sign is the first step when you get up: the fascia has spent the night shortened, and stretching it suddenly hurts.

Light activity relieves it, and symptoms return if the activity goes on too long. That is the clue that the problem is about dose.

Most cases resolve in two to three months. Patience is part of the treatment: whoever changes remedy every week never finds out whether one of them worked.

## What you can do at home

These measures relieve symptoms while you wait for an assessment:

1. **Ice on the heel and the arch.** For 10 to 15 minutes, three or four times a day, with at least an hour between applications. It eases the discomfort; the tissue does not repair itself with cold.
2. **A tennis or golf ball under the foot.** Seated, roll the sole slowly. It helps massage the fascia and move the tissue.
3. **Calf stretch.** 30 to 60 centimetres from the wall, one foot in front of the other. Lean towards the wall with your hands on it and slide the back foot further back without lifting the heel off the floor. Hold for 10 seconds, alternate feet and do 10 reps per side.
4. **Fascia stretch.** About 30 centimetres from the wall, feet shoulder width apart and one in front of the other. Hold the wall edge, sink into a squat with the heels on the floor and hold the low position for 10 seconds. Change feet and repeat up to 20 times.
5. **Low-heeled shoes.** Nothing above 2.5 cm while it lasts. An insole can spread the pressure better if the arch needs it.
6. **Less time barefoot.** At home, shoes with some support load the fascia less than spending hours barefoot.

If the pain stops you from walking, get it looked at before you treat it yourself.

## How to train while it settles

The fascia responds better to relative rest than to absolute rest. Three weeks without moving do not fix the tissue and leave the leg weaker.

Change what sets the pain off and keep the rest:

- Replace running with the bike, the pool or the elliptical for a few days. The guide to [cardiovascular training](/en/cardiovascular-training-in-your-program/) explains how to fit it into the week.
- Cut the volume you walk or stand for, not only what you run.
- Build calf and soleus strength, which is what the fascia appreciates in the medium term.
- Return to running by time rather than distance, with small increases.
- Keep the [warm-up](/en/gym-warm-up/) before the session and gentle [stretching](/en/stretching/) afterwards.

The progressive loading protocol, with its phases and its numbers, is in the guide to [common gym injuries](/en/common-gym-injuries/).

## When to get advice

Book an appointment if the pain has gone more than a month without improving, if it wakes you at night, if you cannot put weight on the foot or if it appears in both feet at once with no clear reason.

Conservative treatment includes physiotherapy, stretching, footwear and, sometimes, cortisone injections. Anti-inflammatories relieve and do not repair the tissue, and some doctors consider that injections can slow healing. The decision belongs to the professional.

Surgery is considered when everything else fails. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, only 44% of operated patients reported complete disappearance of symptoms. That is a figure to weigh twice before going into an operating room.
