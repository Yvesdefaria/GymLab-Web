The donkey position is the oldest version of calf work and the one least seen in a gym full of machines. The body folds forward, the hips rest on a support and the heels rise on a block. What separates it from a standing raise is not the muscle, which is the same, but how the load arrives and how far the ankle can travel.

## Why the tilted torso changes the exercise

With an upright torso, the weight of a bar or a machine falls on the shoulders and the traps have to hold it. That tension compresses the neck and limits the movement: the body gets short exactly when the ankle needs to get long.

In the donkey position the weight rests on the hips and runs along the spine. The traps stay free and the torso can hang forward without fighting the load. The practical result is a heel that drops further and a plantar flexion that rises higher, because nothing pulls the body up.

## The stretch does not come from the hip

It is tempting to explain the donkey position as a calf stretch through the hip. It does not work that way. The gastrocnemius crosses the knee and the ankle, not the hip, so bending the hip does not lengthen it. What stretches the muscle is the ankle as the heel drops, and that part of the range is the same standing as it is bent over.

What does change is the freedom to use that range. With no load on the traps and no balance to keep, the body focuses on a single joint. The [anatomy of the calf and soleus](/en/calf-anatomy/) makes clear which joints each muscle crosses and why the hip is not part of the count.

## How to set up

The hips rest on the machine pad or on the edge of a bench, with the torso tilted and the back straight. In the partner version, the weight sits over the sacrum, not the lower back, and the hands hold a fixed support to keep the position.

The balls of the feet go on a block or a step, with the heels outside and the ankle free. The knees stay extended without locking, as on the [standing machine](/en/standing-calf-raise-machine/). A low block does not let the heel drop far enough; a very high one lets the foot slide.

## Range and pause

The rep starts with the heel below the block and the calf stretched. From there the ankle rises to full plantar flexion and holds for a second at the top. The descent is controlled back to the stretch.

That second at the top is what separates a useful set from a bounce. The calf has a short range and momentum shortens it further. If the heel rises and falls without stopping, the work is shared between the tendon and inertia, and the muscle keeps little of it.

## How much weight and how many sets

The dose this page proposes is three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. If the machine or the partner allows real loading, the alternative is three to six sets of 6 to 10 reps with a one to two second pause at the top. It is a recommendation from this article.

The position takes a fair amount of weight because the body is supported. Even so, range rules: if the load forces a shorter descent or a bent knee, there is too much weight. In the partner version, the load changes by changing the person, and that is already a practical limit.

## Common mistakes

- **Bending the knee on the way up.** The gastrocnemius shortens from above and the soleus takes over without the set asking for it.
- **Resting the weight on the lower back.** The support goes on the hips; if the weight sits higher, the back arches and the movement turns uncomfortable.
- **Cutting the stretch.** Without the heel below the block, the rep starts halfway.
- **Watching the toes.** Toe direction changes emphasis very little; what changes the stimulus is range and the pause.

## What to pair it with

The donkey position keeps the knee extended, so it shares its muscle with the [standing machine](/en/standing-calf-raise-machine/) and the [barbell raise](/en/barbell-calf-raise/). Choosing one of the three is a question of available equipment; doing all three in the same session repeats the same stimulus.

Covering the soleus needs a seated variation, such as [dumbbells on the thigh](/en/seated-dumbbell-calf-raise/) or the seated machine. The guide to [standing versus seated raises](/en/standing-vs-seated-calf-raises/) explains the split, and the [calf routine without machines](/en/calf-routine-no-machines/) solves the home version.

If you are looking for less common ways to load the calf, the guide to [unconventional calf training](/en/unconventional-calf-training/) gathers variations that do not depend on the standing machine. The donkey raise belongs there: awkward to set up, but it leaves the ankle free and the torso out of the way.
