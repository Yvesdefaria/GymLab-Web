It is hard to find a gym without music. What is surprising is how little attention the people running the room pay to it: in general the only thing that seems to matter is that the place is filled with some kind of noise. Televisions added to entertain people who would rather forget they are training, and aerobics rooms without soundproofing, point the same way.

## What music adds

Music has a mobilising power thanks to its rhythmic and emotional nature. What it adds is an atmosphere that can help you focus and turn your attention inwards on the effort.

Two concepts get confused here: excitement and stimulation. Excitement is a turbulent, disordered mobilisation of superficial energies. Stimulation unlocks deeper energies and activates them in a controlled way. Training with music works when it stimulates, not when it merely speeds you up.

## How to choose the right music

The most widespread opinion says that the more frantic the gym music, the more stimulating it is. That principle is false.

There are three criteria: music that stimulates without exciting, that favours focus and turning inwards, and that creates a relaxed, easy atmosphere without going flat. They are general rules, but they give you a coherent line to follow. That is the training music worth looking for.

## Bringing your own music to the gym

If your workouts have become too ordinary, changing the music is a simple way to motivate yourself and train better. Bringing your own songs to the gym gives you control over the atmosphere, which the room's music does not allow.

The obvious question is whether music can affect exercise and muscle strength. You can test the answer in any session.

## How it boosts your training

The test used a group of trained bodybuilders who worked shoulders twice under different conditions, to look at the effect of training with music on strength. Both routines consisted of three sets of dumbbell press, three sets of Smith machine rows and three sets of lateral raises, all at 10-rep maximums with every set taken to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/).

In one session they trained with their favourite music; in the other, without music. On every set of every shoulder exercise they averaged at least one extra rep with music, and in some cases two more.

Motivation explains much of the effect. Music pushes the way a soundtrack pushes a dramatic film scene. More formal studies have found that runners can increase their speed and reduce fatigue, so it is reasonable to expect something similar when you lift weights: less fatigue and more strength.

## A practical tip

Before your next workout, build a playlist of your favourite songs and use it as part of the plan. You will get more strength and less fatigue, mainly from the motivation the music provides.

There is a second reason: with headphones on you remove distractions such as conversations with other gym members. That protects your focus and also stops the [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) from stretching out.

Music supports the plan, and the plan comes first. If the routine has basic flaws, the best playlist will not hide them: check the most [common training mistakes](/en/common-training-mistakes/) before trusting your progress to a soundtrack. Training with music is one more tool, and it works best when the rest of the plan is in order.

If you are one of the few who still trains without music, try one session with your playlist. If you already do it, share which music really motivates you: someone else's list is a good way to find your own. And if the room still feels intimidating, the guide to your [first day at the gym](/en/first-day-at-the-gym/) helps you arrive with a clear plan.
