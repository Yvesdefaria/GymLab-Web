The day the magazine published has one virtue and one problem. The virtue is order: meals every two or three hours, protein in every one, carbohydrate around training. The problem is scale: 9,394 calories and 910 grams of protein for a champion of about 100 kilos. Reading it as a template would be a mistake; reading it as an extreme case, with its numbers, teaches more.

## The day, meal by meal

The text spreads eight meals between seven in the morning and one at night, and adds three intakes around the session. The table keeps the times and groups the pre-workout, the intra-workout drink and the post-workout shake into one row so the arithmetic closes.

The eight meals add up to 910 grams of protein, 881 of carbohydrate and 239 of fat. The restaurant meal alone contributes 2,407 calories: more than a full day for many people. The table records what one professional ate in a specific phase.

## The arithmetic that does not scale

At about 100 kilos, 910 grams of protein is roughly 9 grams per kilo, and 9,394 calories is about 94 per kilo. The range that covers muscle building for most people who train sits between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo. The difference is four times the useful ceiling.

Excess protein does not turn into extra muscle. It is used for energy, stored or excreted, and along the way it costs money, displaces fibre and fills the stomach with a nutrient that was already covered. The page on [too much protein](/en/too-much-protein/) details the real cost and why there is no prize for overshooting.

Eating every two hours is not required either. Heath's day has that frequency because his food volume does not fit in fewer meals; for someone at 70 kilos, four or five meals are enough. Frequency is a consequence of the total, not a cause of the result.

## What can be learned

First, protein at every meal, including the restaurant one. Second, carbohydrate around training: rice before and after, potato and sweet potato in the big meals. Third, vegetables in the afternoon and evening meals.

The other lesson is planning the social meal. The restaurant dinner sits inside the day, not outside the plan: it is chosen, counted and balanced in the next meal. That flexibility is more useful than the exact calorie figure.

And the last lesson is what not to do: copy the portions. A 9,394-calorie menu does not transfer to a 75-kilo person without turning the surplus into fat within weeks.

## The record, updated

Phil Heath was born in Seattle in 1979. The text says he played basketball, returned to bodybuilding in 2002 and won the 2005 NPC USA, the show that grants the pro card. In his first professional year, 2006, he took the New York Pro and the Colorado Pro.

His actual record is longer than the one the original article carried: seven Mr. Olympia titles between 2011 and 2017. That part is verifiable. The rest, the nickname and the anecdotes, reads as what it is: magazine material, not a checked biography.

## How it translates to your week

If you want to use this case as a reference, keep the structure: four to six meals, protein in each, carbohydrate near training and vegetables in the main meals. Set the total from your weight and expenditure, and leave Heath's dose in the archive.

The routine and diet of [Ronnie Coleman](/en/ronnie-coleman-routine-and-diet/) show another elite case with equally extreme numbers. The [Lee Haney diet](/en/lee-haney-diet/) drops to about 3,460 calories and the [Dorian Yates diet](/en/dorian-yates-diet/) to 3,500, with a split that can be adapted. Comparing the three teaches more than any isolated figure.
