Behind the thigh sit three muscles that pull on the knee and, except for the short head of the biceps femoris, on the hip as well. When they fall behind, the imbalance shows in your profile and in the joint.

## The three muscles at the back of the thigh

### The biceps femoris

It has two heads. The long head arises on the ischial tuberosity, in the pelvis; the short head, on the linea aspera of the femur. Both join into one tendon that inserts on the head of the fibula and the lateral condyle of the tibia. It is the only one of the three that takes part in external rotation of the leg.

### The semitendinosus

It arises on the ischial tuberosity and inserts on the proximal part of the medial surface of the tibia. Its tendon is long and superficial, and you can feel it on the inner side of the thigh near the knee.

### The semimembranosus

It also arises on the ischial tuberosity and ends on the medial condyle of the tibia, behind the semitendinosus. It is the deepest of the three and the least visible.

## What they do

All three flex the knee and, except for the short head of the biceps femoris, extend the hip. That second function sets them apart from the quadriceps and explains their role in gait: when you walk or run they brake knee extension and control the descent of the body.

With the knee bent they also rotate the tibia, the biceps femoris outward and the two medial muscles inward. And they support the knee alongside the quadriceps, which is why an imbalance between the two groups shows up in the joint.

## Two stimulus routes

They can be trained in two ways, and the two are not interchangeable.

The first is knee flexion: seated curl, lying curl, standing curl, ball curl or manual resistance. The hip stays still and the muscle works by shortening from the knee.

The second is hip extension: stiff-leg deadlift, Romanian deadlift and good mornings. The knee barely moves and the work comes from the pelvis.

Both routes load the same group from different angles, and a complete plan includes both.

## How emphasis shifts

You cannot isolate one of the three muscles: what stance changes is the split of work, not the muscle that contracts, and [quadriceps myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) explains the same rule. On the hamstrings the lever that matters is the joint that moves.

With the knees slightly bent in a deadlift, the upper part of the group takes more emphasis; with straight legs, the stimulus centres on the lower portion and the associated tendons.

Foot rotation on the curl changes little, even though it gets repeated a lot. Turning the toes out or in tilts the balance somewhat towards the biceps femoris or towards the two medial muscles, but the effect is small and does not replace choosing the right exercise.

## Why train them

The hamstrings get skipped when the goal is size and strength in the lower body. The result is an imbalance you can see in profile and feel in the knee.

Strong hamstrings contribute to hip extension and lumbar stability, and they complete the shape of the thigh. Together with the quadriceps, they form the stable base that protects the knee in fast movements.

## How to distribute the work

A well-built hamstring session combines a knee flexion and a hip extension, with more volume on whichever your anatomy handles worse. The [hamstring routine](/en/jay-cutler-hamstring-routine/) shows an example with sets and reps, and the [hamstring training guide](/en/hamstring-training-guide/) develops the technique of each curl.

On the leg press, a high foot position shifts part of the work towards this group, although the movement still comes from the knee. [Leg press foot position](/en/leg-press-foot-position/) explains how much changes and how much does not.
