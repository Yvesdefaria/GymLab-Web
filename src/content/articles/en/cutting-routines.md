Getting lean is a phase: you eat less and train to keep what you have. This page collects the eleven published plans in the block and explains what to check before picking one.

## What changes on a cut

Three things. Calories come down, cardio goes up and the weight on the bar stays where it is. The deficit does the job training cannot do, and training protects what the deficit would otherwise take.

Cardio stops being a punishment for eating and becomes a tool with a dose. Two or three sessions of 30 to 40 minutes at a conversational pace are enough to start. Intervals come in when time is tight or weight stalls.

## What does not change

Sets still finish near failure, rests are still there so the set comes out clean, and progression still exists. A cut progresses less and preserves more, but a plan without overload is a plan that only loses.

Neither does the muscle you want to keep change. Stop giving it a reason to stay and it leaves. The load is that reason, which is why every plan in this block insists on it.

## The three rules

- **Keep the load.** Do not trade weight for reps just because you are in a deficit. Add reps or density until the target set comes out clean, then deload. If strength drops two sessions in a row, the deficit is the problem, not the plan.
- **Control the deficit.** Moderate and sustained. Slashing calories moves the scale for a few days and then takes muscle, strength and motivation with it. A deficit that lets you train beats one that leaves you on the couch.
- **Count cardio as a tool.** It has a dose and a cost. If it steals strength from your weight session, there is too much cardio, not too little. The [aerobic exercise](/en/aerobic-exercise/) and [HIIT training](/en/hiit-training-guide/) guides explain how to spread it out.

Every routine in the table runs on the same logic. What changes is the number of days, the volume and the cardio dose.

## Which one to pick

With three days, the weights for fat loss plan orders the week into legs, push and pull. If you prefer pairs of opposing muscles and a little more density, supersets do the same job with another structure. With four days, the torso and legs split gives you two upper sessions and two lower ones, with cardio in the gaps.

With five days, the advanced three-month plan and the heavy-load plan carry more volume. The first alternates options every week; the second adds cardio minutes every two weeks.

If time is the constraint, the 30-minute sessions solve the day. Read the cardio column before you commit: two routines with the same number of weight days can ask for very different cardio time. For a short shock, the high-frequency routine lasts one to three weeks and the hundred-rep plan stays under two. Neither is a year-round plan.

## The diet

The deficit is built in the kitchen and paid for in the gym. Without a moderate deficit, eight weeks of weights and cardio improve fitness and leave weight where it was. With one, strength holds and the scale moves.

If you come from a volume phase, the [volume routines](/en/volume-routines/) index explains what is kept when calories drop. If your goal is the opposite, the [how to gain weight](/en/how-to-gain-weight/) guide covers the other direction. And if you want the strength base to cut without losing kilos off the bar, the [strength routines](/en/strength-routines/) are the starting point.

[High-intensity training](/en/high-intensity-training/) and [supersets](/en/supersets/) are the two techniques that show up most in this block. Neither replaces a well-set deficit, and the rest is patience.
