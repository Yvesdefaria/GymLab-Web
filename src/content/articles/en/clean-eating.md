The word clean suggests there is dirty food, and that idea does not help anyone hold a plan. A food does not clean or contaminate the body: it brings calories, protein, fat, fibre, vitamins and minerals in certain amounts. The useful decision is what gets eaten most of the week and what is left for exceptions.

## What it means in practice

Clean eating, in the sense that helps, is a diet with more food cooked at home and fewer products that are opened and eaten without thinking. Frozen food, tinned food and jars count: a pack of cooked chickpeas or a bag of frozen vegetables solves a meal as well as a market run.

The base is simple: a protein source, a carbohydrate source, plenty of vegetables and measured fat. Variety comes on its own when the dishes change, not when twenty different products go into the trolley.

Cooking is the lever that changes the result most, because it hands back control. A grilled breast with weighed oil, a pot of lentils and a tray of oven vegetables cover three meals without an app or a complicated recipe.

## The 80/20

A plan that bans foods loses to one that gives them a slot. If 80 percent of meals come from cooked food, the other 20 allow a social dinner, a dessert or a beer without the month collapsing. Total restriction usually ends in binges, and a binge weighs more than the planned dinner.

Social meals get planned: eat less at the previous meal, walk more that day and return to the plan at the next meal. One meal ruins nothing; three weeks of daily exceptions do.

## The myths that can go

Eating every two or three hours does not speed up metabolism. The cost of digesting food is around 10 percent of the total and depends on how much is eaten, not how often. Three or five meals work the same if the total adds up.

Carbohydrates are not needed to metabolise protein. The body uses amino acids to build tissue and gets the glucose it needs through gluconeogenesis when the diet does not supply it. Cutting carbohydrates entirely does not improve body composition on its own; the deficit decides.

No food prevents cancer. Risk depends on the whole diet, weight, alcohol and tobacco, and serious reviews find no miracle food. There is no obligation to drink two to three litres either: thirst, urine colour, heat and training set the need, and the water in food counts.

Meals do not burn fat. The thermic effect exists and is small, and no food has a magic effect on fat tissue. The body does not need detoxing either: the liver and kidneys do that work every day.

## How to stock the kitchen

A short list solves the week: eggs, Greek yoghurt, cooked legumes, oats, rice, frozen vegetables, tomato, olive oil, vinegar, spices and seasonal fruit. With that you can put together breakfast, lunch and dinner without improvising.

Batch prep saves decisions. Ninety minutes on a Sunday are enough to cook 500 grams of rice, roast a tray of vegetables and leave a protein ready, whether chicken, tofu or legumes. Wednesday night will be grateful.

## How it is measured

Adherence is measured in days per week, not in perfection. If the plan is followed less than 80 percent of the time, the problem is usually the plan and not willpower. Energy in training, hunger between meals, digestion and the three-week weight trend say more than a single day.

Weighing food for a week, without obsession, calibrates the eye. After that you estimate with references: a palm of protein, a fist of carbohydrate and two hands of vegetables. The guide to [tracking what you eat](/en/how-to-track-what-you-eat/) develops that method.

One bad day does not erase the week. Discipline is built from small repeated decisions and from a kitchen that does not force improvisation. The guide to [diet discipline](/en/diet-discipline/) works on that part, and [diet mistakes](/en/diet-mistakes-to-avoid/) review the most common slips.
