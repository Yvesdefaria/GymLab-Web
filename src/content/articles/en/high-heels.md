A high heel does three things at once: it lifts the heel, narrows the toe box in most models and changes how load is shared. The result shows in the foot, the knee and the back, and it appears on a short walk as much as on an eight-hour day.

## What changes when the heel rises

The ankle sits in plantarflexion, as if you were standing on tiptoe. Body weight shifts forward and the forefoot takes a larger share of the load. The higher the heel, the bigger the change, and a narrow toe box makes it worse by squeezing the toes in the same direction.

The posterior chain adapts. The calf, soleus and Achilles tendon work through a shorter range and, over years of use, can lose length and elasticity. The pelvis tilts and lumbar lordosis tends to increase to compensate. None of it hurts on day one; it appears once the body has made the adaptation.

## Heels and cellulite: the part that does not hold

Cellulite is a feature of subcutaneous tissue: fat lobules push against collagen bands and the skin shows dimples. It depends on genetics, sex and hormonal change. A shoe does not create it, and no cream removes it.

The venous return argument has a real part. Hours on your feet or sitting down make leg swelling worse, and a high heel reduces the work of the calf pump. From there to saying that heels cause cellulite is a jump the studies do not make. The page on [water retention](/en/water-retention/) explains where normal puffiness ends and oedema begins.

## How to lower the cost if you wear them

Rotating heights and saving heels for occasions cuts exposure. Toe box width matters as much as height: a 4 cm heel with room for the toes punishes less than a 6 cm heel with a narrow front.

Mobility and strength work is the other half. The guide to [correct posture](/en/correct-posture-for-training/) covers trunk control; [strong lower back](/en/strong-lower-back/) covers the area that feels the lordosis; and [protecting the knees](/en/protect-knees-quad-training/) explains what to do with the quads.

## A short routine for ankle and calf

Warm-up: five minutes of walking, ten ankle circles per side and two sets of ten slow raises with no load.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standing heel raise | 3 | 12 to 15 |
| Seated heel raise | 3 | 15 |
| Ankle mobility at the wall | 2 | 10 per side |
| Single-leg balance | 2 | 30 seconds |

Load rule: start with bodyweight and add a backpack when fifteen reps feel easy. Progression: add a set every two weeks and move to one leg once the thirty-second balance is steady.

## When to change the habit or see someone

Pain that lasts more than two weeks, tingling between the toes, a toe that no longer straightens or back pain that wakes you at night are reasons to ask. A podiatrist or physiotherapist can assess gait and mobility.

As a reference, a 2 to 3 cm heel changes the ankle angle little and one above 5 cm clearly shifts the support. The practical decision is a matter of hours and rotation.
