Your eyes do not move the bar, but they decide where the chest goes. In a loaded squat, the gaze is the first warning that the descent is falling apart: when your eyes drop to the floor, the chin follows and the spine loses the angle you had chosen.

## What the gaze does in the squat

The gaze holds the neck in a neutral position while the chest stays tall. The head is heavy and sits on the neck; tilt it and the rest of the spine tends to follow. A fixed point at eye height keeps the back of the neck in line with the spine through the whole range.

The reference is simple: pick a point at your eye height, two or three metres away, and do not lose it until the set is over. If you train in front of a mirror, the point can be your own reflection; if you train at home, a mark on the wall, the edge of a shelf or a bottle on a ledge will do.

## The study: ten rugby players

A paper published in the *Journal of Sports Sciences* and written by researchers at the University of Miami measured squat biomechanics in ten rugby players with at least a year of experience in the lift.

The protocol was short: two sets of five reps at 25% of their one-rep max, repeated with the gaze on a different target each time. One target was straight ahead, one was a corner of the ceiling and one was a corner of the floor.

With the eyes on the floor, forward hip tilt increased by 8% and spinal flexion by 2% compared with looking ahead. The authors found no difference between looking ahead and looking up.

## What the 8% and the 2% mean

The study describes how the body moves with each gaze target. Ten players, two sets of five reps and one measurement session: there is no follow-up over months and no record of pain. The two numbers describe a position, and turning them into an injury forecast adds something that was never measured.

What they do say is that the gaze is part of the chain. Look down and the hips tilt further, and the spine flexes a little more than you planned. In a heavy set, that margin shows in the back before anywhere else.

The rest of the evidence on the squat points the same way: technique and progression carry more weight than any single detail. The guide on [whether the squat is dangerous](/en/squat-safety/) reviews the knee and back studies in more detail.

## Looking at the ceiling is no better

In the study, the high gaze did not change the kinematics. Looking at the ceiling requires extending the neck, and the neck gains nothing from holding a heavy bar in that position. The head follows the trunk, so taking it back costs you the balance reference.

The working point is the line of your eyes, with the gaze parallel to the floor.

## How to set it up at home

A garage or a living room has no ceiling corners to look at. Any stable reference will do: a strip of tape on the wall, a door hinge, a bottle on a shelf or the edge of a window. What matters is that it sits at eye height and does not change between sets.

Film the set side-on with your phone and you will see in two seconds whether the gaze dropped to the floor on the last reps. It is the cheapest fatigue signal you have.

## The full setup in five points

The gaze is one part of a setup that starts before the bar moves.

1. **Shoulders:** the bar rests on the rear deltoids and the traps, with the hands a little wider than the shoulders.
2. **Feet:** shoulder-width or hip-width apart, knees slightly bent in the starting position and toes turned out a little.
3. **Lower back:** keep the natural curve, the abs braced and the chest tall.
4. **Descent:** the hips lead, travelling back and down while the knees bend.
5. **Ascent:** push through the feet with the weight spread evenly and bring the hips forward to the starting position.

## How low to go

The useful depth is the one you can hold with a neutral spine and no pain, and that height changes with each person's mobility. Some lifters go until the hips sit below the knees; others find their limit sooner.

## The gaze does not replace technique

Fix the point, lower under control and let the set end when the position breaks. The gaze helps keep the chest tall, and there its job stops. To review the rest of the execution, the guide on [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) covers the pause, the lowering and locking out the joint.
