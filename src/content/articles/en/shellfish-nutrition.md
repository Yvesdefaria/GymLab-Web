Crabs, lobsters, langoustines, clams, mussels, scallops and cockles share a similar profile. Most of their weight is water and protein, and fat stays below 5 % in almost every species.

## What shellfish brings

For every 100 g cooked, most species land between 15 and 25 g of protein, a figure that competes with lean meat without its saturated fat. Oysters are the exception, lighter and lower in calories.

Minerals are the other argument. Zinc and iodine appear in amounts few foods cover, and selenium and iron come along. B12 is high, especially in mussels and clams. With that combination, shellfish fits a cutting diet, where every calorie has to justify its share of protein.

## Portion and frequency

A normal portion is 125 to 150 g raw, without shell. Two or three times a week covers shellfish's share of the general fish and seafood recommendation. The guide to [fish consumption](/en/fish-consumption-guide/) spreads the week's servings and explains why oily fish has its own rule.

Fresh shellfish is at its best between November and March. Outside that window you find it frozen, which keeps protein and minerals well. When buying, look for closed shells, a sea smell and no cloudy liquid.

Cooking changes the weight. A hundred and fifty grams of mussels in the shell yield far less meat, and a 125 to 150 g raw portion ends up as 100 or 120 g on the plate. The protein count is made on what you eat, not on what you buy.

## The two limits: salt and purines

Shellfish is salty by nature, and cooked in salted water it adds more sodium than it seems. The other limit is purines, compounds the body turns into uric acid. Both matter if you have high blood pressure or have had a bout of gout.

With high blood pressure or gout, the amount and the frequency are a clinical decision. This page describes the limit; the plan comes from the doctor handling the case, with the blood test and the medication in front of them.

## What it is credited with and does not hold

In popular articles you find properties that the composition of shellfish does not support. Crab does not stand out for vitamin C, and lobster is not a calcium source comparable to milk. The B12 in scallops does not protect against colon cancer, tryptophan does not treat insomnia and tyrosine does not improve mental capacity. Nor is there a basis for presenting shellfish as a way to prevent cardiovascular disease or cancer.

What does hold is the basics: complete protein, iodine, zinc, selenium, B12 and little fat. That is enough to earn a place in the week. The list of invented properties repeats because it sounds good and almost nobody checks it; a composition table is enough to see that shellfish is a good food and not a medicine.

## Fresh, frozen or canned

Frozen shellfish keeps protein and minerals, and is usually the practical option out of season. Canned mussels or clams add salt, so it is worth reading the label and draining them before use. Cooked shellfish sold ready to eat is convenient, though it loses texture and gains sodium.

## How to cook it

The simplest cooking is the best: clean seawater or water with sea salt, and little time. Shellfish goes past its point in two minutes and the texture turns from firm to rubbery. Pair it with vegetables, salad or rice to complete the plate. Breading and butter sauces add fat and salt that the shellfish did not need.

## Shellfish in your diet

If the problem is quantity, the guide to [portion sizes](/en/portion-sizes/) explains how to measure with your hand and plate without weighing every meal. To complete the week, [nuts](/en/nuts-nutrition/) bring unsaturated fat and minerals in a small portion. And if you eat out, the [sushi](/en/sushi-and-protein/) guide helps you pick pieces with quality protein without filling the plate with rice and sauce.

With two servings a week and a portion of vegetables alongside, the plate covers protein, iodine and fibre without adding fat. That is all the strategy a food needs when it already does its job.
