Shawn Ray competed for three decades and never won the Mr. Olympia. Even so, his record places him among the best: twelve top-five finishes at the Olympia, half of them top three, and wins at several Ironman and Arnold Classic contests.

He weighed between 92 and 97 kilos and beat rivals twenty kilos heavier. It was the triumph of symmetry and balance over the biggest bodies. The list of champions he never joined is in the guide to [Mr. Olympia winners](/en/mr-olympia-winners/).

## An old-school competitor

Once he found the formula that worked for him, he stopped trying other systems. His trust in what suited him best and his self-discipline sustained a long career.

He trained with intensity and, besides the weights, did boxing drills with his [training partners](/en/training-partner-benefits/). He failed the university nutrition course and never counted calories or grams of protein, carbs or fat. He did not weigh food or log sessions either.

His four elements were individuality, confidence, intensity and fun. The mirror was his best friend during prep, and the scale did not feature in his plans.

## The split: six days, three on and one off

He trained six days a week with three on and one off. On training days there were two sessions, one in the morning and one in the afternoon.

Calves and abs appeared three times a week, usually Monday, Wednesday and Friday. For the large muscles he did four exercises; for biceps and triceps, three. Every exercise had one warm-up set and four working sets.

The first two working sets ran 10 to 15 reps, sometimes only 8. The last two were a triple drop to 30 reps: ten with one weight, drop the load and do ten more, drop again and do the last ten. That scheme was his favourite tool, and the [training volume](/en/training-volume/) of a session like that adds up fast.

The rest day every fourth day was not negotiable. [Muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) explains why a plan with double sessions needs it.

## The diet: six strict days and one free day

He followed a strict diet six days and the seventh was a cheat day. He kept protein in every meal and then ate whatever he fancied. He usually had two slices of pizza instead of a whole one, because the protein had already filled him up.

He ate every three hours and drank around nine litres of water a day during prep. When preparing he ate out often: after training he had no energy to cook and preferred a restaurant.

His sample diet looked like this:

- **Meal 1:** two steaks (about 450 g), broccoli and two slices of watermelon.
- **Meal 2:** twelve egg whites and half a cup of oats.
- **Meal 3:** three chicken breasts (about 350 g), broccoli and asparagus.
- **Meal 4:** three chicken breasts (about 350 g) and broccoli.
- **Meal 5:** two steaks or beef (about 450 g), a baked potato and salad.
- **Meal 6:** twelve egg whites.

The amounts are his. The list adds up to nearly two kilos of meat and two dozen egg whites a day, and it is not a recommendation for anyone.

## The final week

In the seven days before a show he changed the carbs, not the protein. In the first half he cut oats, potato and rice to three meals and left the rest as vegetables and salad. If he needed to empty out more, he removed the potato and rice in the last days of that stretch.

Wednesday and Thursday brought the load: a large steak with two sweet potatoes, oats with banana or honey, and steak with potato again. On Friday he adjusted by what he saw in the mirror: if he was full he cut carbs, and if he was flat he repeated Wednesday's food. On Saturday he ate two baked potatoes before competing.

During that week he also manipulated his water. He raised his intake a great deal and cut it back in the days before the contest. It is a dangerous practice, and this page does not reproduce the litre ladder he used.

> **Notice:** Manipulating water and salts in the final week can cause dehydration, dizziness and kidney problems. This page describes what one competitor did and is not a recommendation or a plan to copy.

## Cardio: how he cut it before the show

He stopped cardio eight days before the event, with the last session on the Friday. The last leg session fell on the Tuesday before.

Out of season he followed no programme. He started fourteen weeks out with three 45-minute sessions a week on the treadmill or stationary bike. On the treadmill he used a 4% incline and 6.5 km/h; on the bike, level 3.

Ten weeks out he did one 45-minute session a day, Monday to Friday. At eight weeks, two sessions a day. At five, four sessions a week. At three, three sessions. In the final week, nothing.

He preferred to arrive prepared and taper the cardio rather than add it at the end. Anyone who accelerates the process in the last weeks tends to show up flat. The guide to the [deload week](/en/deload-week/) explains that logic applied to training.

## Posing and tanning

He practised posing from five weeks out. On training days, after the morning session and before cardio, he did three sets of quarter turns and mandatory poses, each held for ten seconds. He kept going until the day before the contest.

Music was the centre of his routine. Choosing the song was the hardest part; he looked for one that represented his physique and that the audience would associate with him.

He tanned from five weeks out: four sun sessions a week, thirty minutes per side. In bad weather he used three UV sessions. The day before, he applied two coats of colour.

## What remains of his method

Prepare early, arrive on the Wednesday to shake off the flight, look at the mirror before the scale, accept criticism and depend on no nutritionist or coach.

His preparation assumed years of base and a body used to that volume. This page reports it as what it was: his method.

He was never satisfied with his physique at any competition. Satisfaction, he said, only comes with the Sandow, and while he did not have it he looked for another challenge to motivate him.

His career rested on an idea he repeated several times: bodybuilding is learned for life and you never graduate. Shawn Ray's preparation is the small print of that idea, with its numbers, its habits and a final week that would read as a warning today.
