It is a single-joint exercise: only the hip moves. The gluteus maximus performs the extension, the hamstrings assist and the lower back stays still as a stabiliser. Once the spine starts moving, the exercise changes hands.

## Which muscles work in hip extension

The gluteus maximus is the main hip extensor and the one that gives the area its size. The hamstrings cross the hip and take part in the same movement, so the work is never the glute's alone.

The lower back does not extend the hip, but it keeps the torso stable. That difference is the point of this page: the leg moves, the spine does not. The range and the attachments are in [hip and glute anatomy](/en/hip-anatomy/).

## How to do it standing

Stand with the feet at hip width and the knees slightly bent. Put one hand on a wall or on the cable frame to keep your balance.

Take one leg forward until the thigh is roughly parallel to the floor and extend it back from there. That starting range widens the movement without moving the torso. Hold the contraction for one second and return slowly.

The support leg does not change position. If the hip on the working side opens outwards to gain height, the range is coming from somewhere else.

## Where the range ends

The extension ends 10 to 20 degrees past the neutral line of the body. From there, the leg does not rise any further: the lower back does.

That is the real limit. If you feel the lower back more than the glute at the end of the movement, you have gone past the useful point. The fix is simple: lower the height, squeeze for a second where the glute still works and come back.

Thinking about driving the heel back helps more than thinking about lifting the leg. The first image keeps the hip in place; the second invites an arch.

## Variants: standing, kneeling, bench and machine

All versions move the same joint. What changes is the support, the range and the load you can add.

In the kneeling version, the knee of the working leg can stay bent. A bent knee simplifies the movement; a straight leg makes it harder and brings the hamstring in more.

## How to add load

Bodyweight runs out fast. A resistance band, an ankle weight and a low pulley add resistance without changing the pattern. The cable version has its own page, with the three levers that set the emphasis: the [cable glute kickback](/en/cable-glute-kickback/).

Machines that rest the foot on a platform also move the knee, so the quadriceps joins in. It is still hip extension, but the share changes.

## Sets, reps and rest

The recommendation for this page: three sets of 12 to 15 reps per side, resting 45 to 60 seconds. The one second pause at the top is part of every rep.

With no load, the exercise stops being enough within a few weeks. The progression is a band or an ankle weight, then a cable or a machine, adding weight once all three sets are complete and the back stays quiet. The criterion for deciding when to add load is in the [progressive overload principle](/en/progressive-overload-principle/).

## Where it fits in the routine

Hip extension works as the last exercise of a glute day, when the load cannot be high and what counts is direct work. It also serves as activation before the big movements, with two light sets.

It does not replace the squat, the deadlift or the hip thrust. The exercises that move more weight build most of the volume, and this one finishes the job with the glute already tired. To choose the main lift, the reasons behind the [hip thrust](/en/hip-thrust-benefits/) help you decide.
