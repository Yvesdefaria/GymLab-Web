Most curls lose tension at the bottom. Lying under a high pulley, the cable arrives from another direction and the biceps keeps holding the weight while the arm is extended. The whole difference is where you put the pulley.

## The geometry of the setup

The bench goes under a high pulley with your head about 30 centimetres from the weight stack. You lie face up, knees bent and feet flat, and grip the bar or the rope at shoulder width.

The arms start extended toward the ceiling with the elbows pointing up. The upper arm does not move during the set: only the elbow turns. The wrist stays fixed and the range ends near your forehead.

The pulley has to sit above and behind your head. If it is level with your chest or at the foot end of the bench, the cable pulls in line with the forearm and the exercise loses the thing that makes it different. That setup detail is the exercise.

## Why the tension stays at the bottom

In a standing curl, the bar hangs in line with the forearm at the bottom. The force passes through the elbow and the biceps stops holding almost all the weight. That is the point where the rep can rest.

Here the cable comes from above and behind the head. At the bottom it still forms an angle with the forearm, so the biceps keeps holding the weight. The difference is the direction of the cable.

The range is short and the tension sits in the middle. With a weight that lets you reach your forehead without moving the shoulder, the set works the biceps from start to finish, without the dead zone of the standing curl.

## The short-head family

The shoulder stays flexed with the arm in front of the torso, just as on a preacher bench. The short head works shortened and the long head has less room. The cable adds tension at the bottom, which the preacher bench does not have.

The [preacher curl with an EZ bar](/en/preacher-curl-ez-bar/) and the [spider curl](/en/spider-curl/) cover the same head with different support. This one adds the cable and changes the axis of the resistance.

## Bar, rope or single handle

The straight bar keeps both hands fixed and is the easiest to load. The rope lets the wrists turn slightly through the rep and is gentler on the elbows. A single handle exposes whether one side is doing more work, which a bar can hide.

## Mistakes that move the elbow

- **Pulling the elbows toward your feet.** The elbow stops pointing at the ceiling and the curl turns into a pulldown.
- **Lifting the hips.** The body looks for momentum and the shoulder joins the movement.
- **Pulley too low.** Without the cable above your head, the angle disappears and the exercise looks like a lying bar curl.
- **Pulling with the shoulder.** If the arm drifts toward the torso, the delt does part of the work.
- **Dropping the return.** The cable brings the weight back fast and the biceps loses the phase that stretches it most.

## The dose

Three sets of 10 to 12 reps with 90 seconds of rest and a one-second pause near the forehead. The dose is this page's recommendation; the original gives no numbers. It goes at the end of the session, when the biceps is already tired and the weight matters less than the tension.
