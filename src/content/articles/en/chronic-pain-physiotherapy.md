> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a physiotherapist or a sports doctor. If the pain is intense, appears suddenly or comes with a loss of strength, get advice before you start.

Living with constant pain drains you physically and mentally and limits your day in ways that people who do not have it rarely understand. It is easy to blame age, bad posture or irreparable wear and resign yourself to suffering in silence.

Getting quality of life back starts with understanding what is happening in your body. Chronic pain is defined as pain that persists beyond three to six months, when the damaged tissue should already have healed.

## Why chronic pain is not a life sentence

A myth condemns many people to a sedentary life: the belief that pain is a sentence for life. The idea that after a certain age, or after a certain injury, the body breaks and there is no way back does not hold up.

Chronic pain rarely means something is torn or broken right now. Current evidence points to an alteration in how the nervous system processes signals. The body has become overprotective.

The central nervous system works like a car alarm. When there is an injury, it goes off to protect you. In chronic pain, that alarm has become so sensitive that it goes off because the wind blows. Central sensitisation is an alarm that stays too sensitive long after the damage.

The human body is highly adaptable, and it needs the right stimuli to unlearn that pain response. Assuming that suffering is your lot is the first obstacle.

## Manual therapy: a window, not a cure

Manual therapy has its place. A good professional uses their hands to improve the mobility of a stiff joint, reduce acute muscle tension and, above all, open a pain-free window.

The problem arrives when you believe massage will cure you passively. Manual therapy is the key that opens the door, and you have to walk through it by moving. Loosening a tissue without teaching it to bear load changes nothing.

## Therapeutic exercise: the core of the treatment

Recovery happens here. Movement is the most powerful and cheapest analgesic there is. With specific, prescribed exercises you tell your brain: look, I can make this movement and nothing bad happens.

Therapeutic exercise strengthens muscles, improves the health of tendons and cartilage and raises your confidence. It does not mean heavy squats on day one. It means finding the exact point of effort your body tolerates today and raising it progressively.

> **Note:** Current evidence points to isometric analgesia as a good starting point. Holding muscle contractions without joint movement, at medium or high intensity, reduces cortical inhibition and produces local analgesia. That lets you raise tissue tolerance before moving on to dynamic exercises.

Exercise also regulates rest, and rest regulates pain. If you sleep badly, recovery and the perception of the ache get worse; the guide on [how much sleep you need to progress](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) covers the hours and the routine.

## Stress and sleep turn the pain up

Stress is a physiological response that releases cortisol and adrenaline, substances that, kept high over time, sensitise the nerves. If you are going through a spell of anxiety at work or personal problems, it is common for your back or knee to hurt more.

Your mental state acts as a volume dial for pain. A complete treatment takes it into account: resting well and managing stress matter as much as doing the rehab exercises.

[Muscle recovery](/en/muscle-recovery/) and a well-planned [deload week](/en/deload-week/) help when the problem overlaps with training. Dropping volume for a few days does not delay progress and often unlocks it.

## Understanding pain also reduces it

One of the most powerful tools is understanding why it hurts. Knowing that pain does not always equal tissue damage removes the fear of moving, and losing that fear is the first step.

Central sensitisation produces two phenomena worth knowing. Allodynia is pain from stimuli that should not hurt, such as the brush of clothing. Hyperalgesia is an exaggerated response to a stimulus that does hurt. Neither means the tissue is torn right now.

When you understand that the stab does not mean you are breaking, your brain lowers its guard. Gradual exposure to the movements you avoid retrains that response.

## Two mistakes worth avoiding

### Absolute rest

The idea that, if your back hurts, you should lie on the sofa for weeks is a relic. Except in the first 48 hours after an acute trauma, absolute rest weakens muscles, atrophies tissue and stiffens joints.

If running hurts, you can walk fast. If your back hurts when you lift weights, you can swim. [Returning to training after an injury](/en/common-gym-injuries/) happens with modified load, not with immobility.

### Passive patches

Pills, heat patches, cooling ointments, acupuncture without exercise. They are plasters. They relieve you temporarily and make the day more bearable, which is fine, but they do not change the body's capacity to cope with life. If you do not address the mechanical or neurological origin, you end up in the same place once the effect wears off.

## Signs that you are on the right track

Measuring progress only with the question of how much it hurts today from 1 to 10 is misleading, because pain fluctuates. These signs say more:

- You sleep better and wake up with less stiffness.
- You can sit, stand or walk for longer before the ache appears.
- The pain is less intense and, when a flare-up comes, it lasts far less than before.
- You feel more confident and less afraid of movements you used to avoid.

[Consistency](/en/how-to-stay-consistent-working-out/) weighs more than any brilliant session. In rehabilitation, three months of regular work beat two intense weeks followed by giving up.

## Frequently asked questions about physiotherapy and chronic pain

**How long does chronic pain take to go away?** There is no magic date. It depends on how long you have had the pain, your habits and your commitment. Deep changes can take from a couple of months to more than a year.

**Is it normal to hurt during or after rehab exercises?** Yes. An ache of 3 or 4 out of 10 during rehabilitation is within the expected range, because the body is adapting to new loads. What matters is that it does not spike and returns to normal within 24 hours.

**Do I have to stop training if I have chronic pain?** Rarely. The ideal is to modify the training: swap the barbell for dumbbells, reduce the range or lower the weight. Stopping training entirely usually makes the picture worse.

**Do scans show the cause of the pain?** Not always. Many people without pain have hernias, protrusions or wear on their scans. The image shows anatomy, not pain. Treat the patient, not the X-ray.

**Can stress create physical pain out of nothing?** Not out of nothing, but it amplifies minor aches until they become disabling, through systemic inflammation and a hypervigilant nervous system.

**What if the pain appears after a hard session?** [Delayed onset muscle soreness](/en/delayed-onset-muscle-soreness/) follows a different pattern: it appears with new work, especially eccentric, and fades within days. If the pain moves around, lasts weeks or wakes you at night, get it checked.
