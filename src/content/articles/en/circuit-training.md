For maximal strength there are better structures. A circuit changes the shape of the session; it does not replace heavy work.

## When circuit training fits

It fits four situations. The first is a return to training: someone who has been off for months can move through stations with comfortable loads and learn the pattern without piling fatigue into a single set. The second is support for running, cycling or team sports, where strength assists rather than leads. The third is conditioning: the circuit keeps the heart rate up and adds muscle work in the same session. The fourth is fat loss, as long as diet and total expenditure are in place.

The ideal frequency is one or two sessions a week. You can reach four, but then it pays to split the work with another structure, such as a double split.

## What the format costs

Each station gets less load than a straight set, because the muscle arrives tired from the previous one. Fatigue accumulates and progression is harder to read: adding weight at one station changes the rhythm of the rest. And the energy spent after the session is small next to the session itself, so a circuit does not turn a poor diet into a good one.

If strength is the priority, the circuit is not the main format. It can be the light session of the week, not the heavy one.

## The rules for building one

One exercise per large muscle group; the small ones assist. Between 12 and 20 reps per station, with a load that never reaches failure. Two to four rounds and under 60 minutes per session. Rest between stations is the time it takes to change machine, about 20 seconds. Alternate opposing groups: chest with back, biceps with triceps.

To start, choose machines and one group per station. With experience, move to free weights and bodyweight: squats, lunges, dips and pull-ups. That version demands more and recruits more muscle.

## The warm-up

Five minutes on a bike or rower at an easy pace, shoulder and hip mobility, and one light round of the first three stations at half the load for 8 to 10 reps. The warm-up is not another round: it ends when you can run the first station through a full range.

## The load: a margin, not failure

Pick a weight that leaves three or four reps in reserve on the first round, and two on the last. If the third round no longer reaches the minimum of 12 with good technique, drop the load by 10 percent. Failure adds nothing here: it breaks the rhythm and lengthens recovery.

## An eight-station example

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lat pulldown | 3 | 15 |
| Leg extension | 3 | 15 |
| Bench press | 3 | 15 |
| Dumbbell squat | 3 | 15 |
| Back extension | 3 | 15 |
| Crunches | 3 | 15 |
| Lateral raise | 3 | 15 |
| Lying leg curl | 3 | 15 |

> **Note:** Each set is one full round of the eight exercises. Rest 20 seconds when changing station and two minutes between rounds.

## How to progress

First add reps: from 12 to 20 at the station that falls short. When you complete 20 on every set, add 5 percent to the load and go back to 12. Add a fourth round only when the first three finish with two reps in reserve. Every six to eight weeks, cut the volume for a week before carrying on.

Log the load and reps at each station. Without a record, progression becomes a feeling.

## How to fit it into the week

Two days: Tuesday and Friday. Three days: Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Four days: Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Friday, with two different structures. Avoid two circuits on consecutive days.

Cardio for fat loss belongs at another time of day or on another day. Adding 30 or 45 minutes at the end lengthens the session, lowers the quality of the stations and contradicts the 60-minute ceiling.
