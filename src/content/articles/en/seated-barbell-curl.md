Seated on a flat bench with the bar over the thighs and the elbows already bent, the exercise starts where others finish. The range runs from about 90 degrees of flexion to the top, and back. Nothing more.

## The position

Flat bench, back straight and feet on the floor. The bar is gripped supinated at shoulder width and rests on the top of the thighs.

The elbows stay at the sides and the bar starts at thigh height, with the elbow at about 90 degrees. From there it rises to the chest, squeezes for one second and lowers to the same point. The bar does not rest on the legs between reps: if it does, the set is over.

The feet stay flat and slightly apart, and they do not push the floor to help the lift.

If the bench has a backrest, the back can rest on it, but the pad stays upright: tilting it back turns the partial into a swing.

## A partial by design

The range is half of the full curl. The lower part, the one that stretches the biceps, is not trained. The partial is the exercise as designed, and that should be said before programming it.

The advantage is that it allows more weight in the section where the biceps has mechanical advantage. The disadvantage is that it leaves out the long position, where the muscle receives one of its best stimuli. The guide to [range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) develops the idea and explains why a partial does not replace a full curl.

That is why the partial goes last. Before it, the session already covered the full range with a standing curl; after it, the partial adds load in the top half without taking anything from the rest.

It also checks whether the weight of the full curl was honest: if the top half does not move with a similar load, the standing curl was living on momentum.

## Why it does not pick both heads

The shoulder angle decides which biceps head works, not the seat: with the arm behind the body the long head stretches, with the arm in front the short head works. Seated, the shoulder stays neutral and both heads work in a middle position.

The [anatomy of the biceps and forearm](/en/biceps-forearm-anatomy/) shows the path of each head. No seated position changes that share, and grip width does not change it either: the bar moves the hands, not the shoulder.

## How much weight and how many sets

Three sets of 8 to 10 reps with 90 seconds of rest. The dose is a recommendation from this page; the original gives no numbers.

Add weight when all three sets reach 10 reps without the torso drifting back and without the bar bouncing on the thighs. Because the range is short, the urge to overload appears fast.

## The mistakes

- **Leaning back.** The torso looks for momentum and the partial becomes a swing.
- **Resting the bar on the thighs.** If the bar rests, the set stops and the stimulus is lost.
- **Starting with straight elbows.** Without the 90-degree angle, the exercise stops being the partial you want.
- **Using it as the first exercise.** It takes the place of the full curl and the session loses range.
- **Chasing failure with the back.** When technique breaks, the weight has passed the arm limit.

## Where it fits

It is the last biceps exercise of the day, or the second in a short session after a full curl. The [barbell curl](/en/barbell-curl/) sets the load reference, and the [seated dumbbell curl](/en/seated-dumbbell-curl/) is the seated version with a full range.

[Training volume](/en/training-volume/) explains how to count these partial sets, and the [biceps routines](/en/biceps-routines/) place the exercise in the plans that already start with a full curl.
