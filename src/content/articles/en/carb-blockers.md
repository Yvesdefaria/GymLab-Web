## What they promise and what they sell

The idea is simple: a pill that blocks a digestive enzyme, leaves part of the sugar unabsorbed and subtracts calories from the meal. It sells as permission to eat bread without guilt and as an alternative to fat burners.

The body does not work that way. Starch digestion has several gates, and blocking one does not stop the process. Whatever is not absorbed in the small intestine reaches the colon, where it ferments and produces gas.

## Nopal

Nopal is an extract from the stem of a Mexican cactus. It lowers the glucose response in small studies and has been studied as support in diabetes, where the goal is to flatten the sugar spike after a meal.

Moving that effect to a healthy person who wants to lose fat is a hypothesis, not a result. In diabetes nopal replaces no treatment, and in people without diabetes there is no weight-loss data.

## White bean extract

Bean extract is sold as an alpha-amylase inhibitor, the enzyme that breaks starch down. The most cited trial gave 3 g with lunch and 3 g with dinner for eight weeks: 1.7 kg of loss against 750 g on placebo. The difference exists and it is small.

The detail sits in the rest of the protocol. Every participant followed a high-fibre, low-fat diet with at least 100 to 200 g of complex carbohydrate a day. In that context the pill was the passenger: the diet change explains the result. Two earlier trials with the same kind of product found no inhibition of absorption at all.

## Gymnema

Gymnema lowers blood glucose in animals and is sold to curb a sweet tooth. In humans there are no trials supporting that job, and the 400 to 600 mg dose on the labels has no study behind it.

A plant that works in a rat and has not been tested in people is not a treatment; it is a hypothesis with a price tag.

## Chromium

Chromium is a mineral involved in glucose and insulin metabolism. A normal diet covers the need, and double-blind trials with chromium picolinate have found no reduction in body fat.

In someone without a deficiency the supplement adds nothing. And chromium deficiency is rare outside prolonged intravenous feeding.

## The one thing that does change

Layer described in 1986 that these products, rather than blocking, may slow absorption. Slowing is not blocking: most of the sugar still gets in, and the effect on body fat has never been shown.

The calorie arithmetic shows the size of the problem. To subtract 100 calories from a meal, a pill would have to prevent the absorption of about 25 g of carbohydrate. The extracts tested do not reach that figure even under the controlled conditions of a trial, and variability between people does the rest.

## The verdict

A carb blocker costs money and does not change body composition. The whole category lives on a promise it has never kept, and the packaging is renewed every few years with another extract and the same logic.

What does work is duller: fewer total calories, more protein, more fibre and some strength work. The guide to [losing fat and gaining muscle](/en/lose-fat-gain-muscle/) lays out that arithmetic without pills.

If the goal is to gain weight instead of losing it, the problem is the opposite one and the guide to [mass gainers](/en/mass-gainers/) explains why a tub is not the answer either. For performance, [amino acids](/en/amino-acids/) clarify how much protein is actually needed, and [carnitine](/en/carnitine/) is another case of a sale with no result. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) closes the list of what you do not need to buy.
