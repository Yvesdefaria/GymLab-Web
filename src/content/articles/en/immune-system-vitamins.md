The immune system is a network of cells that needs enough raw material and an environment that does not sabotage it. When that material is missing, the response gets worse; when it is in surplus, it does not improve. Pills do not change that logic.

## What a vitamin can and cannot do

A diagnosed deficiency of vitamin D, vitamin C, zinc, iron or protein impairs the function of lymphocytes and of the body's barriers. Correcting it returns the response to its normal level. That is the real scope of supplementation, and also its limit.

Above that level, trials find no extra benefit. Megadoses of vitamin C do not prevent colds in the general population, and high-dose antioxidants can blunt part of the adaptation to training.

## The deficiencies that matter

Vitamin D is the only one that deserves a routine check in winter. Between October and March, at mid latitudes, the skin makes far less of it and the diet contributes little: oily fish, eggs and fortified dairy. A 25-hydroxyvitamin D test shows whether there is a deficit. Without that number, supplementing is guesswork, and high doses without control are not harmless.

Vitamin C comes from food. An orange, a kiwi and a serving of broccoli cover the daily need with room to spare. One-gram supplements add no protection for anyone already eating fruit and vegetables.

Iron and zinc are worth checking when there are symptoms or risk factors. Low ferritin explains fatigue and worse performance, especially in menstruating women and in vegetarian diets. Zinc corrects a shortage, and an excess competes with copper and causes digestive upset.

Protein and calories count too. A diet very low in energy or protein weakens the immune response and slows recovery from infection. Before buying a bottle, it is worth checking whether the plate arrives.

## A multivitamin does not absolve the diet

A multivitamin fills gaps cheaply when eating is irregular. It does not replace fruit, vegetables, oily fish or legumes, and it does not make up for a week of fast food.

Trials of multivitamins in healthy adults give flat results: fewer days of illness in some studies, no difference in others. What does appear, at high doses of beta-carotene or vitamin E, is an increased risk in specific groups. The cheap pill is not insurance.

## Seven to nine hours of sleep

Sleep is the factor that shows up most and costs the least. In an experiment with deliberate rhinovirus exposure, people sleeping less than seven hours had almost three times the odds of catching a cold; below five hours, more than four times.

Sleep loss also reduces the response to some vaccines and raises markers of inflammation. For an adult who trains, the reasonable range is seven to nine hours, with stable timing.

## Moderate exercise, with a dose

Regular physical activity is associated with fewer upper respiratory infections. Walking, strength training two or three times a week and moving daily all fall into that category.

Very long, demanding sessions open a window of several hours in which the immune response drops. That does not turn sport into a problem: the overall pattern remains protective. What weighs is stacking high loads without rest and sleeping little, not one hard session on its own.

## The practical order

Food first, then the test, then the specific supplement. If a deficiency is suspected, measure it. If the result is normal, there is nothing to correct. If it is low, the correction is prescribed and the test is repeated.
