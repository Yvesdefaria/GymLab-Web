The word amino acid appears on every supplement tub and in almost no conversation about food. The map is worth putting in order, because most of what is sold as a free amino acid already arrives with an egg, a yoghurt or a plate of lentils.

## Twenty amino acids, nine you cannot make

The body uses twenty amino acids to build proteins. Nine are not produced fast enough and have to arrive with food: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. The other eleven are made from them or from other substrates.

The figure of eight essential amino acids still circulates. It belongs to children and to some clinical situations; in healthy adults the list has run to nine for decades, and histidine is the one missing from old counts.

Conditionally essential is the third group. Glutamine, arginine, cysteine or glycine are made in the body, but under stress, illness or growth the supply can fall short. In a healthy adult who eats enough, that is rarely a problem.

## Essential, branched-chain and whole protein

Three of the nine essentials have a branched side chain: leucine, isoleucine and valine. They are the BCAAs. The name made them famous and also cut them short, because a BCAA supplement delivers three of the nine pieces muscle needs and none of the other six.

A whey shake, a chicken fillet or a plate of lentils with rice reach the same essential count with more protein per euro. Loose BCAAs have one advantage: they suit someone who already covers protein and wants a small dose around the session.

## Peptide absorption

The gut absorbs amino acids in short chains of two or three, called dipeptides and tripeptides, better than free ones. Peptide transporters work faster than the transporters for single amino acids, which is why hydrolysates arrive sooner.

That describes entry speed, not the muscle result. Once in the blood, muscle uses whatever amino acids are there, from a peptide or from a protein already digested. The peptide advantage is minutes in a specific situation, not kilos of muscle a year.

## How much protein you need

The reference for strength training is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight a day. At 80 kilos that is 128 to 176 grams, spread over three or four meals of 25 to 40 grams. Eggs, milk, yoghurt, fish, meat, legumes and cereals cover the figure with no tub.

Going above 2.2 adds no muscle in most cases and complicates the diet. Dropping below 1.6 leaves synthesis on the table, and that is the expensive mistake.

## Promises that do not hold

Arginine is sold as a growth hormone booster. The studies that show a rise use intravenous doses, not oral ones, and the peak lasts less than an hour. A growth hormone spike does not build muscle on its own.

Tryptophan and tyrosine appear as natural antidepressants. Trials in people with depression do not support that use, and in sports nutrition there is no training effect that justifies the price.

## When they make sense

Free amino acids help when total protein falls short: poor appetite, very restrictive diets, digestive problems or a recovery phase when eating is hard. There, a small essential blend is more useful than BCAAs alone, because it includes all nine pieces.

In every other case, whole protein does the job and costs less. A tub of free amino acids usually costs more per gram of nitrogen than a bag of whey or a kilo of chicken.

## How to read the label

Blends are sold by ratios, such as 2:1:1 of leucine, isoleucine and valine, or by a percentage of essentials. Those figures describe the formula, not the quality. What matters is how many grams of essentials a serving holds and what they cost.

If the label does not state grams per amino acid, there is no way to compare. Whey lists its full profile, costs less and brings the rest of the protein as well.
