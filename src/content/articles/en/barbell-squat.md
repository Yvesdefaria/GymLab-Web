Few exercises have as many versions as the squat and few are performed with as little agreement on the basics. This page orders the standard movement, the one that serves both strength and size, and leaves the differences between variants to other guides.

## The stance

The feet go at shoulder width or slightly wider, toes turned out between ten and thirty degrees. The right stance is the one that lets the lifter descend with the knee travelling forward over the line of the foot and stand up without it caving in.

A very narrow stance demands more control of ankle and knee. A very wide one shortens the range and adds hip and adductor work. Neither changes which quadriceps head grows: the four share one tendon and are recruited together, as [quadriceps anatomy](/en/quadriceps-anatomy/) sets out.

The knee travels past the toes on the way down. That path is normal and necessary; what must not happen is the knee collapsing inward or the heel lifting.

## Bar height

The bar rests on the back, never on the neck. Two placements cover almost everything: high, on the traps, and low, below the spine of the scapula and resting on the rear delts.

The high bar keeps the torso more upright and shifts more of the work to the quadriceps. The low bar leans the torso a little more, shortens the range and usually takes more weight, with more demand on the hip and the lower back.

The choice depends mostly on proportions. This table is a starting point, not a rule:

The test is simple: if the torso pitches forward on the way down with a high bar, a lower bar usually fixes it. If the back rounds with a low bar, move it up.

## Bracing: one single rule

Before descending, breathe in through the nose, fill the abdomen and hold the pressure until you are past the hardest point of the way up. Exhale at the end, already standing. On light sets breathing can be freer, but on heavy sets you brace and do not let go.

That is the only breathing instruction on this page. Inhaling on the way down and exhaling on the way up sounds tidy and works badly: the air that leaves halfway up is exactly the air holding the spine. The [squat safety guide](/en/squat-safety/) develops the rest of the safety framework.

## Depth: thighs parallel

The reliable reference is the thigh parallel to the floor. At that point the hip crease sits level with the knee and the quadriceps range is complete for everyday use.

The second reference is inclination: the torso and the shin end up at a similar angle. If the torso approaches horizontal while the shin stays almost vertical, the bar has drifted forward or ankle range is missing.

Going below parallel is possible and sometimes useful, as long as the back stays neutral and the heel stays down. It is not compulsory, and under heavy loads it asks for more control. A ninety-degree knee angle is not a reference: it measures the joint, not the position of the thigh.

## Gaze and neck

The eyes go forward or slightly down, with the neck in line with the spine. Looking at the ceiling throws off balance and looking at the floor rounds the upper back. The [squat head position guide](/en/squat-head-position/) covers the detail.

## Common errors

The first is cutting the descent as soon as the weight climbs. A half squat with a lot of weight does not train the full range and often ends with an aching knee.

The second is letting the hips rise before the chest. The torso leans, the bar drifts forward and the lower back pays for the difference.

The third is lifting the heels. It is almost always the ankle: a few centimetres of heel lift or a slightly wider stance solves it while mobility improves.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose depends on the goal. For strength, three to five sets of five to eight reps with two or three minutes of rest. For size, three sets of eight to twelve reps with ninety to a hundred and twenty seconds.

In both cases progression rules: if the weight has not moved for several weeks, the problem is in the plan and not in the exercise. ## Where it fits in the plan

The squat opens the leg session. It comes after the warm-up and before the leg press, extensions and accessories, because it demands the most and benefits most from arriving fresh.

It is not compulsory. Anyone who cannot put a bar on their back has alternatives: the [dumbbell sumo squat](/en/dumbbell-sumo-squat/) keeps the pattern with less axial load, and the [power clean](/en/power-clean/) trains hip and knee extension at speed. The [clean](/en/clean/) uses this same squat as its catch.
