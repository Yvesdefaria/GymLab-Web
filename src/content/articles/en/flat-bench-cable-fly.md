## How to set it up

Place a flat bench between two low pulleys, the same distance from each. Set the pulleys at bench height or a little below: if they sit higher, the cable pulls from above and the exercise turns into a standing crossover.

Put the bench slightly in front of the line between the pulleys so the cables stay loaded even when the hands meet over the chest.

Clip a handle to each pulley and lie down with the head, shoulders and hips in contact with the bench. Bend the knees and plant the feet. Take the handles with the palms facing up and reach out over the chest with a small bend in the elbows, 10 to 15 degrees.

That bend stays fixed for the whole set. The elbow neither opens nor closes to press: it transmits what the shoulder does.

## The line of pull

The flat bench cable fly is the horizontal version of the incline cable fly. The setup is the same, with a flat bench instead of a 30-degree bench, and the work spreads across both heads of the chest instead of loading the clavicular one.

With a dumbbell on a flat bench, resistance pulls towards the floor through the whole range. The top of the movement loses tension, which is exactly where the chest contracts fully. With two low pulleys, the vector changes along the arc and tension holds at the finish.

That is the real difference and the reason to choose the cable. [Chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/) explains why both heads work together on every rep, and the general case for cables is in the [cable exercise benefits](/en/cable-exercise-benefits/) page.

## Execution: the arc, the fixed elbow and the finish

From the extended position, open the arms out to the sides along a wide arc until the chest stretches. The elbows sit at shoulder height or slightly below. On a flat bench, an elbow far below the bench line forces the shoulder and adds no useful range.

On the way back, trace the same arc and bring the hands together in front of the chest, at sternum height. Do not cross the wrists or pass one hand in front of the other: crossing adds internal rotation at the shoulder and does nothing for the contraction.

Squeeze for a second with the hands together. Return slowly, keeping the cable under control, to the open position. The elbows do not change angle at any point of the range.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **High pulleys.** The cable pulls from above and the exercise becomes a badly set standing crossover.
- **Dropping the elbows below the bench.** The shoulder loses its position and the chest stops being the muscle that works hardest.
- **Crossing the hands.** It adds rotation at the shoulder and shortens the useful range.
- **Turning it into a press.** If the elbow extends on the way in, the triceps join and the chest loses the arc.
- **Letting the stack drop on the way back.** The eccentric is half the exercise.
- **Too much weight.** With too much load the arc shortens and the movement becomes a shove.

## Dose and progression

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. This is an isolation movement: pick a load that lets every rep follow the same arc and the same pause.

The normal progression is load, in small jumps on each pulley, once 15 reps come out clean. Then tempo: three seconds back and one second of contraction.

## Where it belongs in the session

After the presses, once the chest is worked and heavy load is no longer the point. The cable finishes the job with constant tension and does not compete with the press for weight.

It also works as a first station in a cable session, or as a partner to the [incline cable fly](/en/incline-cable-fly/) to cover both regions of the chest with the same kind of resistance.

The [machine fly](/en/machine-chest-fly/) does the same work with a guided path, and the [flat dumbbell fly](/en/flat-dumbbell-fly/) swaps the cable for free weight. The [high cable crossover](/en/high-cable-crossover/) is the standing version and the [low cable crossover](/en/low-cable-crossover/) is the one that rises from the waist.
