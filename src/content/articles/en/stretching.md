**Stretching** is essential in daily life to improve suppleness, tone and mobility. It is also gentle gymnastics that invites you to listen to the inside of your body instead of looking only at its appearance: the neck frees up, the belly flattens, the chest opens and the legs become agile.

The natural gesture of stretching when you wake up maintains physical shape and brings wellbeing. It can also improve athletic performance, as long as you choose the moment and the dose.

## Benefits of stretching

Stretching is a natural gesture that maintains physical shape. Instinctively, the first thing you do when you wake up is stretch. Beyond that revitalising effect, it serves several functions in training.

### Maintaining or increasing range of motion

A repetitive sporting movement can reduce range of motion by tightening muscles and tendons. Some tension is essential, especially in strength sports, but excessive tension with a reduced range only brings injuries and lower performance. Regular stretching prevents that problem.

In disciplines such as swimming or gymnastics, flexibility work is done regularly to increase the range of a joint when that range means more performance.

### Increased muscle tone

Stretching reinforces the muscles. By using the muscle's passive resistance, stretching speeds up the synthesis of the proteins that make up muscle fibres. The body gains tone and strength endurance.

### Warm-up before effort

Stretching warms the muscles, tendons and joints, and prepares the body for physical effort.

### Anti-stress effect

Thanks to their euphoric and oxygenating effect, stretches minimise the stress that can paralyse muscles, for example before a competition.

### Injury prevention

Most muscular efforts compress the joints and squeeze the spine. Stretching decompresses the back and the joints, and speeds up joint, tendon and muscle recovery.

## Types of stretching

There are different techniques, but three main methods have proven effective.

### Postural stretching

This is the most practised method. It aims to keep the body in good physical shape and suits beginners and sedentary people best.

It relies on movements with muscle contractions and stretches, built progressively and slowly. That lets you get to know the deep, postural musculature and work the whole body while becoming aware of its flexibility.

Lengthening comes from positions of flexion, extension and torsion. Stretches must be done slowly so you do not stimulate the antagonist muscles. Once you reach a comfortable position, hold it for 15 to 20 seconds so the fibres relax, lengthen and take in oxygen.

### Dynamic stretching

This is the most recommended method in sports training programmes. It adds dynamism and power because it acts on the elasticity of muscles and tendons.

It relies on rebound-style movements performed with some speed. The technique consists of throwing your legs or arms in a given direction in a controlled way, with no spring or swing. The agonist muscle contracts quickly and lengthens the antagonist, which is suddenly stretched.

### PNF stretching

PNF stands for proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation. It is used in rehabilitation therapy and is divided into four phases:

- Stretch a muscle slowly and gradually to its maximum.
- Perform an isometric contraction for 15 or 20 seconds, always in the position of maximum lengthening.
- Relax the muscle for about 5 seconds.
- Stretch the muscle you just contracted for about 30 seconds.

## How to breathe during stretching

Knowing how you breathe and at what level of the body you do it improves the effectiveness of every movement. Do not hold your breath during a stretch: you would stop the muscle from taking in oxygen and encourage tension.

During a stretching exercise, breathing increases progressively and then stabilises. Pulmonary ventilation is controlled by the respiratory centres in the brain, and it rises to cover the energy needs of the muscle cells.

There are two types of breathing: **active**, inhaling through the nose, and **passive**, exhaling through the mouth.

### Exhale and inhale at the right moment

Holding your breath during an effort makes the musculature rigid. In stretching you need to relax the body. Inhale calmly and deeply so the muscle loses as much of its resistance capacity as possible, and synchronise your breathing with each stretch, **exhaling in the most intense phase**.

## Five key moments to stretch

For an athlete, flexibility is not an end in itself. Being very flexible is impressive, but beyond a certain point, too much flexibility harms performance. You need to find the balance between flexibility and stiffness.

The masters of Soviet weightlifting defined it well: a muscle should be flexible enough to have slightly more range than the discipline requires, to prevent injury and not obstruct the movement, but never so much that it hinders performance. A stretch can raise performance and it can lower it, so pay attention to how you use it.

### Before training

Avoid stretching before your muscles are warm, because it can cause tears and muscle strains. Many athletes do a light cardiorespiratory warm-up before stretching, and there is nothing wrong with that.

With lifting, however, numerous studies have shown that stretching before training lowers strength in the work that follows. If your main goal is strength and power, do not stretch before lifting. In Olympic lifters and throwers, sprinters and other strength and power disciplines, those exercises have a negative effect on performance.

### During the warm-up

If you pull an elastic band for a few seconds, it starts to warm up. Stretching warms muscles and tendons for the same reason. If you pull the band too far, it stretches out and loses all its strength; worse, it can tear. The same happens with muscle.

Warm-up stretches should always be gentle. Medical research links warm-ups with prolonged stretching to poor performance from a loss of muscle elasticity: the muscle loses reactivity and becomes less explosive. That loss lasts only a few hours, but it is enough to ruin a competition or a good session. Do not force stretches while warming up.

### During the effort

When the activity is broken up, as in tennis, football, rugby or bodybuilding, you can stretch during the pauses. Stretching can have two consequences: in the best case it restores muscle tone and favours recovery, with a performance improvement; in the worst, it speeds up the drop in muscle tone and brings fatigue forward.

Those responses depend largely on how much fatigue has built up. Stretching can help in the first part of a session and hurt at the end, or the other way round. Its effects, good or bad, show up quickly, so you can drop the practice as soon as you notice it does not suit you. Do not be dogmatic about stretching during effort.

### After training

This is the best moment. The effort is over, so stretching will not harm performance. Ideally you stretch the muscles you have just worked, because they are warm and need to recover.

Do not lose sight of the golden rule: too much flexibility hinders performance in the long run. A good range of motion is enough to keep injuries away.

### Between sessions

Stretching speeds up recovery between two sessions and reinforces muscle regeneration. It is not tiring, it can be done at home and it needs no equipment.

Do not overdo it either. Past a certain point, long stretching sets tire the muscle instead of regenerating it. Two to four sets of static stretches of 15 or 20 seconds per muscle group are a good base.

The second problem is that you always start with cold muscles, and that can be dangerous. Warm up a little before stretching and increase the intensity gradually.

## How to get more from stretching in the gym

Use the time between sets. It is dead time, and during exercise your muscles are more flexible and respond better.

The ideal moment for a full routine is right after you finish the session. It increases flexibility, helps resynthesise the lactic acid that has built up and clears waste from muscle tissue, which is why it eases muscle soreness.

In real life, weightlifters do not stretch after training: they grab their bag and leave. So set aside some stretching at home in the evening. It will not be as beneficial as stretching right after training, but it is always better than nothing.
