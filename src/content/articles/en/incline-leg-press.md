The 45 degree press is the version that takes the most weight in the family. The angled sled lets you load plates on the sides and push with both legs while the back rests on a pad. That comfort comes with one condition: the pelvis stays where it is.

## The 45 degree press

The sled runs up and down two angled rails and the plates load on the weight horns at the sides. The body lies on a pad that is also angled, so the hip flexes and the push is shared between knee and hip. It is the press that takes the most load, and also the one that asks the most of pelvic control.

## How to set up the body

The feet go in the middle of the platform, shoulder width or a little wider, toes slightly out. The lower back and the pelvis stay in contact with the pad, the head is neutral and the hands hold the handles. The knees line up with the feet and do not collapse inward.

The movement starts by bending ankle, knee and hip together. The sled moves and the body stays put. Push through the whole foot and stop the extension before the knee locks.

## The three decisions

Foot height is the lever that changes the share most: with the feet high, the hip flexes more and the glutes and hamstrings come in stronger; with the feet low, the hip range shortens and the quadriceps takes more. The archive tied the glute emphasis to bending the knees further; the lever that explains it is foot height.

Range is not measured in centimetres. It is measured by the pelvis: go down as far as the lower back stays on the pad.

## The pelvis peeling off the pad

The error that repeats most on the press sits in the pelvis. When the sled goes lower than the hip allows, the pelvis rolls forward, the lower back peels off the pad and the load moves to the area the pad was meant to protect. That is the mechanism that turns a press into a back problem, and it appears on both presses.

The signal is easy to feel with a hand: if the lower back separates from the pad at the bottom, that is your limit. There is no prize for going deeper.

## Common errors

The first is bouncing out of the bottom. The rebound sends the weight back to the knee just when the lever is most demanding.

The second is locking the knees with a jolt at the top. Extend under control and stop before the lock.

The third is setting the feet too low and letting the heels lift. The whole foot pushes; if the heel comes up, the share changes and the knee takes more.

The fourth is half a rep with a lot of weight. The short range does not train the same movement and usually ends with an unhappy knee.

## Sets, reps and rest

The dose is three or four sets of eight to twelve reps, with ninety to one hundred and twenty seconds of rest. The archive gives no numbers; this is the guide's recommendation for a machine that takes heavy loads.

## The incline and the horizontal are not the same machine

Both are leg presses, and that is where the resemblance ends. The incline has an angled sled, takes more weight and shares the work between knee and hip. The [horizontal leg press](/en/horizontal-leg-press/) moves the sled in a straight line, keeps the torso more upright and takes gravity off the spine, which makes it easier to start with and easier on the back.
