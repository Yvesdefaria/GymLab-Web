## The step almost nobody does

The Cuban press ends with the dumbbells overhead, and that finish is why it gets performed badly. The step that sets the exercise apart comes first: elbows bent at 90 degrees, upper arms at shoulder height and a slow turn that carries the hands upward. Without that step, what remains is a more awkward dumbbell press.

The external rotation takes light weight and its own tempo. It is the phase the shoulder notices, and the one most lifters cut short to reach the press.

The version seen in most gyms raises the dumbbells out to the sides and presses from there. The hands never pass through the turn and the cuff never gets to work. The Cuban press is done from the rotation or it is not done.

## The 90/90 position

Stand with the feet at hip width and the ribs down. The dumbbells come out to the sides with the elbows bent at 90 degrees and the upper arms at shoulder height. The palms face back or down, depending on how you hold the dumbbell.

From there, turn the forearms upward without moving the elbows. The hands rise, the elbows stay put and the humerus rotates in the shoulder. That turn is the external rotation, and it is its own movement: it is not the start of the press.

The elbow angle does not change during the turn. If the elbow opens, the forearm lengthens, the lever shortens and the press starts early. The elbow at 90 degrees is the reference that separates the two phases.

## The rotator cuff

The external rotation is driven by the muscles that steady the humeral head from behind: the infraspinatus and the teres minor, two of the four rotator cuff muscles. The supraspinatus assists the lift and the subscapularis does the opposite, internal rotation.

"The inner musculature of the shoulder" describes nobody. The cuff is a group with a name, and the [rotator cuff guide](/en/rotator-cuff-exercises/) covers its work in detail. This exercise loads it with light weight and a controlled path; rehabilitation is separate work.

## The press and the return

Once the hands are up, press the dumbbells overhead without losing the elbow position. The press comes out of the rotation, not the other way around.

The return follows the same order: lower the dumbbells to the 90/90 position and from there turn the forearms back to the start. If you lower straight to the sides, you skip the phase that gives the exercise its point.

## Tempo

The tempo can be counted. Two seconds to turn, one to press, two to return to the 90/90 position and two to undo the rotation. If the turn speeds up at the end, the weight is too heavy.

A one-second pause at the top of the turn, with the hands still, separates the phases and stops the press from stealing the momentum. The pause also shows whether the rotation went all the way: if the hands do not come fully up, the turn stopped halfway.

## Dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 45 or 60 seconds of rest and light dumbbells. The set ends when the rotation stops being slow or the elbow starts to drop, not when the shoulder gives out.

The weight is chosen by the hardest phase. If the external rotation needs momentum, the dumbbell is too heavy even when the press feels easy.

## Where it fits

It goes at the end of a shoulder or chest session, once the shoulder has work behind it and the weight does not matter. The [Arnold press](/en/arnold-press/) shares the turn but uses it inside the press; the [seated dumbbell shoulder press](/en/seated-dumbbell-shoulder-press/) covers the heavy work.
