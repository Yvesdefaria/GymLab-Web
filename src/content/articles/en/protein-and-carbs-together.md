Few nutrition rules have lasted as long as the one about never mixing protein and carbohydrate on the same plate. It repeats in consultations, on social media and in diets that promise weight loss by separating foods. The physiology of digestion tells a different story.

## Where the rule about separating them comes from

Dissociated diets assume the body should not receive protein and carbohydrate at the same time. The popular explanation has two parts: that gastric juices neutralise each other, and that carbohydrate ferments into alcohol in the stomach.

Neither survives physiology.

## What happens in the stomach

Digestion starts in the mouth. Salivary amylase breaks down part of the starch before swallowing, and chewing leaves the bolus in small pieces. Food can take up to an hour to mix with stomach acid, plenty of time for that enzyme to work.

The stomach is acidic, and pepsin, the enzyme that starts breaking protein down, needs that acid to work. An alkaline medium switches it off. The claim that pepsin needs a basic environment reverses the actual chemistry.

When the contents move into the duodenum, the pancreas and the intestine release bicarbonate and enzymes. Protein and carbohydrate sit there together and are digested at the same time.

## The alcohol that does not exist

A carbohydrate does not ferment into alcohol inside the stomach. The fermentation that produces alcohol belongs to yeast, and the human stomach is not a fermentation tank. Gas after a meal of legumes comes from bacterial fermentation in the colon, much further down and with no alcohol involved.

## The 80 and 85% study

An old trial gave 30 grams of casein to sedentary men and measured nitrogen retention at 80%. Adding 100 grams of sugar raised the figure to 85%. It gets cited as proof that sugar improves protein use.

It was a small trial, at rest and with a single meal. Nitrogen retention is not hypertrophy, and five points in a small group do not become a law. Current studies do not find that adding sugar to protein builds more muscle.

## White meat and carbohydrates

No rule forbids carbohydrate with red meat and allows it with chicken. That distinction has no physiological basis. What changes from one meal to another is the amount of fat, fibre and calories.

A meal with meat, rice and vegetables can be perfectly reasonable. Another with the same meat, fried potatoes and bread can overshoot on calories. The difference sits in the whole plate, not in the nutrient pair.

## What actually moves the needle

Three things decide the result: total calories, protein spread across the day and fibre. With 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilo, three or four servings of 25 to 40 grams and 25 to 35 grams of fibre, food combinations move to the background.

Fibre also changes how carbohydrate is absorbed: it slows gastric emptying and flattens the glucose rise. That is why a meal with legumes satisfies more than the same amount of bread.

## Building a plate

Half vegetables, a quarter protein and a quarter carbohydrate is a base that works for almost everything. Protein can be meat, fish, eggs or legumes; carbohydrate can be rice, potato, pasta or bread; fat can be olive oil, nuts or avocado.

Meal order matters little. Starting with vegetables may help satiety, but the effect is small next to the daily total.

The mix does not change protein quality either.
