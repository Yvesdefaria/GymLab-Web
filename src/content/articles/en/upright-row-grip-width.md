You see upright rows with a wide grip and with a narrow grip in every gym. The difference looks like a few centimetres and changes two things at once: the muscles that get the stimulus and the room left for the supraspinatus tendon.

## Which muscles it recruits

The upright row works the middle deltoid as the main muscle, with help from the front deltoid and the traps. The upper back also takes part, stabilising the bar and following the elevation of the shoulders.

With a narrow grip, the middle deltoid loses prominence, the front deltoid and the traps gain, and the range shortens. With a wide grip, the middle deltoid takes more load and the movement keeps its useful range.

## Wide or narrow grip: what changes

The first difference is control. With the hands close together the bar shakes more and it takes energy to keep it still. With the hands apart the bar stabilises and the effort goes to the muscle you are after.

The second is range. A narrow grip limits the movement interval, so the bar travels less distance for the same work.

The third is wrist comfort. A grip that is too narrow forces the wrist to twist inward to keep the bar close to the body.

## The subacromial space

Between the head of the humerus and the acromion there is a narrow space through which the supraspinatus tendon and the subacromial bursa pass. Anything that narrows that space compresses those structures as the arm rises.

A narrow grip narrows the space and can pinch the tendon against the acromion. The wear does not come from one rep but from the sum of reps over weeks. The risk grows if the shoulder is already irritated.

The wide grip leaves more room between the joint and the head of the humerus, so the structures do not bunch up as the arm lifts. That is the mechanical reason to open the hands, not an aesthetic preference.

## The practical rule

The minimum grip is shoulder width, and a little more does no harm. A narrow grip is not a form error in itself; it is the higher-risk option for the shoulder. From there, execution decides:

- The bar rises close to the body, almost brushing the torso.
- The elbows lead the movement and do not pass shoulder height.
- The knees stay slightly bent, never locked.
- The weight allows a full range without swinging.

The exercise stays valid; pulling it high is what hurts. If the elbow goes above the shoulder, the deltoid no longer needs more range and the supraspinatus starts paying for the rest.

The guide to the [most injurious gym exercises](/en/most-injurious-gym-exercises/) places the upright row among the movements to limit, and the correction is exactly this one: wide grip, bar close, elbow below shoulder line.

## When to swap it for another exercise

If the shoulder already hurts when you raise the arm, the upright row is not the exercise to keep pushing. The shrug is the most direct substitute when the goal is the trapezius: same elevation gesture, without taking the arm to the position that bothers it. The technique is in [trapezius training](/en/trapezius-training/).

If the goal is the middle deltoid and the shoulder is sensitive, lateral raises work the same head without raising the elbow above the shoulder.

## How to fit it into the routine

The upright row works as an isolation exercise for the middle deltoid, not as a strength basic. It fits after the presses and before lateral raises, with a weight that lets you complete the range without swinging.

It is not an exercise for chasing a maximum. The bar moves under control, the lift takes as long as it takes and the descent is accompanied. If the last rep forces you to jump or lean the torso back, the weight is too much.

On a Smith machine the path is already guided, but the grip is still your call: open the hands to shoulder width and place the feet slightly forward so the bar does not hit the knees.

On a straight bar, the knurling marks and the length of your arms limit how far you can open the hands. If the bar starts to feel unstable, you have gone wider than your shoulders can control.

> **Notice:** this page is training information, not a diagnosis. If the shoulder hurts when you raise the arm, loses strength or aches at night, see a professional before continuing with the exercise.
