Choosing between the squat and the leg press is like choosing between a car and a van: both carry load, and they serve different purposes. The difference lies in what each one charges and what it gives back.

## What each exercise loads

The squat is a whole-body movement. The bar sits on the back, the erectors and abs hold the trunk, and the hips travel through a long arc while the knees bend. That hip range is what puts the glutes and hamstrings to work alongside the quadriceps.

The leg press does the reverse: the seat supports the back and the movement comes from the knee. The hips barely move, the stabilisers hardly join in and the quadriceps take almost all the work. In exchange, you can get close to failure with less general fatigue.

## What each one costs

The squat charges in systemic fatigue. After four heavy sets, the rest of the session runs uphill: the nervous system is spent and technique suffers. The leg press charges in another currency: by fixing the path, it stops training the balance and coordination the squat demands.

That split explains why the leg press supports your back without taking the range out of your hands. The backrest takes load off the spine, and you still decide the range. Excess depth exists here too: go lower than the hip allows and the pelvis peels off the seat, at which point the backrest stops doing its job. Safety comes from load and control, exactly as it does under the bar.

## The decision rule

If the goal of the session is to pile up quadriceps work near failure, the leg press is the tool: less general fatigue and more useful reps per set.

If the goal is strength, control and transfer to a whole-body movement, the squat leads. It also leads if you want to train the posterior chain and the stabilisers in the same movement.

Most lifters want both, and the plan can deliver both. The mistake is choosing one and expecting it to cover what the other does.

## The order inside the session

In the same session, squat first and leg press after. The squat demands coordination and fine technique, and both fade with fatigue. Start on the press and you reach the bar with tired legs and a worsening pattern.

The squat also sets the reference for the day. When you move to the press, you already know how the legs are responding and can adjust the load with judgement.

## How to split them across the week

One day of squats and one day of leg press also works, especially if you train legs twice a week and want to give each pattern its own session. The advantage is freshness; the cost is that each exercise appears once.

If you take that route, watch the total. Two sessions with the same number of sets as one double session double the weekly work. The guide on [training volume](/en/training-volume/) explains how to square the split without going past what you can recover from.

## Feet on the leg press

Foot position shifts emphasis inside the leg press, although the movement still comes from the knee. Higher on the platform moves part of the work towards the glutes and hamstrings; lower loads the quadriceps more. The guide to [leg press foot position](/en/leg-press-foot-position/) covers the four placements and what changes in each. It is enough to know the adjustment exists and is worth keeping stable between sessions so you can compare.

## Depth and range

In both exercises, the useful range is the one you control. On the press, lowering until the lower back peels off the pad is the signal to stop; in the squat, the signal is losing a neutral spine. [Looking forward](/en/squat-head-position/) helps keep the chest tall on the way down, and the guide on [range of motion](/en/range-of-motion-training/) covers the cases where shortening the range on purpose makes sense.

## The decision in one line

Squat for the whole body and the hips; leg press for the quadriceps and the volume. In the same session, the bar comes first.
