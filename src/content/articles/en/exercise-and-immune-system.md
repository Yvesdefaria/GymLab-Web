Your **immune system** decides, in large part, whether you can string together months of training without interruptions. People who train to be fit, people who compete and people chasing as much muscle as possible have different goals, and all three need the same thing to get there: months of consistent work without injuries or illness breaking the rhythm.

Sports medicine knows that athletic success depends on rigorous training and optimal nutrition, and also on superior immune activity. The statistics repeat across the United States and Europe: thousands of work hours and millions in costs are lost to sick leave, and not only among sedentary people. Athletes go down too.

Infections come from a weak immune system. They are treated as normal and inevitable, yet they need not be, especially in athletes. The severity of an injury and the time it takes to heal also depend on immune activity.

## Exercise and immunity: a double-edged relationship

Hundreds of studies have looked at how exercise affects immunity, and the link is close. Paradoxically, it can be **positive or negative** depending on the intensity and duration of the activity.

Studies measuring the response to light or moderate exercise find mild, temporary changes. Those looking at intense efforts show profound effects. **Monocyte** concentrations in the blood tripled, a sign of a major immune challenge, and the proliferative response of **lymphocytes** was completely suppressed: the defence system was saturated by the trauma of intense exercise.

Even **natural killer cell** activity was suppressed for hours after a hard session. With that first line of defence switched off, the athlete is exposed to infections.

## The limit is your capacity to recover

When training is matched to the athlete's capacity, the immune response goes the other way: it strengthens and increases its activity. Well-trained, healthy athletes have more natural killer cells, higher defensive activity and more monocytes.

Animal and human studies show it: if the programme supplies enough stress to improve performance without reaching exhaustion, the immune system is reinforced. The key is the intensity and duration of the effort relative to the person.

One example. Sedentary young men went from activity close to zero to 40 or 50 minutes of aerobic exercise five days a week. For a trained person that is trivial; for them it was a huge effort. After 15 weeks, their natural killer cell activity had disappeared, leaving them without immune protection.

The finding has been confirmed many times: when exercise is an excessive challenge the body cannot adapt to, defence mechanisms decline until they are wiped out. In elite athletes, exercise within recovery limits strengthens immune activity; when they overreach, as in competition phases or at the end of a long season, activity plummets.

## More muscle, more immune activity

The immune system is complex and affected by many factors: exercise or any form of tension, nutrition and hormones, including growth hormone. Excess cellular oxidation reduces defensive activity, and anything that removes that effect favours it.

There is a direct relationship between muscle mass and immune activity. In patients with HIV, when lean body weight increases, their defences improve markedly. The virus attacks immune cells and leaves the person exposed to any infection; it is not lethal in itself, but through that defensive collapse. Those patients also lose a lot of weight, which weakens the system further. In a clinical setting, treatments that increase lean mass are associated with better immune markers.

> **Note:** this guide is about training and does not propose treatments. The link between muscle mass and immunity is context, not a reason to self-medicate or to replace a healthcare professional.

Bodybuilders tend to be among the athletes with the best self-defence mechanisms. Their type of activity contributes, along with a diet rich in immune-stimulating nutrients.

## Nutrients that support the work

The diet of someone training for strength often supplies vitamins E and C, beta-carotene, coenzyme Q10, the minerals zinc and selenium, and the amino acids glutamine and arginine. None of them replaces a complete diet, but they are part of the picture.

## Watch what you eat

The tube running from your mouth to your anus is not part of the body proper: it is a channel through which nutrients and invading microorganisms pass through the intestinal walls. Everything that enters by mouth has to be chosen carefully, because almost all infections and invasive attacks come in that way. Skin, by contrast, is a barrier that is hard to cross.

## What to do with this information

The training that strengthens your defences is the training that matches your capacity and respects recovery. If you stack very intense sessions without rest, your immune system notices before the mirror does.

Plan your loading and deloading weeks, look after your nutrition and get enough sleep. If infections keep coming back or an injury will not heal, see a healthcare professional.
