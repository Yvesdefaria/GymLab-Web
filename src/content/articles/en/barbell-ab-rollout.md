It is also called the barbell rollout. It needs a smooth floor and two round plates of the same size: the only thing that matters is that the bar rolls without catching.

It is not a starting exercise. It asks for a solid plank, control of the pelvis and some tolerance for working near the limit.

## What the rollout trains

It is an anti-extension exercise. As you roll forward the body becomes a longer lever and gravity pulls the hips towards the floor. The abs and glutes have to brake that fall: they do not move anything, they resist.

That is why the rollout does not resemble a crunch. There is no trunk flexion and no range to shorten; there is a position to hold while the lever arm grows.

## The prerequisite: a solid plank

Before you touch the bar, hold a [plank](/en/isometric-abs/) with the pelvis tucked under, without the lower back sagging, for longer than you think you need. If the hips drop in a plank, they will drop in a rollout.

The test is simple: if your lower back hurts more than your abs at the end of the plank, the rollout can wait.

## Equipment and position

Use a bar with two equal plates. Start with five kilos; when the movement comes out controlled, move to ten. The plates act as a wheel, so their shape matters more than their weight.

Kneel on a mat. Put the bar on the floor and take it with a pronated grip at shoulder width.

Your shoulders sit directly over the bar. That is the starting position: knees fixed, hips high, body compact.

## Execution

With the knees still, roll the bar forward until your arms are straight, without locking the elbows. Pause there and reverse the direction to come back.

The hips do not sag at any point. If they do, the lower back arches and the work moves to the spine.

Control sets the range, not ambition: roll only as far as you can keep the pelvis and come back without help from the hands.

Contract the abs and the glutes for the whole movement. The abs brake the extension and the glutes close the pelvis, and both happen at once.

## Common mistakes

- Letting the hips drop at the end of the range.
- Bending the elbows to shorten the lever.
- Rolling past the point where you can brake.
- Hunting range with the lower back instead of with the shoulders.
- Using small plates that dig into the floor and stop the bar.

## Regressions to start with

If the full rollout will not come, there are three ways to approach it without losing the position.

The first is a shorter range: roll only as far as you can keep the pelvis set, then return. The second is a band anchored to a fixed point in front of you to help brake the way back. The third is a wall: the bar rolls until it touches the wall and the movement stops there, which sets a limit you cannot pass.

## How to progress

Progress comes from range, not from weight. Lengthen it until the arms are straight with the hips in place, then switch to thicker plates, which raise the height of the bar and the difficulty.

The original article sets no sets or reps, and here that makes sense: the position sets the limit. The set ends when the hips start to sink or when the arms bend to hold on. Stop at that moment, even if the clock says there is work left.

## When to skip it

With low back pain, the rollout is not the exercise to start with. The [back pain exercises](/en/back-pain-exercises/) guide covers alternatives with less demand on the lumbar spine.

It also makes no sense without a solid plank, on a floor that is not smooth or with plates that do not roll. In those cases the abs are trained just as well with the flexion family in [ab exercises](/en/ab-exercises/), and the volume is spread with the [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/).
