## What the plate solves

A plate is held with both hands at the sides. That hold forces a neutral grip, with the thumbs up, and splits the weight between the two arms. Setup is quick, the position makes sense on the first rep, and the weight is usually available in any gym.

The restriction is the advantage. A plate does not let one hand compensate more than the other and does not let the wrist turn into an awkward position. The body learns the path with a weight it can control. The flat shape also keeps the plate close to the body: if it drifts away, the wrist notices.

## The position

Stand with the feet at shoulder width and the knees unlocked. Hold the plate at the sides, with the hands at three and nine or ten and two, and bring it in front of the thighs. The arms stay almost straight, with soft elbows and the palms facing each other.

The chest stays tall and the ribs down. Before lifting, brace the abdomen. The plate travels in front of the body, close to the chest, without swinging from side to side.

## The endpoint is the shoulder

The plate rises to shoulder height, with the arms parallel to the floor. That is where the raise ends. Higher than that, the traps and the upper chest take over and the anterior deltoid stops leading. It is the same endpoint the barbell version uses, which keeps the two lifts comparable.

The version that lifts the plate overhead with straight arms belongs to another exercise. An overhead press takes more load and asks for a different elbow position, and the plate is not the implement that solves it best. Mixing the two leaves a movement that is neither a raise nor a press.

The soft elbow stays through the whole path, including the top. If it locks, the arm becomes a rigid lever and the shoulder loses control of the end.

## When it stops being enough

The plate has a low ceiling. Plates jump from 1.25 to 2.5 kilos and then to 5, and the hands holding it tire before the deltoid does. The neutral grip also caps how much the hands can hold at arm's length, so the limit arrives in the forearms as much as in the shoulder. When the last set feels easy and the next plate feels too heavy, the exercise no longer offers a middle step.

That is where dumbbells come in, since they let each side adjust on its own, or the barbell, which takes small increments with plates and splits the load between both hands. The plate still makes sense as a warm-up or a light finisher, not as the main lift for the anterior deltoid.

## Dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 45 or 60 seconds of rest. The load goes up when the last set ends with the arms parallel to the floor and the torso still.

## Mistakes that show up early

- **Swinging the plate.** The weight moves side to side and the shoulder loses the plane of the path.
- **Rising above the shoulder.** The traps take over and the anterior deltoid stops working.
- **Locking the elbows.** The arm blocks and control of the top is lost.
- **Leaning the torso back.** The chest gains height and the plate rises with help from the spine.
- **Gripping the plate too hard.** The hands tire before the shoulder and the set ends for the wrong reason.
