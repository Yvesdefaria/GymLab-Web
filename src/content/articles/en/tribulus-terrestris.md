The sentence shows up in the product's own ads: «it naturally raises blood testosterone levels by up to 40%, which means more muscle mass, more strength and more recovery». It is ad copy, not a lab result. Behind it there is a creeping plant with yellow flowers and a history of animal studies that was never confirmed in people.

## What it is and what it contains

Tribulus terrestris is a plant that grows in dry soil. The extract is made from the aerial parts and standardised for its saponin content, mainly protodioscin. That standardisation matters: two tubs with the same label can carry different amounts of the active compound, and many products add other herbs without saying so in the name.

The mechanism attributed to it is indirect. Saponins would stimulate luteinising hormone production and, through it, testosterone. In healthy people that step has not been demonstrated.

## The trials in trained men

The two most frequently cited papers were done with elite rugby players and with endurance athletes. In the first, five preseason weeks at 450 mg a day of extract changed neither lean mass, strength nor urinary testosterone. In the second, eight weeks at 750 mg a day, alongside other herbs with supposed androgenic action, did not improve endurance adaptation.

A third US study with college athletes reached the same place: no change in body composition or performance.

There are old, small studies that did see testosterone rise. They have not been replicated with comparable doses and methods, and reviews that pool them find no consistent effect.

## From the sheep to the locker room

Much of the enthusiasm comes from studies in rams and pigs. In animals, high doses of extract changed hormonal parameters. Extrapolating that to a man who trains requires assumptions nobody has checked: dose, route, metabolism and the response of the hormonal axis are all different.

When a supplement only works in another species, the reasonable conclusion is that there is no evidence. Missing data are not covered by a physiological explanation.

## The WADA line is misquoted

It is common to read that the World Anti-Doping Agency considers tribulus a dangerous ergogenic aid. WADA does not publish a list of dangerous substances; it publishes a list of substances prohibited in and out of competition. Tribulus is not on that list and is not a steroid precursor.

The real doping risk comes from contamination: an herbal product can carry undeclared steroids. That is a problem with the manufacturer and quality control, not with the species.

## Safety and who should ask first

There are no long-term safety data with standardised extracts. Side effects reported in short trials are mild: digestive upset and headache. Interactions are not well studied.

As a precaution, it is worth asking before taking it with liver or kidney disease, pregnancy or breastfeeding, hormone treatment or long-term medication. Also if the goal is to raise testosterone: that is measured with a blood test, not assumed.

## What to do with the promise

If testosterone is genuinely low, the path is a blood test and a doctor, not a tub. If it sits inside the range, no plant will push it past it. And if what is missing is muscle mass, the order is different: enough protein, overload training and sleep.
