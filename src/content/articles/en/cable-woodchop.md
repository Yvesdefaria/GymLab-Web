It is also called the woodchopper, and the name describes the path: the axe enters above the shoulder and finishes low, next to the opposite leg. The cable reproduces that arc with a resistance you can measure and raise plate by plate.

## Which muscles the woodchop works

The movement loads the internal and external obliques on the side that turns, the transversus abdominis and the spinal erectors, which hold the spine while the trunk changes orientation. This is loaded rotation, the same pattern that shows up when you throw, strike or turn your torso to reach something behind you.

The key is to share the turn out. Rotation comes from the hips and the rib cage; the lumbar spine stays neutral and follows without arching or rounding. When the turn concentrates in the lower back, the abs stop braking and the load moves to the structure with the least margin.

## Starting position

Attach a D-handle to the top of the pulley and stand side-on, about 60 to 90 centimetres from the weight stack.

- Set the feet slightly wider than the hips and point the toes out.
- Bend the knees a little and keep the weight spread over both feet.
- Turn the trunk slowly and take the handle with both hands.
- Keep tension in the back and the abs before you pull. That is the start position, not a step you can skip.

## Step by step

Pull the handle down and diagonally with straight arms while the trunk turns away from the pulley. As the handle comes down, bend the knees a little more: the body follows the arc instead of locking up.

Pause for a moment at the bottom, with the handle near the shin of the far leg, the abs tight and the back flat. Return along the same path, slowly, without letting the stack pull you.

Both phases count. The controlled return is half the exercise and the first thing you lose when the weight goes up.

## The eleven to five line

Picture a clock in front of you: the handle starts at eleven and finishes at five. From the other side, the line runs from one to seven. Hold that diagonal for the whole rep; if the path turns horizontal, the turn is too short and the work moves somewhere else.

## Common errors

- Pulling with the arms as if this were a pulldown. The elbow angle does not change; the trunk is what turns.
- Rounding the lower back to reach further down. The rep ends where the back stays flat.
- Letting the stack drop on the way back.
- Starting too heavy and making up for it with half the arc.
- Turning the head before the chest and losing the shoulder line.

## The low pulley variation

On alternate weeks, set the handle on the low pulley and take the reverse path: from low on one side up to the other. The pattern is the same loaded rotation, with the shoulder as the end point. Alternating the two heights changes the line of pull and spreads the demand across the fibres.

## Sets, load and where it fits

Two or three sets of 8 to 12 reps per side is a reasonable start. Begin with a very light load: in the first sessions the goal is to learn the path, not to move plates. Add weight when the reps come out just as clean in both directions, and measure progress on the stack, not in the speed.
