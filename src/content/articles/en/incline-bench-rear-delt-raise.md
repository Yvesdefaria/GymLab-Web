The exercise shows up in almost every weight room and is almost never done well. The cause is the support. Without the chest against the pad, the body finds a way to help, and the rear deltoid stops being in charge.

## Setup: bench at 30-45 degrees

Raise the backrest to between 30 and 45 degrees. Below 30 the chest is too close to horizontal and the bench gets in the way; above 45 the torso approaches vertical and the rear deltoid loses the plane.

Sit with a dumbbell in each hand and rest the chest on the pad, facing the floor. The knees can go on the bench, if the pad is short, or the feet on the floor with the toes down. Both options work; pick the one that keeps the chest against the pad without pulling on the neck.

The arms hang below the shoulders, with a soft bend in the elbow and the palms facing in. The dumbbells sit under the chest, not in front of the face.

## The path

Open the arms out to the sides in a wide arc to shoulder height. The elbow neither opens nor closes: the bend stays fixed and the movement comes from the shoulder.

The limit is the shoulder line. Above it, the back arches and the trapezius takes the work. Lower with the same control until the dumbbells are back under the chest.

## The scapula follows, it does not push

The chest is supported and the scapula stays quiet against the pad. Squeezing the shoulder blades to pull is the classic mistake on this exercise: the work goes to the rhomboids and the mid traps. Let the scapula follow the arm. The guide to the [rear delts](/en/rear-delt-training/) explains the split.

## What the support changes

In the standing version, the torso is held by the lower back and the hips, and momentum appears as soon as the set gets hard. The bench removes both: there is no hip to push and no back to hold.

The bench hands out no free strength: it removes both sources of momentum, so less weight moves and the usable dose drops. In exchange, every rep reaches the rear deltoid through the same range.

The pad also keeps the chest in a stable position, so the range does not change between reps. On a 15-rep exercise, that consistency is worth more than two extra kilos.

## Mistakes that kill the stimulus

- **Backrest too high.** Above 45 degrees the torso approaches vertical and the rear deltoid loses the plane.
- **Lifting the chest off the pad.** It is the most common way to look for momentum without noticing.
- **Going past the shoulder line.** The back arches and the trapezius joins the movement.
- **Squeezing the shoulder blades.** The work shifts to the middle of the back.
- **Heavy dumbbells.** The arc shortens and the exercise turns into a badly performed row.
- **Stretched neck.** The head follows the line of the torso and the gaze goes to the floor, not straight ahead.

## Dose and where it goes in the session

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 45 to 60 seconds of rest. Start with a weight that allows 15 reps with the chest on the pad; once all of them hold, add a kilo per hand.

It goes at the end of a shoulder session or after the presses. It also works as a second exercise on a day when the plan is volume for the rear part without loading the lower back.

## Against the other versions

The [seated dumbbell rear delt raise](/en/seated-dumbbell-rear-delt-raise/) is the version without an incline bench: the torso leans as far as the thighs allow and gravity changes the end of the range. The [dumbbell rear delt raise](/en/dumbbell-rear-delt-raise/) with the torso bent over asks you to hold the torso for the whole set. The [reverse pec deck](/en/reverse-pec-deck/) replaces the support with a pad that blocks retraction.
