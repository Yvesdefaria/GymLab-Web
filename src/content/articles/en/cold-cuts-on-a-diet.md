The classic question is which cold cut you can eat on a diet. The short answer is any of them, in small amounts and without making it the centre of the plate. The useful answer requires three numbers per 100 grams: protein, calories and sodium. Those three decide whether a portion adds or subtracts.

## What counts as processed meat

Processed meat is meat that has been salted, cured, fermented or smoked to preserve it or give it flavour. That includes cured ham, loin, cured beef, chorizo, salami, mortadella, sausages and bacon. The definition does not distinguish by product quality or price: it distinguishes by process.

Serrano ham is not a sausage in the strict sense, because it does not go inside a casing. For health classification it makes no difference: it is processed meat and shares the same risk category. The same applies to cured beef and cured turkey.

Lean cured products fall in that band because curing removes water and concentrates the nutrients. Chorizo, salami and mortadella sit outside it: they carry less protein per 100 grams and more fat, so they approach 400 kcal. The difference between a slice of loin and a slice of chorizo is in the grams of fat.

A 40-gram portion of cured ham provides about 12 grams of protein and about 100 kcal. That is a modest figure. Covering 30 grams of protein with cold cuts would take 100 grams, and with them would come 1.5 grams of sodium. There is the problem.

## Salt is the real limit

The sodium reference for an adult sits below 2 grams a day, which equals about 5 grams of salt. A hundred grams of cured ham can provide more than 1 gram of sodium, half the limit, without the portion tasting salty. The flavour comes from curing, not from a visibly loaded piece.

That sodium matters in a training plan for two reasons. The first is that it displaces foods with more potassium, such as vegetables, fruit and legumes. The second is that the thirst it produces is sometimes confused with hunger, and the water drunk afterwards does not balance the load. In sensitive people, blood pressure responds to it.

## What the group 1 classification means

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies processed meat in group 1, alongside tobacco and alcohol. That neighbourhood confuses people. The group describes the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk: smoking multiplies lung cancer risk twentyfold, while a daily portion of processed meat is associated with a relative increase of around 18 % in colorectal cancer.

The association comes from observational studies, not clinical trials, and the absolute risk for someone who eats cold cuts twice a week is small. Even so, the direction is consistent across several populations. Reducing frequency is a sensible measure and does not require eliminating the product.

## How to use it as a condiment

The most useful way to include cold cuts is as flavour: a few slices over a salad, a little ham in a legume stew, a small chorizo that aromatises a spoon dish. In those amounts it adds taste and some protein, and does not displace the main source.

The day's protein should come from chicken, fish, eggs, dairy or legumes. The guide to [protein](/en/protein-guide/) explains how much is needed and how to spread it, and the [basic nutrition guide](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-guide/) organises the rest of the plate. Cold cuts, in that scheme, are an extra you count.

## How to read the label

Always look at the information per 100 grams, not per serving: manufacturers choose portion sizes that make the number look smaller. Check the meat percentage, the sodium and the ingredient list. A product with two or three ingredients is usually simpler than one with starches, dextrose and flavourings.

Fat matters less than the total amount. A fattier cold cut satisfies sooner and is eaten in smaller portions, so the calorie difference per meal can be smaller than the label suggests. What does not change is the salt.

## Cold cuts and a calorie deficit

In a deficit, cold cuts have a small slot. Their advantage is convenience: no cooking, they keep in the fridge and they solve a quick meal. Their disadvantage is that calories accumulate without satisfying, because fat and salt do not fill you up like fibre and volume.

A simple strategy is to reserve them for once or twice a week, with a 30-40 gram portion, alongside vegetables or fruit. The rest of the protein comes from fresh sources. The guide to [meat and bodybuilding](/en/meat-and-bodybuilding/) develops that base, and the one on [carb cycling](/en/carb-cycling/) shows how to fit the higher and lower food days.

When cutting, with a narrow calorie margin, every 100 kcal counts. A little ham in the salad justifies them for flavour. A hundred grams of chorizo does not. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) add the rest of the shopping list you do not need to perform in training.
