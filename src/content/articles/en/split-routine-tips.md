Most routine questions start with the split. Without a clear structure, each session becomes a sum of exercises in no particular order. The split routine solves that part and leaves the rest of the plan for later.

## What a split routine is

It is your weekly training system. It defines which muscles you work in each session and how many days you rest between them.

There are thousands of ways to organise it. The choice depends on your goal, your work schedule and how often you can realistically reach the gym. The same person may do well on a four-day split and struggle with a six-day one if the week cannot take it.

## Match your frequency

The first thing you need is your goal. If you want strength or size, train each muscle group once or twice a week. Research has found that muscles need a lot of recovery time to strengthen and develop, and that training a muscle more than twice a week can be counterproductive.

If the goal is muscular endurance, the recommendation changes: three sessions a week for the same group.

[Periodisation](/en/training-periodization/) lets you move that frequency with the phase. You do not have to keep the same split all year.

## Know your intentions

With frequency settled, it is time to distribute the days. The days you want to train, the time per session and the muscle groups you want to pair all come into it.

The most common splits run from full body to twice-a-day training, and from there to two- to five-day divisions. None is better on its own. The one that fits your schedule is the one you will actually follow.

## Fix your weak points

A well-planned split gives priority to the muscles that lag behind. That means training them when you have the most strength and endurance, often with more volume or frequency.

A muscle that is less fatigued always performs better. If your shoulders are the weak point, do not train them after chest, when they are already tired and you cannot give them full attention. Put them first on a separate day, alone or with a group that is not lagging. You can also keep shoulder work after chest and add a dedicated shoulder day.

The arms follow a similar logic. If back and chest stand out over your arms, avoid training triceps on chest day and biceps on back day. Give the arms a full session and make sure they do not arrive exhausted from the previous day's torso work.

The same thinking applies to any group. If your arms trail your torso, working them twice a week while the rest gets one session is reasonable. For back you can use the [back routines](/en/back-routines/) and adjust the volume on the same principle.

## Extend your routine

A split routine does not have to last seven days or give every muscle the same frequency. The week is a convenient convention, not an obligation.

If you want to train each muscle more than once a week, stretch the split to 8 or 10 days and start again. The practical difference is that the routine begins on a different day each week. With a training diary, that is no problem at all.

One example runs ten days: two rest days after training the whole body. The continuous cycle becomes three days on and two off, and you work the full body every five days counting the rest days.

## Add variety

If your schedule allows it, do not repeat the same routine for months. Spend a few weeks on four days, move to five and watch how the body responds.

You can also keep a fixed structure and change the groupings. A four-day routine can last a year if you pair chest with triceps for a while, then chest with back, and so on. The muscular system gets different stimuli and progress does not stall.

[Training volume](/en/training-volume/) is the variable to watch when you move groupings around. That way variety does not become more work than you can recover from.

## Conclusion

These are general recommendations. Nothing bad happens if you train shoulders with chest for a season. When you change routines, move the distribution and try training them alone for a while.

Push-pull routines work well and group shoulders, chest and triceps in the same session. The important thing is to try and adjust. A split routine is a tool, not a fixed rule, and what works for one person may not work for you.

Whatever the split, technique decides how much you get from each set. The guide to [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) covers speed, the eccentric phase and how many sets to assign to each muscle group.
