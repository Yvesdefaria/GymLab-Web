The hip hinge is easier to learn with dumbbells than with a bar. The weight sits at your sides, the body feels balanced and you can drop the load at any moment. That comfort lets you focus on the movement, which is what is actually being trained.

## How to do the dumbbell Romanian deadlift

Feet hip-width apart, dumbbells at your sides, palms towards the thighs. The knees stay slightly bent and do not change during the descent: the hinge comes from the hips, not the knees.

Breathe in, send the pelvis back and let the torso lower with a flat back. The dumbbells travel close to the legs, in front of the midfoot line. The arms stay straight, like hooks, and the shoulders sit over the hands.

The descent ends when the pelvis starts to tuck under and the lower back starts to round. That is where you reverse: push the floor away, drive the hips forward and finish standing, glutes tight and ribs down.

## The stop is the pelvis, not the stretch

The stretch arrives before the end of the range and is not a signal to keep going. The stop is the pelvis beginning to tuck and the lumbar spine beginning to round. Chase more stretch and the back rounds under load.

The practical reference is a side-on video or a set filmed on your phone. Lower to one or two reps before the point where the back loses its line, and that is the range for the day. It changes with fatigue and mobility, so it gets adjusted every session.

## Why dumbbells change the limit

Grip is the first ceiling. Two dumbbells are harder to hold than a bar, especially as the reps climb, and the forearms give out before the hamstrings. Straps come in when grip is the limiter, not to cover a back that is rounding.

The second is the hand path. If the dumbbells drift away from the legs, the weight moves away from the hip and the lever arm on the lower back grows. The fix is simple: dumbbells close, shoulders over the hands and the weight over the midfoot.

The barbell version allows more load and less reliance on grip; it is in the [Romanian deadlift](/en/romanian-deadlift/). The stiff-leg versions, with [dumbbells](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-dumbbells/) or a [barbell](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-barbell/), shift the share between knee and hip and are worth reading before mixing them in.

## Sets, reps and rest

This page's recommendation: three sets of 8 to 12 reps, with 90 to 120 seconds of rest and one or two reps in reserve. The dumbbell grip limits the load before the hamstrings do, so this range is the right call for size and control.

Progression goes through reps before weight: from 8 to 12 inside the same range, then add load. If the grip fails while the reps are still clean, add straps and keep going. The [eccentric hamstring training](/en/eccentric-hamstring-training/) guide covers how to fit the hinge into the week.

## Common mistakes

The first is letting the dumbbells drift forward. The second is bending the knees too much and turning the hinge into a squat.

The third is looking at the ceiling during the descent. The gaze stays forward or slightly down, with the neck in line with the spine. The fourth is bouncing at the bottom to use momentum; the hamstrings handle a brief pause better than a jerk.

## Where it fits

It goes after the squat or as the first movement of a hamstring day, while the hips are fresh. It also works as a second exercise after a conventional deadlift, with two or three sets and less load.
