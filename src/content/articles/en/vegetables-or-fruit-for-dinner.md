The doubt usually comes from the same place: a heavy lunch needs a light dinner. Fruit looks like the quick option and vegetables like the serious one. Both fit, and the practical difference is how much they fill you per calorie.

## Vegetables and fruit share the same bill

Metabolism does not switch off at night. It spends a little less while you sleep, and that drop is small next to the daily total. A food does not make you fatter at nine than at two.

The idea that fruit at night turns into fat mixes two things: the sugar in fruit and a calorie surplus. Fructose is metabolised in the liver like any other sugar and is only stored as fat when the day's balance is positive.

## Fruit at night

A piece of fruit brings water, fibre, vitamins and between 60 and 120 calories. It is a reasonable snack at any hour of the day, including after dinner, and it closes the evening with something sweet without opening the cupboard.

The problem appears when fruit replaces the whole meal and hunger comes back at eleven. An orange is not dinner, and two bananas are not either.

## Vegetables for volume

Vegetables have two advantages at night: they take up space and bring very few calories. Half a kilo of roasted courgette, pepper and onion comes to about 200 calories with a spoonful of oil, and fills a large plate. Fibre and water do the rest for satiety.

They also cook in many ways: oven, steam, griddle, quick sauté or raw in a salad. Variety keeps the habit alive and asks for no complicated recipes. Frozen vegetables solve a Tuesday dinner: they cook faster, keep much of the vitamins and do not rot in the drawer.

## The dinner plate

The plate model works the same as at midday. Half is vegetables, a quarter is protein and a quarter is carbohydrate. Fat comes in the cooking oil or in a handful of nuts.

If dinner is the lightest meal of the day, the plate can stay at vegetables and protein, with carbohydrate in a small portion. If you train at night, carbohydrate helps recovery and there is no reason to remove it. Protein at night works like protein at noon: 30 to 40 grams send the same muscle signal and keep hunger away until morning.

## A real example

A plate of about 650 grams: 200 grams of courgette, 150 of red pepper, 100 of onion and 150 of chicken breast, all roasted with a spoonful of olive oil, plus 60 grams of cooked rice. That is about 550 calories, around 50 grams of protein and a serving of vegetables that is easy to finish.

The same base with chickpeas instead of chicken works for a vegetarian dinner; with salmon, for a fish dinner. The oven at 200 degrees takes 25 minutes and needs no watching.

## Do not fry everything

Fried vegetables absorb oil and lose the calorie advantage. Roasted, steamed or griddled, they keep their volume with little fat. One spoonful of oil per serving is enough for flavour and helps absorb the fat-soluble vitamins.

## The glycaemic index as a tool

The glycaemic index ranks foods by how much they raise glucose, and it is a tool, not a moral test. Vegetables usually sit low, fruit varies and cooking changes the value. Neither is a problem in a balanced dinner.

Counting the index and forgetting calories swaps one number for another. With the plate built properly, dinner needs no calculator.
