Vegan protein powder comes from legumes, grains, seeds or nuts. Pea and soy are the most common sources; rice, hemp and pumpkin appear in blends. Protein per 100 grams varies widely: a pea isolate sits around 80 %, while a whole hemp powder stays near 50 %.

## What is inside a vegan tub

That difference matters more than the vegan label. Two tubs at the same price can deliver very different amounts of protein per scoop. The first sum is the usual one: protein per 100 grams and protein per serving.

## All nine amino acids are there

Plant proteins contain the nine essential amino acids. The idea that only soy is complete is false. What changes is the amount of each: grains tend to fall short on lysine and legumes on methionine. That limit is solved by combining sources across the day, without pairing them on the same plate.

A pea and rice powder is the classic example: rice supplies methionine and pea supplies lysine. A meal of legumes with a grain does the same. The incomplete label describes an isolated protein, not a diet.

## Leucine and grams: the two practical variables

Leucine is the signal that starts protein synthesis, and plant proteins usually have less per gram than whey. That is why a 25 gram serving of pea delivers less signal than 25 grams of whey. The fix is a higher dose or a blend of sources.

At 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day and servings of 25 to 40 grams, most trainees cover the requirement. If plant protein is the only source, a 30 to 40 gram serving or 25 % more powder compensates for the lower density. The page on [soy versus milk protein](/en/soy-vs-milk-protein/) works through that sum.

## Digestibility: what can be said

Plant proteins can digest somewhat worse than animal proteins because of the fibre and antinutrients that come with them. Isolates reduce that effect by removing part of the fibre. Saying they are more digestible than animal protein is false in most cases.

The difference shows up mainly at high doses. At 25 to 40 grams per serving, most people have no trouble. Anyone who notices gas at the start usually improves by lowering the dose and raising it slowly.

## Real advantages and limits

The main advantage is the absence of lactose and the ability to cover protein without animal products. Some sources add fibre and micronutrients that whey does not have. That is all. There is no evidence that it speeds up metabolism or reduces inflammation; body composition depends on total calories and protein.

The main limit is leucine density and, in some products, taste and texture. Allergens are worth checking too: soy and gluten appear in many blends. The page on [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) reviews the promises that do not hold.

## How to choose and how to take it

Compare protein per 100 grams, leucine per serving, allergens and the price of 25 grams of protein. A pea isolate with 80 % protein is usually more efficient than a whole powder with 50 %. If you choose a blend, check that the first source is not rice alone.

Take 25 to 40 grams per meal, three to five times a day, and set the total at 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo. Vegan protein needs no different schedule or special window. The guide to [buying protein powder](/en/protein-powder-guide/) shows how to read the label, and the one on [protein pros and cons](/en/protein-pros-and-cons/) compares the four types.

## For anyone who trains without animal products

Vegan protein does the same job as whey if the grams and leucine arrive. It is not an inferior supplement or a shortcut.
