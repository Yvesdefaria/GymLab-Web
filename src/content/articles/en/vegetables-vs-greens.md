A neighbourhood market mixes aubergines, radishes, chard and tomatoes without asking which category they belong to. That mix explains why everyday language treats the two words as interchangeable, and why the technical answer helps little when filling a basket.

## An administrative classification

The Spanish Food Code defines a hortaliza as any herbaceous horticultural plant that can be used as food, raw or cooked. A verdura is, in the same text, the hortaliza whose edible part is its green organs. The border keeps trade and inspection in order. It does not separate nutrients.

An aubergine is a hortaliza and not a verdura. Spinach falls into both categories with the same composition. Neither word says how much fibre, potassium or vitamin C the food carries.

## The groups that do differ

Hortalizas include very different parts of the plant, and that is a useful difference when buying and cooking.

Dry grain legumes such as lentils and chickpeas are not on this list. They are seeds of leguminous plants and are counted separately, with their own composition and their own place in a meal.

A tomato is a fruit to a botanist and a vegetable in the kitchen. Those labels keep language tidy and do not change what the food brings to the plate.

## The 400 grams that do matter

The figure repeated by health authorities is at least 400 grams a day from fruit and vegetables. It is a population recommendation, not an exact dose, and five 80-gram portions spread across the day meet it.

For anyone who trains, that base covers fibre, potassium, folate and vitamin C, and leaves less room for products that deliver calories with little satiety. Colour variety helps cover different vitamins without counting anything.

Frozen and canned produce counts. Check added salt and sugar and pick the versions closest to plain vegetables, because a jar with sauce can double the sodium in a meal.

## What cooking changes

Vitamin C and folate are lost to heat and dissolve in water. Boiling in plenty of water and throwing it away is the worst combination. Steaming, microwaving with little water or a quick stir-fry keeps more.

There is no need to soak vegetables before cooking. They lose water-soluble vitamins and gain little. Rinsing under the tap and rubbing is enough. Cutting large pieces and keeping the skin where possible reduce the exposed surface.

Vitamins A, E and K are fat-soluble and are absorbed better with some fat. A drizzle of olive oil on cooked vegetables is part of the dish, not an extra.

Alternating one raw portion and one cooked portion covers texture and nutrients without complicating the menu. A lunch salad and sautéed vegetables at night are enough on most days.

## Storage that avoids losses

Most vegetables want cold, dry air. Washing them before storing adds moisture and speeds decay, so wash them just before cooking.

Keep fast-ripening fruit such as bananas and tomatoes away from leafy greens: the ethylene they release speeds their decline. Potatoes and onions do better in a dark, ventilated place, outside the fridge.

Whatever will not be eaten in two or three days gets cooked and frozen. A vegetable frozen at its peak keeps more vitamins than one that spends a week in the drawer.

## Choose without obsessing

Season and distance improve taste and price. They do not turn one vegetable into another. A bag of frozen produce can reach the plate fresher than a tomato that travelled four days.

The practical goal is vegetables on half the plate at two meals and fruit in sight. The rest is cooking and habit.
