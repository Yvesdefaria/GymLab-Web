It is a short exercise and an easy one to ruin. The temptation is to load it like a press, and with weight the fly turns into a push with the arms wide.

## Setup

Bench at 15 to 30 degrees of decline, feet under the rollers and knees bent. Set the backrest before you pick up the dumbbells; changing it later means climbing out of the bench. With the dumbbells on the thighs, lie back and bring them up until the arms are extended over the lower chest. The palms face each other and the elbows hold a fixed bend of 10 to 15 degrees.

The shoulder blades come together and stay on the pad. The head is low, so get in slowly and use light dumbbells the first few times.

## The arc

Open the arms out to the sides until they are level with the shoulders. The decline puts that point closer to the floor than a flat bench does, and gravity pulls the dumbbells in the same direction the chest stretches.

Do not go below shoulder line. That line is the reference, not the floor: once the dumbbells drop below it, the arc is over. The stretch is at its peak there; anything lower is the shoulder working in a position it does not control.

On the way in, the dumbbells come close without touching and without crossing. The elbow does not bend to press: if it bends, the movement becomes a press and the chest stops following the arc.

## Why it is a stretch drill

In a dumbbell fly, resistance always pulls towards the floor. At the bottom, with the arms open, the weight sits far from the shoulder and the chest holds the load at its longest. At the top, with the hands together over the chest, the weight falls almost straight down over the shoulder and the tension disappears.

The decline sharpens both ends: the bottom opens further and the top sits closer to vertical. That is why the exercise is there to tolerate the stretch under load, not to add kilos. Progression means range and control, not weight. A slow arc also adds time under tension, the one variable this exercise controls well.

It follows the same logic as the [flat dumbbell fly](/en/flat-dumbbell-fly/), with a slightly longer range.

## Mistakes

- **Bending the elbows on the way in.** It turns the fly into a press.
- **Going below shoulder line.** The chest gains no range and the shoulder loses support.
- **Using press weight.** The arc shortens and the exercise loses its stretch.
- **Bouncing out of the bottom.** The way out has to be controlled, not an impulse.
- **Turning the head to watch the dumbbells.** The neck turns and the head stops resting on the pad.
- **Chaining sets without sitting up.** The head stays low, so sit up between sets.

## An anatomy note

The pectoralis minor does not move the arm. It runs from the ribs to the scapula and pulls it forward and down, so it takes no part in the arc of the fly. The work belongs to the pectoralis major, with the front deltoid helping at the top. [Chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/) separates the two layers.

## Dose and where it goes in the session

Two or three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 to 90 seconds of rest and light dumbbells. Add a kilo once 15 reps hold the same arc without losing the bottom.

It goes at the end of the session, after the [decline dumbbell press](/en/decline-dumbbell-press/) or the [decline barbell press](/en/decline-barbell-press/), once the chest is worked. It is a good finish for anyone who tolerates the stretch well; for anyone who does not, the [decline bench pullover](/en/decline-bench-pullover/) covers the same range with straight arms.
