Avocado has earned its place in the modern diet for three concrete reasons: good fat, fibre and a texture that can replace sauces and spreads. There is no magic in it, and it does not need any.

Its problem is the same as nuts: it is dense. A hundred grams sits near 160 kcal, and a whole fruit can pass 300. That density explains why it helps in some diets and gets in the way in others.

## The portion: 100 g is half a small avocado

The version in circulation said 100 g equals half a medium avocado. It depends on the size, and the difference matters: a medium avocado of the kind sold loose weighs about 200 g of flesh, so 100 g is half a small one or a third of a large one.

The rough figures that work in practice are 30 to 70 g when cutting, 50 to 100 g at maintenance and 70 to 150 g when bulking. They are starting points, not rules: the day's fat split decides.

## Monounsaturated fat and fibre

Almost all the fat in avocado is unsaturated, and most of it is monounsaturated. When it replaces butter, cheese or saturated fat, it improves the diet's lipid profile. When it is added on top of everything else, it only adds calories.

Fibre is the other strength: 6.7 g per 100 g, more than most fruit. That fibre slows digestion, helps satiety and feeds the microbiota. The [fats](/en/dietary-fats/) guide explains how to fit this serving into the rest of the day.

## Micronutrients

Potassium is the most generous mineral: 485 mg per 100 g, more than a banana by weight. It also brings folate, vitamin K, vitamin E and vitamin C, and some lutein, a carotenoid associated with eye health.

Avocado fat has a practical effect on the plate: it helps absorb the fat-soluble vitamins of the vegetables beside it. A salad with avocado makes better use of the vitamin A and K in the leaves than the same salad without fat.

## The cardiovascular evidence

Reviews of avocado trials find small drops in total and LDL cholesterol, especially in people with a worse profile. There is no clear effect on weight when avocado replaces another fat and calories stay the same.

That is what the current evidence supports. Avocado does not replace treatment or clean arteries. It does not offset a disordered diet either. The cardiovascular idea that does hold is the usual one: less saturated fat and more unsaturated fat.

## In a deficit and in a surplus

When cutting, avocado is useful because it satisfies with little volume and makes the diet easier to keep. It is also easy to overcount: half an avocado in the salad, a little oil in the pan and a handful of nuts for dessert add more fat than it seems. The [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/) guide insists on the total, and the [low-fat diet](/en/low-fat-diet/) page shows the opposite mistake.

When bulking, 100 g of avocado adds 160 kcal without filling the stomach. It is a convenient way to raise calories with micronutrients, as long as the rest of the meal has enough protein.

## Quick ideas

- Toast with avocado, two eggs and tomato.
- Rice bowl with fish, avocado and cucumber.
- Chicken, chickpea and avocado salad.
- Wholegrain wrap with turkey, avocado and vegetables.
- Guacamole with lime, salt and tomato, served with protein on the side.

## Precautions

Avocado allergy exists and sometimes crosses with latex allergy. If you notice itching in the mouth or symptoms after eating it, see a professional. People with kidney disease who must control potassium should also adjust the amount with their doctor.

Digestively, avocado is rich in sorbitol and can sit badly in an irritable gut. It is not a dangerous food, and it is not compulsory either.

## Where it fits

With [cuttlefish](/en/cuttlefish/) or fish it makes a complete meal with lean protein and fat. With [oats](/en/oats/) and fruit it covers breakfast, and with [nuts](/en/nuts-nutrition/) it shares the day's fat space, so try not to combine both without counting.
