## What the high pulley loads

The pectoralis major has two heads, clavicular and sternal, and both contract on every rep. What changes with arm position is how the effort is shared, not which region switches on alone.

In a high-pulley crossover the arm travels down from above and closes towards the midline. That path matches the direction of the sternal portion, the part that runs from the sternum to the humerus. That is why the exercise is felt in the lower and middle chest, not at the top.

Leaning forward does not move that region. It changes the angle the cable pulls from and lets the tension pass through the middle of the chest instead of staying at the shoulder. [Chest anatomy](/en/chest-anatomy/) explains why both heads always work together.

## The setup: pulleys, foot and torso

Set both pulleys above shoulder height, with one handle on each. Stand in the middle, the same distance from both, and put one foot forward for a base.

Lean the torso between 15 and 30 degrees, back straight and the natural curve kept. More lean turns the exercise into an incline cable press; a fully upright posture leaves the chest outside the line of pull.

Start with the arms open to the sides and almost perpendicular to the body, the elbows at a fixed bend of 10 to 15 degrees and the palms facing down or slightly in. That bend stays still for the whole set.

## The path: down and together

Bring the hands forward and down along a wide arc until they meet in front of the lower chest. The elbow neither opens nor closes to press: it carries the shoulder movement.

Hold the position for a second, with the hands close but not crossing one in front of the other. Crossing adds internal rotation at the shoulder and does nothing for the chest contraction.

Return along the same arc, controlling the cable, to the open position. The torso does not move during the rep. If you need to throw the body forward to close, the weight is too high or the starting lean is too small.

## The dose

Three sets of 12 to 15 reps with 60 or 90 seconds of rest. This is an isolation exercise: the load is chosen so every rep keeps the same arc and the same pause.

The normal progression is load, in small jumps on each pulley, once 15 clean reps come out. After that, tempo: three seconds back and a one-second pause. [Muscular failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) is not needed here; leaving one rep in reserve keeps the arc intact.

## Tension through the whole arc

The advantage of the cable is that resistance stays present while the arm moves, including the part of the range where a dumbbell offers nothing. That is why the crossover is programmed for time under tension, not for the weight you can bring together.

That detail changes how you count reps. A set of 15 with a one-second pause and a three-second return lasts longer than a set of 15 with a bounce, and the chest notices. The [advantages of cable exercises](/en/cable-exercise-benefits/) expand the argument.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **Turning it into a press.** If the elbow extends as you close, the triceps takes over and the chest loses the arc.
- **Leaning the torso too far forward.** The cable pulls from another angle and the exercise changes.
- **Crossing the hands.** It adds shoulder rotation and shortens the useful range.
- **Shrugging the shoulders.** The shoulder blades ride high and the chest loses its base.
- **Loading too heavy.** The body swings and the arc becomes a shove.

## Crossovers, incline cable flies and pec deck

This is the standing version with high pulleys. The [incline cable fly](/en/incline-cable-fly/) uses a 30-degree bench between two low pulleys and looks for the same kind of tension from another angle. The [pec deck fly](/en/pec-deck-fly/) does the work on a machine that guides the path.

All three share the fixed elbow and the light load. The comparison between [machines and free weights](/en/machines-vs-free-weights-chest/) helps you decide which one fits your session.

## Where it goes in the session

It is a finishing exercise. It goes after the presses, when the chest is already worked and heavy load is no longer the goal. It also works as the first exercise of a light session or as a partner to an incline press.
