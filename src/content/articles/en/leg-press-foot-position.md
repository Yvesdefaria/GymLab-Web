Moving your feet on the platform is the first thing people try when leg development stalls. The usual promise is that each position works a different area. The reality is more modest: the split changes, not the muscle that contracts.

## What changes and what does not

Before the variants, fix the rule. Foot position shifts emphasis a little and changes which muscles share the work. It does not change which quadriceps head contracts, because the four bellies share the patellar tendon. [Quadriceps myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) develops the full argument.

With that clear, each position makes sense as an adjustment and as variation. Changing the angle now and then renews the stimulus and keeps the body from adapting to a single movement.

## The muscles that work on the leg press

The press works the same muscles as the squat, with less help from the stabilisers: the backrest and the platform support the torso. The quadriceps performs knee extension with its four bellies: rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis and vastus intermedius. The gluteus maximus and the hamstrings, made up of the biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus, perform hip extension.

None of those muscles switches off when you move your feet. What changes is how much each one contributes.

## The normal position and the four variants

The normal position is the reference: feet shoulder-width apart, in the middle of the platform. From there, four basic modifications.

The key word is emphasis. In the high position the hip flexes more, the hamstrings and glutes take a larger share and the quadriceps gives part of the work away. Low does the opposite: the hip range shortens and the quadriceps carries more load. Wide brings the adductors into the movement and shares the effort with the vastus medialis. Narrow reduces that share and the vastus lateralis carries relatively more.

## Vertical changes matter more than lateral ones

Raising or lowering the feet changes emphasis quite a bit. Opening or closing them changes less, and the difference shows mainly in hip comfort and in how much the adductors join in.

That is why the high and low positions are the variants most used to change the work, while wide and narrow work better as comfort adjustments. If the hip protests with the feet together, opening them a little solves the problem without changing the exercise.

## How to try them without punishing the knee

Start light and add weight slowly as you get used to the new position. The body needs a few sets to adapt to the change in leverage, and the first time you try a position is not the moment to chase a record.

Two precautions before adding weight. With knee problems, avoid full flexion and full extension: lower only as far as you stay in control. With low-back problems, the horizontal press is safer than the inclined one because the weight does not fall on the lifter, and in any case do not let the pelvis lift off the backrest at the end of the range.

## Which position to choose

Start with normal and train a few weeks on it. If the quadriceps lags, try low; if the glutes and hamstrings need more work, raise the feet. Lateral changes are for when the hip asks for something else.

In the end, foot position is a detail inside a plan. It adds a little emphasis and some variety. Progression is still what changes the outcome.
