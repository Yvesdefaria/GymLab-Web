The maths looks simple: eat more, train hard and muscle grows. In practice it fails on the size of the surplus. A large surplus speeds up fat gain and forces a long cut afterwards that erases part of the progress.

## Why the surplus is small

Muscle grows slowly. A surplus of 200 to 300 kcal a day covers the cost of digesting more food, the extra cost of training and the margin needed to build tissue. It is an amount adjusted with the scale and with performance, not with a closed formula.

Eating 800 or 1,000 kcal extra does not multiply the gain. The body does not turn the surplus into muscle because you train more; the excess is stored as fat. The speed of the scale is not the speed of muscle either.

## How to estimate maintenance

Two routes work better together. The first is an estimate with the Mifflin-St Jeor formula: 10 times weight in kilos, plus 6.25 times height in centimetres, minus 5 times age, plus 5 for men and minus 161 for women. The result is multiplied by an activity factor between 1.2 and 1.7.

The second is empirical. Eat a stable amount for two weeks, record weight every morning and compare the average of the first week with that of the second. If weight does not move, that intake is maintenance.

The two methods diverge at first and correct over time. The calculator gives the starting point; the scale gives the answer.

## The macro split

Protein stays between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo. Above 2.2 there is no demonstrated advantage for hypertrophy in healthy people. Carbohydrate covers training, between 3 and 5 grams per kilo. Fat stays between 0.8 and 1 gram per kilo to support hormones and vitamin absorption.

The practical order is protein and fat first, carbohydrate after and calories at the end. That way the split does not collapse when the total rises.

## A worked example

A man of 80 kilos, 180 centimetres, 30 years old, three or four weight sessions a week and an office job. Mifflin-St Jeor gives 1,780 kcal of basal metabolism. With a factor of 1.375, maintenance sits near 2,450 kcal.

The 250 kcal surplus takes the target to 2,700 kcal. The split comes out at 144 grams of protein, 385 grams of carbohydrate and 64 grams of fat, which add up to 576, 1,540 and 576 kcal respectively. These are starting figures, not a prescription.

## The scale every two weeks

The target rate is 0.25 to 0.5 % of body weight per week. At 80 kilos, that is 200 to 400 grams a week, or 0.4 to 0.8 kilos over two weeks.

If weight rises more than expected in two weeks, cut 100 or 150 kcal, mostly from carbohydrate. If it does not rise at all and training progresses, add 100 or 150 kcal. Weighing daily and comparing weekly averages avoids reacting to a day of water retention.

Strength and measurements count as much as weight. Two extra kilos in two weeks with an unchanged waist is usually water and glycogen. Half a kilo with reps going up is the signal you want.

## Eat more to burn more, with nuances

Eating more raises expenditure through digestion and through the cost of moving a heavier body, and weight training increases lean mass over time. The effect exists and is small: it does not turn a 1,000 kcal surplus into a 300 kcal one.

NEAT, the movement that does not count as exercise, rises somewhat when you eat more and falls when you restrict. It is one of the reasons the maths is never exact and why the trend over several weeks matters more.

## Mistakes that stall the gain

The most common: starting with a huge surplus, skipping progressive overload, changing the plan every week, leaving protein at the minimum and sleeping little. The guide to [muscle anabolism](/en/muscle-anabolism/) explains why the session and the meals count together, and the one on [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) organises the training.

## Supplements: protein and little else

Protein powder is convenient food. It helps when the quota cannot be met with meals, and it adds nothing missing from a plate of chicken, eggs or legumes. Creatine monohydrate has the strongest evidence for strength and mass, at 3 to 5 grams a day.
