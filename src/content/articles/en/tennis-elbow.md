> **Notice:** This guide is general information and does not replace an assessment by a healthcare professional. If the pain is intense, appears suddenly, wakes you at night or comes with loss of strength, get advice before you keep training.

"My elbow hurts" is one of the most repeated lines in a weights room. It almost always points to the same spot: the outside, the area that complains when you squeeze something and turn your wrist at the same time.

## What tennis elbow is

Tennis elbow is pain at the origin of the wrist and finger extensors, on the lateral epicondyle of the humerus. Those tendons attach there and work every time you close your hand or extend the wrist against resistance.

The name speaks of inflammation, though what shows up in a clinic is a tendinopathy from overload: the tendon accumulates changes from repeated microtrauma and loses its capacity to tolerate load. That difference matters for treatment, because a tendon that is not loaded gets weaker.

It also appears in padel, climbing, gardening and any job that combines a firm grip with repeated wrist movement. A professional makes the diagnosis after examining you; this page describes the picture and does not confirm it.

## The movements that warn you

Pain appears when you grip and when you lift with the palm facing down. Serving a jug, turning a key, opening a jar or holding a barbell all reproduce it quite faithfully.

It can also wake you at night, especially if you sleep with the elbow bent or resting on something. Direct pressure on the outside of the elbow usually hurts even at rest.

Two signs deserve an appointment without waiting: pain that does not settle with relative rest and a loss of grip strength. A weak fist is not normal and should be assessed.

## Relative rest: what to drop and what to keep

The instinct is to stop using the arm until the pain disappears. That absolute rest weakens the tendon and lengthens the problem: when you come back, the tissue tolerates less load than before and the relapse arrives early.

Relative rest means removing the movement that aggravates it and keeping everything else. Change the grip, lower the weight, reduce the range and pick variations that do not force you to squeeze with the wrist turned. The guide to [common gym injuries](/en/common-gym-injuries/) describes the general principle and the loading protocol used with professional support.

Load is the treatment. What changes is the dose and the order, not the decision to train.

## The loading ladder for the elbow

Start with isometrics. Squeeze your fist or push gently against a wall and hold the contraction without moving the joint. The aim is to load the tendon with a stimulus that does not irritate it, and it keeps you working while the pain settles.

Then comes eccentric work. With a light weight, extend the wrist quickly upwards and lower it slowly. Two sets of 20 to 30 reps is the usual starting point, always with a weight that lets you finish without losing control.

Grip work comes last. A rubber ball trains the squeeze, capped at 20 reps. If squeezing the ball hurts, it is still too early.

Stretches go alongside the strength work. Extend the wrist with the arm straight, hold for 4 to 6 seconds and repeat 4 to 6 times. Do the same flexing the wrist with the elbow extended. Stretching does not replace loading; it reduces the tension the muscle puts on the tendon.

Strengthening starts once flexibility has returned and daily pain is controlled. Forcing the order is the most common way to relapse.

## Ice, straps and other aids

Ice relieves. Apply it for 15 to 20 minutes after training or work, with a cloth between the skin and the pack, and treat it as a truce for the pain. It does not repair the tendon and it does not speed recovery.

A counterforce strap modifies the load. It sits below the elbow and shares tension between the tendon and the band, which lets you train with less discomfort. It cures nothing on its own, but it does change how the force is distributed.

Friction massage is applied by a professional, with the fingertips and pressure that builds slowly, for 3 to 5 minutes. Doing it yourself without guidance usually irritates more than it helps.

## Injections, surgery and when to get help

Doctors often use cortisone injections. Sometimes one is enough and in other cases it takes three. They relieve pain, they do not repair the tendon, and the relief can be temporary: that is why the decision and the number belong to the professional assessing you.

Surgery is the last resort, and it does not guarantee a solution either. It is considered when conservative treatment has failed for a reasonable period.

Ask for an assessment if the pain does not improve for weeks, wakes you every night or comes with loss of strength. The guide on [when to see a sports doctor](/en/when-to-see-a-sports-doctor/) helps with the timing, and the one on [chronic pain and physiotherapy](/en/chronic-pain-physiotherapy/) covers the case that has already lasted months.

## How to avoid a relapse

Technique is the first place to look. A bench press or a French press with the elbows in the wrong position repeats the same microtrauma for months, and afterwards it is hard to correct. The guide to [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) goes through those details.

Grip matters too. Holding the bar with your fingers, squeezing too hard or using a grip that forces the wrist to turn distributes load badly. [Grip strength training](/en/grip-strength-training/) explains how to work it without irritating the elbow.

And progression has the final say. Add weight when the tendon tolerates it, not when the plan demands it. If the elbow starts complaining in the warm-up, you are one step late.

Pain on the inside of the elbow and [carpal tunnel syndrome](/en/carpal-tunnel-syndrome/) are different pictures, even when they share gripping movements. If the discomfort changes area, the assessment changes too.
