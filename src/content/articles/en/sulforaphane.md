Broccoli has spent decades as the vegetable nutrition books hold up as an example. The reason has a chemical name and a curious story: a compound that does not exist in the living plant, that is built the moment its cells break, and that heat can destroy before it reaches anything.

## What it is and where it comes from

The plant stores glucoraphanin, a glucosinolate, in one compartment and the enzyme myrosinase in another. While the cell is whole, they never meet. Cutting, chewing or crushing breaks the membranes, the enzyme meets the glucosinolate and turns it into sulforaphane, an isothiocyanate.

That sulforaphane is what gives raw broccoli its pungent, slightly bitter taste. It also appears in radish, mustard and wasabi, and in smaller amounts in cauliflower, Brussels sprouts and cabbage, which store other glucosinolates.

## The chemistry, in the right order

Two names get confused all the time. Glucoraphanin is the glucosinolate inside the plant. Sulforaphane is the isothiocyanate that comes out of it. They are not the same molecule and they do not weigh the same: glucoraphanin sits near 437 g per mole and sulforaphane near 177.

That gap matters when figures from the internet are read. A milligram of glucoraphanin does not turn into a milligram of sulforaphane: the theoretical ceiling is about 0.4 mg per milligram of glucosinolate, and in practice it lands well below that. Tables that mix micrograms of one with micrograms of the other are comparing different things.

That is why this page gives no figure per 100 g. Glucoraphanin content shifts with variety, batch, floret size and days in the fridge, and a single number would be a fiction with two decimals.

## Chopping and waiting: the part you control

Chop the broccoli and leave it to rest for about 40 minutes before cooking and the conversion happens while the enzyme is still alive. Heat can then deactivate myrosinase, but the sulforaphane already formed holds up far better.

The trick has a limit: if the broccoli is already cooked, adding mustard powder or grated radish puts some myrosinase back into the dish and recovers part of the conversion. That is the way out for anyone who only eats cooked vegetables.

Boiling is the worst method for two reasons at once: hot water deactivates the enzyme and also carries the glucosinolate away, since it is soluble. Cooking broccoli in little water and using the liquid reduces the loss without removing it.

## The Johns Hopkins story

Scientific interest starts in Paul Talalay's laboratory at the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, in Baltimore. There sulforaphane was identified as a strong inducer of phase 2 enzymes, the system the liver and the cells use to neutralise foreign substances. In 1997 the same group published that three-day broccoli sprouts concentrate far more glucoraphanin than the adult plant.

That finding explains why sprouts became an object of study. It also explains the outsized enthusiasm around the compound, tested over the last two decades in cells, in animals and in people with results of very different sizes.

## What the evidence covers and what it does not

In cells and animals, sulforaphane activates defence pathways, slows the division of damaged cells and changes gene expression. Those are solid results in that setting and they do not transfer automatically to a person who eats broccoli twice a week.

In people, trials with sprouts and extracts have mostly measured enzyme and inflammation markers. Population studies associate a high intake of cruciferous vegetables with a lower incidence of some cancers, and that association is observational: people who eat more vegetables also do other things differently.

What can be said without exaggeration is that broccoli is a vegetable with fibre, folate, vitamin C and vitamin K, and that sulforaphane is an interesting compound the plant builds when it breaks. It is not a treatment, it cures nothing and it replaces nobody's medication.

## The antioxidant nuance

Sulforaphane is not an antioxidant that neutralises free radicals directly, the way vitamin C or E does. It works indirectly: it switches on the cell's own machinery, and that machinery keeps working for days. The guide to [free radicals](/en/free-radicals/) explains why the body needs some of that signal rather than none.

From there comes the warning almost nobody gives. Loading the body with high doses of direct antioxidants can blunt training adaptation, because the oxidative signal of exercise is part of the stimulus. [Synthetic antioxidants](/en/synthetic-antioxidants/) at supplement doses are the extreme case; a serving of broccoli does not come close.

## How much vegetable, how often

The general recommendation is still five servings of fruit and vegetables a day, with cruciferous vegetables two or three times a week. That frequency covers folate, fibre and vitamin C without depending on any isolated compound, and leaves room for the rest of the vegetables.

On a training plate, broccoli works as a bulky, low-calorie side. The guide to [vegetables and muscle mass](/en/vegetables-for-muscle/) takes apart the idea that vegetables slow growth, and the one on [vegetables for dinner](/en/vegetables-or-fruit-for-dinner/) solves the last meal of the day.

If broccoli competes with another vegetable in the same week, [sweet potato](/en/sweet-potato/) brings the carbohydrate and potassium missing here, and [oat bran](/en/oat-bran-for-weight-loss/) covers the soluble fibre broccoli lacks.
