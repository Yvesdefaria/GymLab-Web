Peanut butter triggers two opposite reactions. For some it is the easiest way to raise calories when eating is hard. For others it is a bomb that ruins a cut in one spoonful. Neither view is entirely false, and both exaggerate.

The food is simple: roasted peanuts ground into a paste. What makes it useful is the mix of fat, protein and flavour. What makes it dangerous is the same calorie density, which invites a second spoon without measuring.

## Fat with protein along for the ride

A hundred grams of butter carries about 51 g of fat and 22 g of protein. The protein is real and not enough to count on it as a main dish: reaching 30 g would mean eating 135 g of butter and about 800 kcal. As a protein source it does not compete with eggs, fish or legumes.

Quality has a known limit too. Peanut protein is low in methionine, an essential amino acid, and in a varied diet that shortfall is covered by the rest of the meals. The mistake is replacing chicken with peanut butter and calling it a meal.

The figures are for 100 % peanut butter, without salt or added oil, and come from USDA FoodData Central. The reference serving is 20 g, one level tablespoon.

Most of the fat is unsaturated, mainly monounsaturated. That does not make it a light food: it is still fat, at 9 kcal per gram, which is why the spoon gets weighed at the start.

## The debate: does it make you fat?

No single food makes you fat; a sustained calorie surplus does. Peanut butter makes that surplus easy because it is dense and tastes good. A level tablespoon is 120 kcal. A heaped one can double the figure, and three heaped tablespoons a day add more than 700 kcal.

When cutting, the answer is not to remove it but to weigh it. Twenty grams inside a meal with protein and vegetables fit almost any plan. The problem appears when the jar opens mid-afternoon and the spoon comes back three times. The [fats](/en/dietary-fats/) guide helps with the daily split, and [nuts](/en/nuts-nutrition/) shares the same logic of a small, dense serving.

## The label lesson

A good peanut butter's ingredient list has one line: peanuts. At most, a pinch of salt. If palm oil, sugar, dextrose, syrup or hydrogenated oils appear, the product is no longer just peanuts.

So-called light versions usually replace part of the fat with sugar or starch, and sometimes carry almost the same calories. Fat is not the problem with this food; the amount is. A jar with oil separated on top is normal: stir it and carry on. If you are allergic to peanuts, avoid the butter and check labels for traces: it is one of the most common food allergies and can be serious.

## Satiety or binge

Peanut butter fills you up and tastes very good at the same time. That combination is tricky. A little on toast takes the edge off hunger; the same product eaten by the spoon from the jar can become a binge the body does not register as a meal.

If that happens to you, buy small jars, keep the butter in a cupboard rather than on the table, and serve it on a plate. Weighing it stops feeling fussy once you see that 20 g covers a whole slice of toast.

## When and how to use it

Before training, a small amount two or three hours before the session brings sustained energy. Do not eat much right before: fat slows digestion and a heavy stomach shows in your sets.

With [rice cakes](/en/rice-cakes/) it makes a snack with fast carbohydrate and fat that slows the glucose rise. It also works in porridge, in a sauce for chicken or mixed with yogurt. In every case, the spoon counts.

## Roasted or natural

Roasted peanuts taste stronger and lose some vitamins to the heat. The practical difference is small. If roasting makes you enjoy the meal and keep the diet, go ahead.

## Where it fits in your diet

It is a nut in paste form and behaves like one. In a meal with [avocado](/en/avocado-in-your-diet/) it competes for the same fat space, so pick one of the two. With [cuttlefish](/en/cuttlefish/) or any lean fish it brings the fat the dish lacks.

Peanut butter is neither a superfood nor a sin. It is a tasty fat with some protein, and its role in the diet is decided by the kitchen scale, not the label.
