The captain's chair is one of the oldest machines in the gym and one of the few that has barely changed: two forearm pads, a back pad and the body hanging. With that you can train the abs with bodyweight and no dependence on grip.

## Why it is easier than hanging

On a bar, the limit usually arrives in the hand before it arrives in the abs. Here the forearms carry the weight and the back rests on the pad, so the body does not have to stabilise itself through the shoulders.

That comfort has a price: the pad removes part of the control demand. It is the same pattern, with less stability work and more room to focus on the pelvis. That is why it works as a step between bench work and the bar.

## Starting position

Step onto the machine and place the forearms on the pads, with the hands on the grips. The back stays against the pad and the body hangs freely from the hip.

Straighten the legs and bring the feet together. Lock that position for the whole set: if the knees bend halfway through, the lever changes and the set stops being comparable.

Keep the shoulders down and back, without shrugging. The neck stays in line with the trunk and the eyes look forward.

## Execution

Take the feet above horizontal. There is the difference between full work and half a movement: stopping with the legs perpendicular to the trunk leaves the final part out.

At the end of the range the pelvis rotates and the glutes come off the pad. That rotation is the sign that the abs have finished tilting the pelvis and the trunk flexes to close the contraction.

Exhale at the top, hold for a beat and lower under control. The descent is not a drop: brake it back to the start, with the legs still and no swing.

## Straight legs or bent knees

The distance from hip to foot is the lever. With straight legs, the same muscle moves more weight and the range gets harder. With bent knees, the foot stays close and the set can last longer.

Each version has its moment. The straight one opens the set, while the abs are fresh, and the bent one closes it when fatigue shows up.

## Common mistakes

- Raising only the knees and leaving the pelvis still.
- Swinging the legs behind the body to build momentum.
- Stopping at horizontal and going no further.
- Pushing on the pads with the arms to help.
- Dropping fast and braking at the end of the range.

## How to progress

The simplest progression is not extra weight but a different split of the set. Start with straight legs and, when technique breaks, bend the knees and finish the exercise as a knee raise.

When twelve or fifteen clean reps come easily, cut the rest in half. The next set starts without full recovery and the abs reach the limit sooner with the same technique.

## Where it fits

It is a good second movement in an ab session, after the loaded work and before the floor exercises. It also works as the first exercise for anyone who cannot yet hold the bar.

It shares the pattern with [hanging leg raises](/en/hanging-leg-raises/), which lengthen the range and add the grip demand.
