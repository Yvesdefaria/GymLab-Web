The dumbbell version has a clear slot: travel, gyms without a leg curl machine, or home sessions with little equipment. It does not compete with the machine lying curl on load, and it still lets you train knee flexion against real resistance.

## How to do the dumbbell lying leg curl

Lie face down on a flat bench with the knees near the edge. The pelvis stays down and the hips stay in line with the torso. The hands rest on the floor or hold the front legs of the bench; resting them on the seat arches the lower back and kills the contraction.

Placing the weight is the delicate part. Ideally a partner sets a dumbbell between your feet, with one plate resting on the soles. High-soled shoes help keep the plate from sliding towards the ankles.

Without a partner, pinch the dumbbell between the soles or use an ankle weight. In the single-leg version, one foot holds the weight and the other leg rests.

Curl until the calves are vertical, squeeze for one second and lower until the calves come close to parallel with the floor. The pelvis does not leave the bench at any point.

## The top is calves vertical

Above vertical, the dumbbell loses support and the lever unloads. The rep ends there, not when the weight falls.

Control the lowering until the calves approach parallel. Letting the feet rest between reps breaks tension and turns the movement into a swing.

## Why the machine wins

The first limit is balance: the dumbbell is held with the feet and any rush throws it off. The second is the pinch: a heavy load cannot be held between the soles without slipping.

The third is the pelvis. The hip extensors and the erectors hold it on the bench; when the dumbbell gets heavy, the back and glutes fail before the hamstrings. That is why progression goes through an ankle weight or a band, not a heavier dumbbell.

A machine also fixes the knee position, so the roller stays above the ankle instead of sliding down the foot.

The machine versions solve all three problems: the [seated leg curl](/en/seated-leg-curl/) changes the hip angle, the [lying leg curl](/en/lying-leg-curl/) keeps the hip extended and the [standing leg curl](/en/standing-leg-curl/) leans the torso. The anatomy of the area is in [hamstring anatomy](/en/hamstring-anatomy/).

## Sets, reps and progression

This page's recommendation: three sets of 10 to 15 reps per leg, with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. The pause at the top lasts one second and the lowering takes two or three.

Progression goes through an ankle weight or a band. The ankle weight adds load in small steps without relying on the pinch; the band lengthens the lever and keeps tension at the end of the range. Switching to a heavier dumbbell usually worsens technique before it improves the load.

## Common mistakes

The first is lifting the pelvis to help with the back. The second is swinging the dumbbell with a jerk and letting gravity do the lowering.

The third is curling past vertical, where the weight falls. The fourth is choosing a dumbbell you cannot hold: the foot opens, the plate rubs the ankle and the rep is lost. The fifth is rushing the setup; with the bell in place, the first rep often starts before the feet are set.

## Where it fits

It goes at the end of a hamstring day when the machine is not available, or as a second exercise after a deadlift. It is a workaround, not the first choice for a complete routine.
