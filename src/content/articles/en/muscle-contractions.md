If you read a training programme or watch a technique video, the terms contraction and eccentric or concentric phase come up all the time. They are the two halves of a movement, the negative and the positive, and a third one joins them, the isometric, which happens when there is no movement at all. Understanding the three muscle contractions clears up plenty of instructions that are usually taken for granted.

## What a muscle contraction is

A muscle contracts against an external resistance, such as a barbell or a dumbbell, acting in the opposite direction to its force. The type of contraction depends on which force wins that exchange.

When the force produced by the muscle is greater than the resistance, the muscle overcomes it and shortens. When it is smaller, the muscle lengthens although it tries to shorten. And when the two forces are equal and opposite, the muscle keeps its length. Those three situations are concentric, eccentric and isometric muscle contractions.

## The eccentric contraction

In the eccentric phase, also called the negative, the muscle lengthens while it tries to shorten. It happens when the external resistance is greater than the force you produce, for example when you lower the bar to your chest on the bench press or control a dumbbell on the way down in a curl.

## The concentric contraction

In the concentric phase, the positive, the force of the muscle exceeds the resistance and the muscle shortens. It is the moment you beat the load, as when you press the bar up from your chest on the bench press.

## The isometric contraction

If the force of the muscle equals the external resistance and acts in the opposite direction, nothing moves. The muscle neither shortens nor lengthens: it keeps the same length. It is a static contraction, like holding a dumbbell still halfway through the range.

## Why the eccentric phase matters

When you lift weights, the concentric phase feels harder than the eccentric one. On the bench press, pressing the bar up from your chest (concentric contraction of the pectoralis major, the anterior deltoid and the triceps brachii) feels tougher than lowering it under control (eccentric contraction of the same muscles).

That feeling leads people to think the eccentric phase matters less. It is a mistake. There is evidence that, to get the most from strength training, you should emphasise both phases. The eccentric one lets you lower more slowly, and that controlled descent is part of the stimulus. The guide to [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) explains how to split the time between the two.

## Eccentric work and soreness

Between 24 and 48 hours after starting a physical activity programme or trying new exercises, muscle pain and discomfort are common. For a while it was thought to be the result of lactic acid build-up. Recent research suggests it may come from a combination of damage to muscle and connective tissue and an inflammatory reaction that activates pain receptors.

That damage is caused mainly by eccentric muscle contractions and the microtears they produce in muscle and connective tissue. The pain can last several days, and during that time range of motion and the ability to produce force quickly are reduced.

Supplements, massage, ice and ultrasound are the usual recommendations for soreness. The best strategy seems to be exercise itself: moving the affected area eases the discomfort. Forget sugar water and miracle cures.

One last detail that helps you read the set: the [rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/) does not change the type of contraction. Every set has an eccentric and a concentric phase, and as the set approaches [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/), the lowering phase is the first place where technique breaks down.
