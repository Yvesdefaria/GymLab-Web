A plate twist is trunk rotation with a weight in your hands. It is done seated or standing, with a plate, a bar or a stick, and the stated goal is to contract the obliques by turning the torso side to side.

Three versions keep showing up: the rotary machine with foot support, standing bar or stick twists, and plate twists seated or lying on the floor.

In the floor version you sit with bent knees, hold the plate with both hands in front of your chest and turn the trunk side to side. On the machine your feet stay fixed on the platform and the hips turn, with the torso following.

## Where it comes from

In the early twentieth century the rotary apparatus was standard in gymnasiums, alongside wall bars, the Swedish box and wooden clubs. It survived from Swedish gymnastics and can still be found in the occasional old gym. That age explains its prestige and also its problem: it was designed to chase mobility, not to measure the load the spine can take.

## The spine does rotate

The claim that the spine is not designed to rotate is false. Rotation is a normal trunk movement, and it is shared unevenly: the thoracic segment contributes around 35 to 45 degrees and the lumbar segment between 5 and 15. Most of the turn comes from the upper back and the hips, not from the lumbar segment.

## Where the risk sits

On the machine and in seated plate twists, the pelvis stays fixed and the trunk turns with a weight in the hands. The lever is long, the load sits far from the axis and the movement repeats. In that combination, much of the turn ends up in the lumbar spine, the part of the column with the least rotation available, and the plate adds shear to a segment already working near the edge of its range.

Execution makes it worse: many reps are done with momentum, fast, and the plate changes direction at the ends. The oblique produces rotation when the trunk overcomes a resistance, but here the resistance is small and the range is absorbed by passive structures.

There is no need to dramatise it either. A plate twist does not guarantee a hernia. What you have is an exercise with little muscle gain and an accumulated load on the lumbar spine that does not pay for the risk.

## Why it stays in routines

The plate twist has three advantages for whoever teaches it: it looks like it targets the waist, it needs no technique to learn and it can be loaded without much thought. The machine is also already installed in many gyms, and nobody removes apparatus that takes up space and still gets used.

On social media the floor format comes back every so often with a plate and a mat. It is cheaper, but the problem is the same: repeated lumbar rotation with a load that travels far from the axis.

## What to do instead

The rational substitute is anti-rotation work: instead of producing the turn under load, resisting it.

- **Pallof press.** On a cable, side-on to the machine, extend your arms in front of you and hold against the rotation pulling you towards the stack.
- **Side plank.** It holds the trunk against lateral tilt and makes the quadratus lumborum and the obliques work without moving the spine.
- **Dead bug.** Lying face up, extend one arm and the opposite leg while keeping the lower back flat. It teaches you to control the pelvis while the limbs move.

All three can be loaded and progressed, and none asks for lumbar rotation under weight. If you want variety, the guide to [ab workout routines](/en/ab-workout-routines/) includes plate and decline-bench work that respects the range.

## How to replace it in your routine

If the plate twist is your warm-up, swap it for hip and upper-back mobility without load. If it is your oblique work, use anti-rotation and side planks for sets of 20 to 40 seconds.

If the machine is still in your gym, leaving it where it stands costs you nothing. [Isometric abs](/en/isometric-abs/) covers stability without movement, and the guide to [back pain exercises](/en/back-pain-exercises/) lays out strengthening work for when the lower back has already complained.
