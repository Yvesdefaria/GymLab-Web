The fats in a food oxidise when they meet air and light. The result is a rancid smell, a dull colour and a loss of quality you notice on the first spoonful. To slow it down, manufacturers add antioxidants, and some of them are synthetic compounds with a chemical name and an E number.

## Why antioxidants are added

An antioxidant donates electrons and blocks the chain reaction that degrades fats. Natural tocopherols do it, and so do BHA and BHT, two synthetic compounds that have been in the food industry for decades.

The job is technical rather than nutritional. The additive protects the product over months of storage and stops the oil or fat developing off-flavours. Without that protection, many foods would last weeks instead of months.

## The E number is a safety list

The E prefix means the additive is authorised in the European Union after a safety evaluation. The list covers colourings, preservatives, emulsifiers and antioxidants, and each one has a code.

Antioxidants occupy the series from E300 to E321. It holds ascorbic acid, which is vitamin C, tocopherols, which are vitamin E, and the two synthetics most often discussed, BHA and BHT.

The table shows something that usually surprises people: vitamin C and vitamin E are E numbers too. The difference between a natural and a synthetic antioxidant is not the safety of the category but the specific compound and its dose.

## BHA and BHT: what the evidence says

BHA sits in IARC group 2B, the one that gathers compounds "possibly carcinogenic to humans". That classification rests on stomach tumours seen in rodents given very high doses, not on studies in people eating dietary amounts.

Group 2B brings together very different substances, from aloe vera to traditional Asian pickled vegetables. It does not mean a normal dose causes cancer; it means the evidence is limited and more research is warranted. The gap between a high dose in an animal and a low dose in a person is the heart of the discussion.

BHT has a similar history and a later review. Neither is banned in the European Union, and both have an acceptable daily intake set by scientific committees.

## What the ADI means

The ADI is the amount of an additive a person can eat every day for a lifetime without appreciable health risk. It is expressed in milligrams per kilo of body weight and calculated with a wide safety margin over the highest dose that produced no effects in animals.

For BHA the ADI sits near 0.5 milligrams per kilo per day. In a 70-kilo person that is about 35 milligrams a day, a figure the usual diet stays far away from. Real exposure is estimated by comparing the additive content of each food with average consumption of that food.

## EFSA's re-evaluation

The European Food Safety Authority reviewed every additive authorised before 2009, one by one. BHA was re-evaluated in 2011 and BHT in 2012, and in both cases an ADI was kept under the existing conditions of use.

The process is a periodic review that can withdraw an additive, lower its dose or restrict its uses when new evidence appears. The list changes, and that is precisely the point of having one.

## Natural antioxidants are E numbers too

Ascorbic acid, citric acid and tocopherols appear on labels with their E number. A juice with E300 does not carry a mysterious preservative: it carries added vitamin C so it does not oxidise.

In practice, an E number tells you the compound and its evaluation, not its origin. A natural additive can have adverse effects and a synthetic one can be safe at the authorised dose.

## Reading a label without alarm

The order of the ingredient list shows quantity: the first item weighs more than the second. If an antioxidant appears last, its presence is small. That reading gives you more than hunting for loose codes.

The whole product matters too. Something with refined oil, sugar and salt may be less interesting than another with the same additives and a better base. The guide to [light products](/en/light-products-guide/) works through that comparison.

## What to do in practice

The base of the diet is still minimally processed food, and additives fill a small part of the total. There is no need to memorise the E300 to E321 series or to avoid every code on a packet.
