The squat is performed with a bar on your back: you bend your knees and descend until your thighs are parallel to the floor. Few exercises have raised as much debate about whether it damages the knees or the lower back.

## Technique decides safety

Any strength exercise done badly can cause harm. The cause may be excessive volume, excessive weight or poor technique. Technique is therefore the first safety factor, ahead of load.

## What the studies say about the knee

Since the 1960s, studies have looked at athletes, animals and people in rehabilitation. One study measured the anterior-posterior displacement of the tibia relative to the femur in subjects who squatted at different depths for eight weeks. Data was also collected from weightlifters, powerlifters and age-matched controls.

Over those eight weeks, instability did not increase with squat depth. In other words, the squat did not negatively affect knee stability.

Using the same instrument, another group found that acute bouts or peaks of exercise across a range of activities, including squats, reduced knee stability. The possible cause was muscle fatigue or elevated body temperature. The conclusion is that acute instability is not necessarily caused by one specific exercise, since many exercises can produce it through other factors.

In animals, resistance exercise increased the strength of the ligament-to-bone junction, as well as ligament diameter and collagen content. Bone and ligament preparations withstood more maximum load at high speeds.

In athletes rehabilitating damaged knees, closed-chain exercises such as the squat are used. The muscles of the femur contract together with other leg muscles, which increases knee stability and puts less stress on the anterior cruciate ligament.

Some recovery phases require avoiding the squat and other exercises that load the knee. Once soft tissue has healed, the exercise is chosen for its ability to build strength and protect the joint.

## Stress on the lower back

If the lift is done badly, forces on the lower back can be intense. The most common errors are lifting excessive weights and leaning too far forward, so the weight is lifted with the back instead of the legs and hips.

A bar on the upper back increases compressive forces on the spine. Keeping a straight posture spreads those forces and reduces the risk. In half squats with a loaded bar, lumbar forces have been estimated at 6 to 10 times body weight.

Reducing compression and shear forces requires flexibility in the knee, hip and spine. Abdominal strength also protects the back. During a lift with excessive weight, holding your breath through the effort raises intra-abdominal pressure and helps stabilise the spine. A belt may be advisable on a maximum rep.

Stress fractures of the vertebrae, such as spondylolysis, and the forward slip of one vertebra over another, spondylolisthesis, can appear in athletes. It is hard to determine whether strength training causes them. Keeping the trunk muscles strong is essential to protect the spine during the squat.

Squat programmes should be modified for athletes with back problems. Low back pain is a common complaint, and strains and sprains are more likely with sudden extension and rotation. A well-executed squat does not fall into that category.

In a study of weightlifters, the incidence of back pain was relatively low: 8 out of 80. Spinal flexibility, lifting with a straight back and strong paraspinal muscles protect the lifter. In that sample, low back pain was less frequent than in the general population.

## When the squat is not for you

Athletes arrive with different strength levels and, in some cases, previous injuries. All of these factors belong in the programme design.

The recommended depth is the point where the thighs are parallel to the floor. With sound technique and steady progression, some athletes can go lower. The value of the deep squat for athletic performance is still debated; in general it is recommended only if the sport requires practising it, and depth should increase gradually.

Training under extreme fatigue or to failure on squats can cost you control of technique, weight and posture. A knee that rotates under load raises the risk of meniscus damage.

You also need enough recovery between sessions. Weight and reps are adjusted to each athlete's capacity.

> The squat is safe for people with no history of knee injury, as long as they respect technique and progression. With previous damage, pregnancy or persistent pain, check with a doctor before starting.

## How to programme the squat

Teaching correct technique through a qualified coach is probably the most important point in squat safety. From there, progression leads.

Increase weight and depth gradually and do not exceed your body's capacity to adapt. Back pain, knee pain and other signs of overtraining are warning signals.

Fatigue is a risk factor. When technique breaks down, the set is over.

## Correct technique in twelve points

1. Set your feet about shoulder-width apart.
2. Descend in a controlled way.
3. Rise at whatever speed you choose, but always under control.
4. Exhale after the hardest point of the ascent.
5. Avoid bouncing or twisting on the way down.
6. Keep your spine straight.
7. Descend only until your thighs are parallel to the floor.
8. Keep your feet flat and fully planted.
9. As a general rule, keep your knees from passing your toes.
10. Progress weight and depth gradually, without exceeding your capacity to adapt. Back or knee pain tells you the pace is too fast.
11. Treat fatigue as a risk factor.
12. Keep your technique or stop the exercise.

## Conclusion

The squat matters because it is functional and resembles many sporting movements. Follow the right technique and guidelines, and it is a safe exercise for people with no history of knee damage.

It is a multi-joint exercise that uses large muscles and has strong potential for adding lean mass when prescribed well. It trains the legs and the back, among other areas. People with previous injuries or special considerations, such as pregnant women, should consult a doctor before starting any programme.
