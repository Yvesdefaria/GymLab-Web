Niacin is an umbrella name. It covers nicotinic acid and nicotinamide, two similar molecules the body uses to build NAD and NADP. Those coenzymes take part in the oxidation and reduction reactions that extract energy from carbohydrates, fats and protein.

## The two forms of vitamin B3

Nicotinic acid and nicotinamide reach the same metabolic destination. The difference appears at high doses: the acid produces intense skin flushing and nicotinamide does not. That distinction explains why the industry picked one form for supplements and pharmacy picked the other to treat cholesterol.

NAD also serves as a substrate for enzymes that regulate cellular stress and the circadian rhythm. The products built on NAD precursors come from there; they are a different thing and are not sold as niacin.

## How much niacin you need

References are written in niacin equivalents (NE), because the body makes part of it from tryptophan: 60 milligrams of that amino acid count as 1 milligram of niacin.

## Pellagra, a twentieth-century disease

Niacin deficiency causes pellagra: dermatitis on skin exposed to the sun, diarrhoea, dementia and, without treatment, death. The three Ds of its symptoms gave it the name.

Pellagra caused epidemics in the southern United States and in parts of Europe during the first third of the twentieth century. Joseph Goldberger showed in 1915 that the cause was diet and not a microbe, and in 1937 niacin was identified as the missing nutrient. Populations eating maize without the traditional alkaline treatment, or sorghum as almost their only staple, were the hardest hit. Nixtamalisation, which uses lime, releases bound niacin and explains why traditional tortillas did not cause the disease.

Today it appears in chronic alcohol use, malabsorption and monotonous diets.

## Food niacin and pharmacy nicotinic acid

A steak and a handful of peanuts supply around 12 milligrams of niacin. The nicotinic acid prescribed for cholesterol is dosed in grams: between 1 and 2 grams a day, sometimes 3. The difference is two orders of magnitude.

At that drug dose, nicotinic acid lowers LDL, raises HDL and reduces triglycerides. The trials that tested it alongside a statin found no extra cardiovascular benefit and more adverse events: flushing, itching, liver toxicity, higher glucose and higher uric acid. That is why it is a prescription medicine with periodic blood tests, not a supplement for lowering cholesterol on your own.

Flushing appears minutes after taking nicotinic acid and lasts half an hour. It is a prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation, uncomfortable and not dangerous. Nicotinamide does not produce it, which is why it goes into supplements, although at very high doses it can also damage the liver.

## The 35 mg limit and the flush

The upper limit exists to avoid flushing and digestive discomfort from synthetic forms. At 35 milligrams a day most people notice nothing; above it, flushing becomes common. Reaching that figure with natural food in a single sitting is hard.

A typical B complex carries between 15 and 50 milligrams of niacin. The 50 mg version exceeds the limit and adds nothing for anyone eating meat, fish or whole grains regularly.

## Niacin and training

Niacin takes part in energy metabolism, and the promise of more energy or better performance grows from there. Trials do not support it: covering the reference lets the coenzymes work, and supplementing above it adds no strength or endurance.

NAD precursors such as nicotinamide riboside are sold for cellular energy and ageing. They are different molecules, with small studies and inconsistent results. [Vitamin B6](/en/vitamin-b6/) shares the undeserved reputation of a performance booster with B3, and the review of [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) helps separate what fills a gap from what only fills a jar.
