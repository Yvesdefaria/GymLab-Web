## Two machines under one name

The first family has two padded arms. You rest the inside of the forearm and the elbow on the pad and push with the elbow, not the hand. The second family has two handles, vertical or horizontal, and you push with the hands like a seated crossover.

The difference changes the exercise. On an elbow-pad machine the arm stays fixed and the movement comes from the shoulder. On a handle machine the hand can pull on the grip and the elbow moves a little more. Change machines while keeping the same setup and the exercise changes without telling you.

## Setting the seat

Sit with your back against the pad and both feet flat on the floor. Set the seat so the upper arm lines up with the shoulders or slightly below. That reference works for both families.

On an elbow-pad machine, the elbow sits at shoulder height with the forearm resting on the pad. On a handle machine, the hands sit at chest height or a little below, elbows bent and shoulders down.

If the seat is too low, the arms start above shoulder line and the range shortens. If it is too high, the arms start below and the shoulder opens more than it needs to. Try both heights with a light weight before choosing.

## The path and the hold

Bring the arms together in front of the chest until the pads or the hands meet. Hold the position for one or two seconds, without relaxing the back or rolling the shoulders forward.

Return slowly to the open position without letting the weight stack rest. That rest breaks tension exactly when the chest is most stretched. The return trip is half the exercise.

Three sets of 10 to 15 reps with 60 or 90 seconds of rest. The load is chosen so the hold completes on every rep and the back stays against the pad. If the machine lets you adjust the range, start in the middle and widen it once the hold comes out clean across all sets.

## The shoulder caveat

On a pec deck, the arm travels from external to internal rotation while the machine applies resistance. That combination loads the front of the shoulder, and the range of motion decides how much. The machine does not forgive a badly chosen range, because the stops mark the end of the path without asking.

Two adjustments cut that load without removing the chest stimulus. The first is seat height: the arms should not start above shoulder line. The second is range: there is no need to open all the way to the machine stop.

If you feel a pinch at the front of the shoulder as you push, shorten the range or switch to a [dumbbell fly](/en/flat-dumbbell-fly/) or a [cable crossover](/en/high-cable-crossover/). Both let you adjust the angle without the machine's fixed path.

## Mistakes that show up in the first set

- **Seat too low.** The arms start above the shoulder and the range shortens.
- **Opening to the machine stop.** The shoulder takes more tension than it needs.
- **Pushing with the hands on an elbow-pad machine.** The elbow leaves the pad and the arm loses its lever.
- **Letting the stack drop.** The return is lost exactly where it does the most.
- **Lifting the back off the pad.** The torso moves forward and the work is shared differently.
