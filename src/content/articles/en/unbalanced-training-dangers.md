Symmetry and balance across every muscle may not be your goal when you train, but they are the cheapest way to avoid a good share of gym injuries.

Two kinds of bodybuilder share every gym floor. There is the one who trains each muscle group chasing symmetry and proportion, and the one who wants an enormous chest and arms with hard abs to look good with a shirt off. The second type neglects everything that does not show in the photo, and eventually pays for it.

Explaining to the average gym user, whose main goal is to impress someone, that training for a balanced physique is not just about appearance usually falls on deaf ears. The short-term logic is sound: the muscles you can see give visible, fast results. The problem arrives after years of training only those groups, when injury risk multiplies.

Nobody will ever tell you "wow, your rotator cuff is monstrously strong" or "the size of your rear delts and rhomboids is impressive". You know that already. But in the long run those areas are as critical as the pectorals and the arms.

## What a balanced training programme means

Balanced training means working every body part equally, or more intensely if it is already lagging, so that they develop in proportional size and strength.

Instead of endlessly loading the big, strong areas to make them even bigger, a balanced approach focuses on the weakest ones. The aim is to keep proportional weaknesses to a minimum.

Training for symmetry and proportion creates the illusion of a bigger physique, and it also keeps you away from injury. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, so training the neglected groups raises your strength levels in every exercise.

## The great forgotten: the rotator cuff

It is not unusual for bodybuilders to ignore small groups completely, such as the internal and external rotators of the rotator cuff around the scapula that stabilises the shoulder.

The four relatively small rotator muscles are critical for seating the head of the humerus in the shoulder capsule and preventing impingement and dislocation of other important structures in that joint.

Because the rotators work together with the rest of the shoulder musculature, when you build bigger, stronger delts without a matching increase in the strength and size of the cuff, those muscles stop working well together. Injury risk rises.

Worse still is the bodybuilder who neglects a large group entirely, such as the quadriceps.

## How to fix imbalances

Many bodybuilders try to train for balance by adding every group to their routine, but they still do not give enough attention to the commonly undervalued ones. The numbers give them away. With 16 sets for chest and only 12 for back, a significant imbalance arrives over time. Adding rotator cuff exercises two or three days a week, for example on shoulder day, chest day and back day, creates more balance than training them once a week.

The list below gathers the muscles that usually get left out, the postural changes you can see and the injuries an unbalanced physique can bring, along with the exercises to train them.

### Rhomboids and middle trapezius

- **Visible changes:** rounded shoulders, hunched forward.
- **Possible injuries:** shoulder impingement, rotator cuff tear, rotator cuff tendinitis, tendinitis of the long head of the biceps.
- **Exercises:** rows and pulldowns with an emphasis on squeezing the shoulder blades together. The rear delts need training too.

### Lower trapezius

- **Visible changes:** lack of development in the lower trapezius, which sits between the mid-back and the lower inner portion of the shoulder blades.
- **Possible injuries:** shoulder impingement, rotator cuff tear, rotator cuff tendinitis, tendinitis of the long head of the biceps.
- **Exercises:** lie on an incline bench, form a V by raising straight arms from the floor to shoulder height and keep the elbows locked.

### Rotator cuff: the subscapularis

- **Visible changes:** none.
- **Possible injuries:** shoulder impingement, rotator cuff tear, rotator cuff tendinitis.
- **Exercises:** the various rotator cuff exercises, with controlled internal rotation and a light weight.

### Rear deltoid

- **Visible changes:** rounded shoulders, hunched forward.
- **Possible injuries:** none.
- **Exercises:** reverse flyes with dumbbells or on the pec-deck machine, keeping the shoulders and elbows raised perpendicular to the body.

### Hamstrings

- **Visible changes:** a more pronounced lordotic curve than normal in the lower back.
- **Possible injuries:** hamstring strain.
- **Exercises:** Romanian deadlift, lying, seated or single-leg standing leg curl, and the glute-ham raise machine.

### Multifidus (deep back muscles)

- **Visible changes:** none.
- **Possible injuries:** lower back strain.
- **Exercises:** back extensions, back extensions with rotation and deadlifts.

These are only a few examples. The body has a large number of muscles, and if you do not vary your training enough, imbalances appear easily.

## In short

A balanced physique is the cheapest way to avoid injury, and an aesthetic question too. Train the groups you cannot see as seriously as the chest and arms, check the ratio between sets and add rotator work two or three times a week.

Training only what you can see may give you the immediate results you want, but it will not do you good if you keep it up for years.
