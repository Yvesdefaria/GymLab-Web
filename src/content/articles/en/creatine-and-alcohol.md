The idea that alcohol cancels creatine is repeated as if a study sat behind it. There is none. Nor is there a trial showing that creatine protects against alcohol. What exists is solid information on how alcohol affects training, and that is enough to decide.

## There is no interaction to manage

Creatine is stored in muscle and takes part in rapid energy production. Alcohol is metabolised in the liver and affects the central nervous system. The body handles each one separately.

No study has found that alcohol reduces creatine uptake by muscle, or that creatine worsens intoxication. If you drink occasionally and take 3 to 5 grams a day, there is no timing gap to respect and no dose to change.

The claim that the benefits will not appear if you drink is false. What happens is simpler. If you drink heavily and sleep badly, training suffers, and creatine cannot compensate for a session that never recovers.

## What alcohol does cost

Sleep is the biggest bill. Alcohol shortens the time it takes to fall asleep and, in exchange, fragments the second half of the night and reduces deep sleep. That stage supports muscle recovery, so a night of drinking leaves the next day's session with less room.

Protein synthesis also suffers at high doses. In a study with young men, a large alcohol intake after training reduced the protein synthesis response in the following hours. The fall was clear at a high amount, not with a glass of wine at dinner.

Calories are the third cost. Alcohol supplies about 7 kilocalories per gram, close to fat, and does not fill you up. A long night can add more calories than a whole day of surplus, and none of them brings protein or micronutrients.

The fourth cost is hydration. Alcohol suppresses the hormone that retains fluid and increases urine output, so a night of drinking leaves the body drier than it feels. The next day's session starts with less fluid and worse temperature control.

## How much is little

The low-risk reference in Spain is two units of alcohol a day for men and one for women, with a weekly maximum of fourteen and nine respectively. One unit is 10 grams of pure alcohol, the equivalent of a small beer or a small glass of wine.

That limit is not a recommendation to drink. The World Health Organization holds that no level of alcohol is safe for health, and the limit serves to order consumption, not to justify it.

For training, the practical difference is frequency and amount. One beer with dinner changes nothing; four drinks on a Friday and a Saturday morning session are a different story.

## The practical rule

Do not train drunk. Injury risk with free weights rises, technique goes, and effort perception is distorted.

Do not drink to recover. Alcohol does not rehydrate or restore glycogen, and the sleep it brings is worse than the sleep it takes. If you are going to drink, alternate with water and eat something with protein first.

Keep creatine at 3 to 5 grams a day, including the days you drink. There is no reason to stop it or to double it.

## Who should avoid it

Alcohol is contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding, in people with liver disease and in anyone taking medication that interacts with it. Minors and drivers have their own legal prohibition.

If drinking has become hard to control, the conversation belongs with a health professional, not with a supplement guide.
