Knowing what muscle is made of helps explain why it responds one way to heavy sets and another to long sets. Muscle fiber types shape the strength, endurance and growth potential of each muscle.

## What a muscle fiber is

Muscle fibers are the cells that make up muscles, built in turn from contractile units called myofibrils.

There are three types of muscle in the body. Two are involuntary, such as the muscles that run the heart or the internal organs. The third is skeletal muscle, the one that matters in training.

Skeletal muscles are voluntary, because you can contract them at will. They attach to bones through tendons, so contraction produces movement or maintains posture. Skeletal muscle is estimated to make up 40% to 50% of total body mass in an adult man.

## Muscle tension and motor units

Muscle contracts in response to a suitable nerve impulse. Each motor unit is made of a nerve and the cluster of cells it supplies, and can produce tension on its own. Moving a limb, however, requires enough individual fibers to develop the right tension. Activating a single motor unit cannot do it.

Producing enough tension to move a bone means increasing the firing rate of each motor unit, which is not very efficient, or increasing the number of motor units involved.

The all-or-nothing law holds in muscle: when an individual motor unit activates its group of fibers, all of them contract to their maximum potential. A fiber either gives everything or does nothing. The brain controls both functions to raise tension with precision and produce coordinated contractions. If it did not, arms, legs and the rest of the body would move in unthinkable directions.

Muscle tension appears to be directly related to the size of the muscle cell and to the cross-sectional area of the muscle.

### Fast and slow motor units

Like cars, muscles have fast and slow motor units of different sizes.

Small motor units activate at levels requiring up to 50% of the maximum isometric tension the muscle can generate. They reach their maximum firing rate quickly and can hold it for appreciable periods.

Large motor units activate as the threshold needed to produce tension exceeds 20% of the maximum possible. Low levels of tension over long periods depend on small units, while high levels rely on large ones.

Size-based recruitment does not always happen that way: in some explosive movements, fast motor units can be recruited first.

## Type I fibers

There are basically two large groups of skeletal muscle fibers: type I fibers, also called slow-twitch or red fibers, and type II fibers, which are fast-twitch or white fibers. Within type II are type IIa and type IIb fibers.

Type I fibers rely on cellular respiration and use large amounts of oxygen. As a result, they have great fatigue resistance but produce less force because their contractions are slower. Their growth potential for hypertrophy is low.

Because of those traits, they carry the effort in long-duration activities such as distance running and marathons.

## Type IIa fibers

Type IIa fibers are fast oxidative fibers. Because they also use oxygen, they resist fatigue, though much less than type I fibers. In exchange, their contraction power is greater.

They are an intermediate type: they resist fatigue longer than IIb fibers and produce more force than type I fibers.

## Type IIb fibers

Type IIb fibers have the fastest contraction and therefore the most power. In intense exercise, such as lifting weights, they are the key fibers.

They also have the greatest growth potential, and developing them makes muscles look bigger. In exchange, they fatigue first: they can produce greater efforts, but for less time.

## How fibers are recruited

The body works on economy. When you do any physical activity, the first fibers used are type I. If they are not enough because more force is needed, the body recruits type IIa and finally type IIb.

Suppose you can lift 50 kilos:

- With a 2-kilo load, only some type I fibers are used.
- With a load around 50%, about 25 kilos, all type I fibers in the affected muscle are used, along with some type IIa.
- With a 100% load, 50 kilos, all type I fibers are used, as well as type IIa and type IIb.

That order explains how muscle fiber types are recruited according to the load. You can also recruit more fast fibers with a lighter weight if you move it as fast as possible. Moving it faster requires more power, and the faster fibers come into play.

The muscle does not really know the weight on the bar, but it understands the effort needed to lift it. If that effort is not intense enough, type IIa and IIb fibers are not fully activated and you do not benefit from their growth. Whether you lift a heavy load close to your 1RM or a lighter load as fast as you can, you need to activate those fibers for complete development.

## How to train for your fiber type

Gaining muscle means hypertrophying the muscles, and there are two types of hypertrophy: sarcomeric and sarcoplasmic.

Many people stall because they do not combine both. They either train sarcomeric hypertrophy for a long time or stay with sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, and without balance they never reach maximum growth. A volume programme favours sarcoplasmic hypertrophy; a strength programme favours sarcomeric hypertrophy.

### Sarcomeric and sarcoplasmic hypertrophy

The sarcoplasmic part grows with volume routines: many people run volume routines almost all their lives and, of course, grow. The sarcomeric part grows with strength routines, and it grows too, though less than the sarcoplasmic part.

Train both. If you run strength routines you get stronger, lift more weight and, when you move to the volume phase, you can move more load, break more fibers and grow more.

### How to combine strength and volume

Periodise your training. The order of the phases matters because muscle fiber types adapt to different stimuli. If you train strength for at least six weeks, the number of sarcomeres increases; if you follow with six weeks of increasing volume, the size of the sarcomeres increases. The result is a much greater gain in size than training only volume or only strength.

The simplest approach is six weeks of strength and then six weeks of volume. To set the sets and reps for each phase, read the guide to the [optimal rep range](/en/optimal-rep-range/). If you work close to the limit, the guide to [muscle failure](/en/muscle-failure-training/) helps you control the effort, and the one on [training volume](/en/training-volume/) tells you how much work to spread across each phase. If your goal is power, [ballistic training](/en/ballistic-training/) makes use of the fast contraction of type II fibers.
