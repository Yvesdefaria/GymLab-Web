The link exists, and it is worth measuring before stretching it. Trials in healthy men show small changes when dietary fat drops a long way. The effect depends on energy status, genetics and how much you eat in total. No food works as hormone therapy.

## Cholesterol is raw material, not a lever

The liver makes cholesterol and uses it, among other things, to produce testosterone. From there comes the idea that eating more cholesterol produces more testosterone, and the step does not hold. The body synthesises most of the cholesterol in the blood, and diet moves that figure little in most people.

What diet does change is the fat you eat and the energy available. In both cases the effect on testosterone is small compared with sleep, a calorie deficit or genetics.

## Very low-fat diets

Trials in young, healthy men who cut fat to 10 or 15 percent of calories show modest drops in total testosterone, in the region of 10 to 15 percent. The changes are real, and they usually reverse when fat goes back up.

They deserve a careful read. Samples are small, the diets last a few weeks, and many did not measure symptoms or strength. Fifteen percent of calories is a sensible floor for someone who trains, not a magic border: below that it gets harder to cover essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins.

The often-cited study of 27 men with obesity found something else. On a low-fat, high-fibre diet, insulin fell by nearly 40 percent and SHBG rose by another 40. That protein binds testosterone and reduces the free fraction. The finding is interesting and describes men with obesity in a specific setting, not a lean athlete.

## Energy status matters more

An aggressive calorie deficit lowers testosterone more than any fat adjustment. It works through available energy: when the body detects scarcity, it reduces the output of hormones that support growth and fertility. Sleeping little and training too much push in the same direction.

Obesity is also associated with low testosterone. Fat tissue contains aromatase, the enzyme that converts testosterone into estradiol, and a large fat mass shifts the balance. Losing fat can raise testosterone in that case, and the effect is larger the higher the starting point.

## Genetics and individual variation

Two people on the same diet can have different levels. Genetics explains much of the variation, and so do age, sleep, alcohol and training. That is why no food works as hormone therapy.

Alcohol deserves its own paragraph: it lowers testosterone and worsens sleep quality, two effects that add up. The guide to [trans fats](/en/trans-fats/) and the one to [dietary fats](/en/dietary-fats/) order the rest of the map without turning it into a promise.

## Neither saturated fat nor linseed oil is therapy

Saturated fat is not a hormone stimulant. Raising it changes the lipid profile and does not make up for missing calories. Linseed oil supplies essential fatty acids and has no effect on testosterone that justifies paying for it.

The same goes for supplements sold as boosters. [Tribulus terrestris](/en/tribulus-terrestris/) has not been shown to raise testosterone in healthy men, and the guide to [steroids](/en/steroids/) explains why the pharmacological route is not a dietary solution.

## What to do with the diet

Keep fat between 20 and 35 percent of calories, mostly unsaturated and low in saturated. Cover protein, between 1.6 and 2.2 grams per kilo, and do not cut calories further than the goal requires.

If the goal is to gain muscle, the guide to [building muscle](/en/build-muscle/) sets the context. If it is to get lean, a moderate deficit and patience work better. In both cases, dietary fat supports the plan and does not lead it.

## When to see a doctor

Persistent fatigue, loss of libido and changes in body hair or strength are reasons for a blood test, not for changing the diet blindly. Testosterone measurement has a time of day and conditions, and a doctor needs to interpret it.

Be wary of saliva tests and of plans that promise to double testosterone in weeks. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) covers the most expensive shortcuts.
