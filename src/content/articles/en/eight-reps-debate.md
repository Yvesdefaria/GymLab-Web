Walk into any gym and count the sets you see: there is a good chance most of them sit around 8 reps. The number became the norm before there was any research on muscle fibres, when methods passed by word of mouth and everyone copied whatever worked for the biggest person in the room.

Once something becomes popular, scientists start studying it. Today plenty of studies back up training, but they are built on what works for most of the test subjects, not all of them. Every person has different genetics, and what has been shown to work for the general population may not work, and probably does not work, for every single individual.

For example: if a study on a muscle-building method shows a statistically significant improvement in 70 out of 100 subjects, the researcher can conclude that most people would gain muscle with that method. There is still 30%, close to a third, who got no benefit.

The practical conclusion is that you need to know your body and find out what works for you, because even evidence-backed information does not cover the whole population.

## Why genetics decide your range

Your optimal rep range depends on your genetic make-up and on how much of each fibre type you have. It also depends on how you train to make use of those fibres.

Some fibres come into play in explosive or heavy movements, while others activate during endurance activity. Each of us has a different percentage of each. That is why some people are genetically gifted at lifting very heavy weights or running very fast, while others can swim or run enormous distances.

Someone genetically inclined to powerlifting, who lifts huge amounts but tires quickly, would not benefit from training like a triathlete and would probably never be a good triathlete even if they tried. Conversely, someone inclined to endurance will struggle to gain the mass and power of a good bodybuilder or powerlifter.

Training cannot turn one fibre type into another, but it can change the size of each type. There is selective hypertrophy based on the type of training. If you want to maximise muscle size, focus your work on the fibre type you have most of; even so, you will need to add elements that let the other fibres grow too.

Even with three fibre types, keeping a single rep range across every set does not use your full potential. You need to mix rep numbers to work all your fibres effectively.

## The three types of muscle fibre

### Slow-twitch fibres (type I)

They are characterised by a slow contraction time compared with the other two types and a high resistance to fatigue. That makes them ideal for endurance activities. They have a small diameter, so they will hardly affect muscle size if you have an equal percentage of all three types.

They store very little creatine phosphate and little glycogen, but they are very efficient at storing triglycerides and using them as energy, which makes them ideal for long-distance activities: running, the treadmill or the elliptical. They do not help produce much force, so do not expect powerful, explosive muscles from work built on them. Rep ranges of 12 or more per set are considered to recruit type I fibres.

### Fast-twitch fibres (type II)

Most of the activity in weight training recruits fast-twitch fibres. They have a much faster contraction time than type I, but they need immediate energy and fatigue far sooner. They store large amounts of creatine phosphate and glycogen to produce energy quickly, and they do not hold triglycerides as fuel. They are used in anaerobic activities and burn ATP at a fast rate, which is why creatine is such a popular supplement in bodybuilding.

Type II fibres split into two subcategories: II-A and II-B.

**Type II-A:** moderate resistance to fatigue, halfway between type I and II-B. Their large diameter makes them a good contributor to muscle size. They burn mostly glycogen, though they can oxidise triglycerides to some extent. They are recruited during prolonged, highly demanding anaerobic activity: heavy supersets, drop sets and sets of 10 to 15 reps taken to the limit.

**Type II-B:** they tire quickly and are only good for very short anaerobic activities. They are recruited in high-force movements that last very little time. They produce more power and have a much larger diameter than type I fibres, so bodybuilders and powerlifters benefit more from training II-A and II-B than type I. Like II-A, they store plenty of creatine phosphate and glycogen and barely hold any triglycerides. They are the ones a 50-metre sprinter uses, or a bodybuilder doing sets of 4 to 6 reps; II-A are what an 800-metre runner calls on, or a bodybuilder on a moderately heavy set of 8 to 10 reps.

## How fibres are recruited

During movement, the amount of force a muscle produces depends on the fibre type recruited. When the muscle contracts and stretches quickly, fast-twitch fibres take responsibility for generating force. In isometric contractions, when the muscle is under stress without movement, slow-twitch fibres produce as much force as fast-twitch ones.

To recruit as many fast-twitch fibres as possible, you need to keep the muscle contracting and stretching under large amounts of stress for brief periods. When you start hard reps in the gym, the muscles produce force by recruiting as many fibres as they need. Recruitment is a tightly controlled process, and since our bodies aim to be as efficient as possible, the fibres recruited start with the smallest, type I, and move to the largest, type II-A and II-B in that order, as needed.

The order is almost always that one, whatever the stress. The exception may be an extremely fast stretch, when fast-twitch fibres can activate first. If the training is very low intensity, the muscles may need only type I fibres.

The good news is that the more force the muscles need to generate, the more fibres are recruited. The duration of that need also brings more fibres and more types into play. Heavy weight exercises, or methods such as supersets and drop sets, recruit more of the large-diameter fast-twitch fibres.

## What is your fibre composition?

If you wonder what proportion of fibres you were genetically given, there are both scientific and unofficial tests you can use to find out.

A muscle biopsy can be done at a medical centre to find the exact configuration. If you only want a general idea of where you fit, try the following experiment, which works for muscle groups rather than individual muscles:

> In the gym, work out your one-rep maximum on a compound movement such as the bench press or the squat. With 80% of that maximum, do as many reps as you can. If you get fewer than 7, that muscle group is probably more than 50% fast-twitch fibres. If you can do between 7 and 12, the group probably has a similar proportion of type I and type II fibres.

You cannot train to turn fast-twitch fibres into slow-twitch ones, or the other way round. You are stuck with what you were given. That said, if you train them correctly, II-A fibres can take on the characteristics of II-B and vice versa. It depends on whether you favour very heavy training or moderate-intensity training.

Although weight training does not intrinsically change fibre type, it does change fibre size. Knowing your genetic configuration can help you maximise your body's capacity to grow.

Fast-twitch fibres have a much larger diameter than slow-twitch ones, so even with 50% type I and 50% type II, the fast ones may account for a larger share of total muscle size, say 65%. If you train mostly at high intensity, you can reach a point where 75% or 80% of your muscle volume comes from fast-twitch fibres, even though the proportion between types is equal.

And here comes the bodybuilding caveat. If you start from a slow-to-fast fibre ratio of 50/65, with 65% of overall size coming from fast-twitch fibres, and then focus on training the slow-twitch type, the muscle gains endurance but becomes smaller and weaker for lack of any need to recruit the fast fibres. The size ratio shifts back to 50/50 because the fast fibres atrophy. You need to train every fibre and put the main focus on II-B, the largest in diameter.

## How to activate every fibre type

To guarantee gains in strength and size you have to train with weights heavy enough that you can only manage a low number of reps. That ensures your muscles recruit II-B fibres, the ones that produce force. And since recruitment follows a specific order, reaching II-B means the other two types are called into action as well.

With moderate weights and a higher number of reps, there is no need for II-B fibres to join in, and you lose part of the size their hypertrophy could add on top of what type I and II-A already provide.

## The eight-rep theory

The recommended range for inducing muscle growth sits between 5 and 8 reps at 80% of your one-rep maximum, because II-B fibres are recruited. If you want pure size, studies have shown that moving moderate-to-heavy weights in a range of 6 to 12 reps is ideal. That variety hits all three fibre types at some point.

Always using the same rep scheme makes your body adapt easily and gains fall away. You need to vary not only the resistance but also how long the muscle contracts and how long you rest between sets. That keeps the muscle guessing and recruits more fibres.

Use heavy weights for sets of 5 to 8 reps on compound movements at the start of the session to build the foundation, but also use moderately heavy weights for sets of 6 to 12, plus supersets and drop sets, to get the most from the muscle and recruit and stimulate growth in as many fibres as possible.

The eight-rep theory became best practice because studies showed that, to recruit all three fibre types, including II-B, bodybuilders had to train at 80% of their one-rep maximum for 5 to 8 reps. However, to maximise growth and density you need varied reps, plus variations in rep duration and rest between sets. Every training session should contain sets of pure power and mass, some for endurance and some in between.

Sticking to a single rep range cuts into your ability to build muscle.

## In short

Eight reps work as a starting point for most people because they recruit all three fibre classes, but they are not a law. If your muscle groups have more fast-twitch fibres, heavy work of 5 to 8 reps stimulates them sooner; if they have more slow-twitch fibres, they need longer sets to activate them.
