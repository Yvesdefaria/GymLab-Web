Protein has a reputation for being dangerous. The reputation comes from old diets with very little water and from people with kidney disease who do have to control it. In a healthy adult, the evidence shows no kidney damage from raising protein.

## What the kidney does when you eat more protein

When protein rises, the kidney filters more: glomerular filtration increases, which is a normal adjustment. That response has been read as overload, and in a healthy kidney it is not. Studies in adults with normal kidney function find no loss of function on high-protein diets kept up for months.

The exception matters. Anyone with a diagnosed kidney disease, a single kidney or reduced function should ask their doctor before raising protein, because there the limit is real. The same applies to advanced liver disease. The guide to [creatine and the kidneys](/en/creatine-and-kidneys/) clears up a similar fear.

## Calcium and bone

For years it was said that protein drags calcium out and weakens bone. The relationship does show up in urine, but intestinal calcium absorption also rises, and the final balance does not worsen. In young women with enough calcium, 2.4 grams per kilo for ten days did not affect bone integrity.

Very low protein diets, below 0.8 grams per kilo, are associated with worse bone health. Bone needs protein to build its matrix, and a shortfall is more dangerous than an excess.

## The real cost: displacement, fibre and money

With 200 grams of protein a day, the stomach fills with protein foods and leaves little room for fibre. That is the first cost. High-protein diets tend to fall short on fruit, vegetables and legumes, and that gap has digestive and cardiovascular consequences over the years.

The second cost is money. Animal protein is usually the most expensive part of the plate, and grams above 2.2 per kilo rarely add muscle. The third is variety: a monotonous diet covers protein and fails on micronutrients.

## How much protein is enough

For anyone training strength, the useful range is 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo a day, with a practical ceiling of 2.4. Above that ceiling there is no extra hypertrophy, only extra calories. In 25 to 40 gram servings, the spread covers the day without effort.

Excess protein is stored as fat when total calories exceed expenditure, like the rest of the energy. Outside that case, the body oxidises the amino acids it does not need and removes the nitrogen in urine, a process that needs water. The source also changes digestion: the guide to [casein and whey](/en/casein-and-whey/) explains why milk is absorbed at two different speeds.

## When excess does matter

There are situations where it is worth holding back: diagnosed kidney disease, liver failure, congenital disorders of protein metabolism and some phases of medical treatment. Also when protein replaces complete meals and fibre falls below 25 grams a day.

If the doubt is medical, the answer is a blood test and a consultation, not a forum. For everyone else, a high-protein diet within the range is safe as long as the plate stays varied.

## How to raise it without displacing the rest

Spread protein across three to five meals and leave room for vegetables and fruit in each one. Prioritise eggs, fish, legumes, dairy and lean cuts before powders. Food first and powder second cover the range without filling the plate with protein alone. Drink enough for urine to stay pale, especially if you raise protein a lot and train in the heat.

A shake helps close the gap when food does not get there. The guide on [whether athletes need more protein](/en/do-athletes-need-more-protein/) reviews the evidence, the one on [protein](/en/protein-guide/) sorts the sources and the one on [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) avoids products that promise more than they can deliver.
