The glycaemic index is measured with a portion of food that supplies 50 g of available carbohydrate and compared with pure glucose, set at 100. It is an average of healthy people, not an individual label. Portion and cooking change the result.

## What the glycaemic index measures

The test measures glucose during the two hours after a meal. A low value sits below 55, a medium one between 56 and 69, and a high one at 70 or more. The figure does not include quantity: that is what glycaemic load is for, multiplying the index by the grams of carbohydrate and dividing by 100.

The response depends on what accompanies the plate. Fibre, fat, protein and acid slow stomach emptying. That is why the guide to [combining protein and carbs](/en/protein-and-carbs-together/) matters more than the isolated number.

The values are indicative and vary between tables and cooking methods. They help order decisions, not calculate a dose.

## Cooking and portion change the number

Boiled potato sits at 78 and baked at 85 because it loses water and the starch concentrates. Pasta cooked al dente digests more slowly than overcooked pasta. Canned and home-cooked legumes are close, though cooking time moves the result. A very ripe banana rises more than a green one.

The archive was right about one thing: cooking and the company on the plate matter. Its example of lamb with potatoes, bread and wine against lamb with salad describes two different meals. The guide to [carbs at night](/en/carbs-at-night-and-fat-gain/) dismantles the part that blamed the clock.

## Sheep milk is not lactose-free milk

The archive recommended semi-skimmed sheep milk for people with lactose intolerance, on the idea that it barely contains any. Sheep milk is around 4.5 % lactose, close to cow milk at about 4.8 %. If lactose causes trouble, the options are lactose-free milk, hard cheese and small portions of yogurt, not a change of animal.

The intolerance is to a component, not to the whole food. Dried figs also appeared as a very low-index food, and they sit at 61 with plenty of sugar per 100 g. Two or three figs change the result; a large handful does not.

## Alcohol lowers glucose, it does not raise it

The archive said alcohol temporarily raises glucose and tones the body. The effect is the opposite: the liver stops producing glucose while it metabolises alcohol, and blood glucose can fall, especially on an empty stomach. That does not make it a healthy drink. It supplies 7 kcal per gram, does not satisfy hunger and reduces control over what you eat next.

The split between fermented and distilled drinks is not a glucose rule. Beer adds carbohydrate and wine almost none, but the alcohol is the same. If you drink, do it with food and count the calories like any other.

## Butter and bread

Butter is not an industrial trans fat. It contains saturated fat and a small amount of natural trans fat, which does not behave like the trans fat in old margarines. Most modern margarines carry no industrial trans fat either. The practical advice is to use olive oil as the main fat and keep butter for when it adds flavour.

Bread is not forbidden at lunch or dinner. The archive limited it to breakfast and described bloating when it accompanied fatty meals. That heaviness depends on volume and on each person's digestion. Rye bread fits at lunch if it counts in the daily total. The guide to [healthy diet myths](/en/healthy-diet-myths/) covers the rest of the bans without a basis.

## How to build the day

The archive breakfast holds: wholemeal or rye bread, wholegrain cereal, fruit and protein. The lunch and dinner menus do too, with one condition: the carb portion matches expenditure and the goal. [Guacamole](/en/guacamole-calories/) is another fat that fits on the plate.

Drinks are water, tea, coffee and infusions. A glass of wine fits if it counts in the total. The archive's fat-free menus still work, with olive oil standing in for the missing fat.

## For the athlete

In hypertrophy, protein rises, but carbs remain the fuel for training. Before a long session, a fast-digesting carb such as a [rice cake](/en/rice-cakes/) works; for the rest of the day, legumes and rye bread hold hunger better. The [DASH diet](/en/dash-diet/) guide offers another menu frame with the same principle: foods with fibre and low calorie density.

Rye crispbread fits that scheme as a protein base. Its index is medium and its portion small, and the [crispbread](/en/crispbread/) page explains how many calories each slice adds. A full meal, on the other hand, needs more than a dry sheet.
