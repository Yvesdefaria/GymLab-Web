The scale mixes fat, muscle, glycogen, water and gut contents into a single number. That number can stay the same for weeks while the waist shrinks and training numbers rise. Recomposition works on the ratio.

## What body recomposition is and why the scale misleads

The body is made of fat mass and fat-free mass: muscle, bone, organs and water. Losing weight is simple: you only need to dehydrate. Changing composition is slower and does not always move the needle.

Muscle is denser than fat, so a more muscular body can weigh the same and look more compact. The tape measure, your clothes, photos every four weeks and the kilos you lift tell the story better than weight alone. The guide to [losing fat rather than weight](/en/lose-fat-not-weight/) develops that idea.

## Who can recompose

Recomposition is easier when the body has room to respond. The profiles most likely to succeed are:

- Anyone starting strength training. The stimulus is new and adaptation arrives fast, even on fewer calories.
- Anyone returning after a break. Muscle memory recovers tissue that already existed.
- Anyone carrying more body fat. There are reserves to sustain the deficit without compromising muscle.
- The skinny-fat lifter, with normal weight, little muscle and high fat. They need strength and enough food, not another restrictive diet.

For an advanced trainee the margin is small and the process drags. They are often better served by separate bulking and cutting phases instead of chasing both at once.

## The three rules that decide

### A small deficit, 200 to 300 kcal

Losing fat requires eating less than you spend, and building muscle requires material and energy. A deficit of 200 to 300 kcal a day leaves room for both. Cutting faster increases hunger, worsens training and speeds up muscle loss.

Metabolism does adapt somewhat when you eat less, especially as you lose weight, but the body does not hold on to fat on purpose. The real problem with an aggressive deficit is adherence: it lasts two weeks and then gets abandoned.

### Protein between 1.6 and 2.2 g/kg

In a deficit, protein protects muscle and maintains satiety. The range of 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilo of body weight per day covers most people who train with weights. Eggs, chicken, fish, legumes, tofu and dairy share the total without needing powder.

Distribution matters less than the total. Three or four meals with a serving of protein each work as well as six.

### Progressive strength

Without a strength signal, the body has no reason to keep muscle in a deficit. Three or four weekly sessions with compound exercises and a few isolation movements are enough. The key is overload: one more rep, one more kilo or better technique than last week, with one or two reps in reserve at the end of each set.

## The realistic pace

Strength usually responds within two to four weeks. Visible changes arrive later, from eight to twelve weeks, and depend on the starting point. The scale can sit still for entire fortnights because of water retention, sodium or glycogen, and that does not mean the plan is failing.

If after two months the waist, photos and training numbers have not moved, adjust one variable: slightly less food or a few more steps a day. Changing everything at once makes it impossible to know what worked.

## Signs that it is working

Clothes are the first sign. Trousers that are tight at the waist and unchanged in the thighs suggest fat is falling while muscle holds. Training numbers rise while the waist shrinks, and energy lasts through the day without crashes.

## When to stop recomposing

Recomposition is not a permanent state. If strength falls for three weeks in a row, sleep breaks down or hunger dominates decisions, the deficit is too large or the timing is wrong. Maintaining weight for a while or choosing a clear bulk or cut phase makes more sense then.

## Optional supplements

Creatine monohydrate, 3 to 5 grams a day, has the best support for strength. Protein powder solves the total when food falls short. Caffeine helps on hard sessions.
