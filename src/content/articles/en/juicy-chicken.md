Chicken is the most repeated protein in training diets for price, versatility and neutral flavour. It is also the one that dries out most, because breast has little fat and plenty of water. The difference between a juicy piece and a shoe sole comes down to two numbers: temperature and time.

## Why chicken breast dries out

White meat has little fat and a high percentage of water. As it heats, muscle fibres contract and push liquid out. The longer and hotter the piece cooks, the more water leaves and the drier it gets.

The right point is in the centre of the meat, not on the surface. A piece golden outside can be raw inside, and one juicy outside can be overcooked.

## 74 °C in the centre

The safety reference for chicken is 74 °C in the thickest part. At that temperature the bacteria that matter most in poultry, such as Salmonella and Campylobacter, are inactivated.

The thermometer goes into the centre of the piece, without touching bone or pan. In a breast, the coldest point is the thick end. If it comes off at 74 °C and rests for five minutes, carryover heat finishes the job without drying it.

## Brine: one teaspoon per cup

A brine works two ways: salt dissolves part of the surface protein and water gets into the meat. The result is a juicier piece, seasoned on the inside.

The basic ratio is one teaspoon of salt per cup of water, about 240 millilitres. Two breasts need about four cups of water and four teaspoons of salt. Dissolve it well before submerging the meat.

A thin breast needs 30 minutes to 2 hours. A whole piece or a whole chicken needs 4 to 12 hours in the fridge. Going over the time leaves the meat soft and salty.

Pepper, garlic, bay or lemon peel can go in. Acid softens the surface, and a whole lemon over hours leaves the breast with a pasty texture.

## Technique: thickness, heat and rest

Three details change the result more than the recipe. The first is thickness: a thick breast is flattened by hand or split in two, so the whole piece reaches the same temperature at once.

The second is heat: a hot pan, a little oil and the piece undisturbed for two or three minutes. The crust forms and the meat releases from the pan on its own.

The third is rest. Five minutes on a plate, loosely covered with foil, is enough for the juices to spread.

In a pan, a two-centimetre fillet needs about four minutes per side. In the oven, 200 °C for 20 or 25 minutes for a whole breast. The thermometer decides, not the clock.

## Storage and reheating

Cooked chicken keeps in the fridge for three days at most and in the freezer for up to three months. Before storing it, cool it fast: separate it into pieces, spread it in shallow containers and put it in the fridge within two hours.

The cooked rice served with it also cools fast. Leaving it out all afternoon at room temperature is the scenario that causes most food poisoning at home.

When reheating, the internal temperature returns to 74 °C. In the microwave, covering the plate and adding a spoonful of stock or water keeps the texture. A thawed chicken is not refrozen raw; once cooked, it can be frozen.

## How much protein it provides

Two hundred grams of raw breast provides about 48 grams of protein, 225 kcal and just over 2 grams of fat. It is one of the cheapest ways to cover the daily protein target.

Skin adds fat: a breast with skin can double the total. For a lighter meal, remove it before serving and use it to add flavour during cooking.

## Cooking in batches

Roasting four breasts at once saves time and avoids improvising. Store them in airtight containers, label them with the date and use them in salad, with rice or sautéed with vegetables.
