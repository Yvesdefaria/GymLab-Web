It is a rare exercise and that is why it costs. Most programmes train the trunk in the vertical plane, with crunches, or in the anteroposterior plane, with planks and rows. The lateral plane gets far less, and you notice it as soon as you have to hold a load with one arm.

## What the lateral plane trains

The main player is the **quadratus lumborum**, the muscle that links the crest of the pelvis to the last rib and to the spine. It is the trunk's main lateral flexor: when it contracts on one side, it bends the spine towards that side and keeps the pelvis level.

Next to it work the internal and external obliques on the side that moves, the rectus abdominis and the spinal erectors, which hold the spine while it changes orientation. The gluteus medius and the rest of the lateral chain follow the movement without leading it.

That group has a concrete job: braking the tilt when something pulls you sideways. Carrying a box with one arm, holding a shopping bag or keeping your balance with a weight in one hand depends on it. Training it through a range is the most direct way to give it work.

## What hanging from the bar adds

The start position is already part of the exercise. Hanging from a high bar pulls the shoulders up and stretches the spine without compression. At the end of a loaded session, that decompression in the shoulders and the back is welcome, and the grip gets an extra stimulus that no crunch reproduces.

Do not look for anything more in the stretch: the benefit is in hanging with straight arms and letting the body fall. If the shoulders complain, the bar is too high or the grip cannot take the weight; in that case, build the grip first and come back later.

## Starting position

- High bar, overhand closed grip, hands slightly wider than the shoulders.
- The bar has to let the legs stretch fully without touching the floor.
- Rigid arms, without bending the elbows during the set.
- Straight legs, with a slight knee and hip bend, locked in that position.
- Take more air than usual and hold it as you start to rise. Let it out on the way down.

## Execution

From the hang, contract the side you are going to move hard to start the movement. The pelvis and the legs rise together towards that side, as one piece, and the waist acts as the pivot.

Keep the legs in the lateral plane for the whole path. Rise as far as you can, pause for a moment at the top and come down under control to the hang. Pause briefly and repeat towards the other side.

The order matters: the pelvis lifts first on the side that moves, and then the muscles turn the pelvis together with the legs. Those are the two actions of the exercise, and both are lost when the body swings.

## The 30 to 45 degree ceiling

The normal range is not large. For most people the maximum sits between 30 and 45 degrees from vertical. That margin changes with strength and with flexibility.

If the movement passes 45 degrees easily, it has most likely turned into something else: a hip turn, a swing or a front raise in disguise. The useful range ends where the flank stops leading.

## Do not pull with the arm

The most common error is pulling the arm towards the side you are moving to. That diverts the line of pull and spreads the work between the lat and the shoulder, which is exactly what the exercise tries to avoid.

You can use the shoulder without bending the elbow to give a small initial push, as long as the flank still leads. If you notice the arm doing half the path, release the shoulder tension and start again from the hang.

## Do not swing

Swinging from side to side lets you start the legs without a strong contraction, and that contraction is the exercise. A swing is easy to spot: the pelvis shoots up, the legs arrive at the top without effort and the body comes back on its own.

The clean version is slower and shorter. Rise, hold, lower and stop. If you need momentum to pass 20 degrees, the set is already over.

## The prerequisite: hamstring flexibility

Before hanging, you should be able to hinge from the hips with an arched back down to horizontal. That test measures hamstring flexibility, which is what limits the range when the legs travel straight.

If you cannot get there, work on the hamstring stretch and use the bent-knee variation in the meantime. The side raise with bent legs keeps the plane and lowers the flexibility demand.

## Load and programming

The weight of the legs and the pelvis is usually enough. When the exercise comes out easy and clean, add weight at the ankles, little by little, and check that the range does not shorten.

Two or three sets of 5 to 8 reps per side are enough: this is a demanding movement and it gains nothing from high volume. Put it at the end of the abs session or after a heavy day, when the decompression from the bar adds up.

Once or twice a week keeps the lateral plane current. The [hanging ab exercises](/en/hanging-ab-exercises/) page gathers the whole bar family, and [hanging leg raises](/en/hanging-leg-raises/) are the front version that builds the base before you add the lateral component.

Light loaded rotation through a short range is not the problem; the risk shows up with heavy loads, a fixed pelvis and a long lever at the end of the range.
