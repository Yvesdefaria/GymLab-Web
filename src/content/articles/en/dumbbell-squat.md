There are two common ways to squat with dumbbells: one in each hand at the sides, or a single one held in front of the chest. The second is the goblet squat and the one this page argues for. It is also the most useful way to learn the pattern before moving to the bar, alongside the [Zercher squat](/en/zercher-squat/) and the [Bulgarian split squat](/en/bulgarian-split-squat/).

## The goblet hold

The dumbbell is held with both hands under the head, elbows in and the weight against the chest at sternum height. The forearms stay almost vertical and the shoulders stay down.

That hold puts the load in front of the centre of gravity. The torso has to stay upright to keep balance, and the quadriceps gets more work than with dumbbells at the sides. It also keeps the arms out of the range, so the squat does not depend on shoulder strength.

## Stance and descent

The feet go at shoulder width, toes slightly out. The weight spreads across the whole foot, heel down from the start. Keep the ribs down so the dumbbell does not pull the torso into an arch. The same hold works with a kettlebell.

Lower by bending hip and knee together, with the dumbbell against the chest and the elbows in. The descent ends when the thighs approach parallel. Pause there for one second without bouncing, then stand by pushing through the whole foot.

The knee travels forward over the line of the foot. What must not happen is the knee collapsing inward or the heel lifting. The [bodyweight squat](/en/bodyweight-squat/) covers the same pattern without load and is a good way to check the range.

## The heel that lifts

When the heel comes up, the problem is almost never depth: it is the ankle. Limited dorsiflexion stops the shin from travelling over the foot, and the body compensates by lifting the heel to reach the bottom. Cutting the descent short does not fix the limitation, it only hides it.

There are three solutions and none requires giving up parallel. The first is a wedge or a small plate under the heels, which returns a few degrees of ankle. The second is a slightly wider stance with the toes turned out more, which spreads the demand. The third is specific ankle work outside the set. The [squat safety guide](/en/squat-safety/) develops the general framework.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting the dumbbell drift from the chest.** The further it goes, the more load on the back and the less control of the torso.
- **Flaring the elbows.** The hold breaks and the dumbbell moves away from the sternum.
- **Letting the knees cave.** The knee follows the line of the foot, especially on the way up.
- **Bouncing at the bottom.** The one-second pause is part of the technique and gets timed.
- **Staying light forever.** The dumbbell allows progression, but only if the load goes up when the range is clean.

## Sets, reps and rest

Three sets of eight to twelve reps, with ninety seconds of rest. That is a dose for size and for learning: it allows reps to accumulate without the position breaking.

Progression follows an order. First earn the full range with the current dumbbell, then add a two-second pause at the bottom, and only then add weight. [Progressive overload](/en/progressive-overload-principle/) explains why the order matters.

## Where it fits

The goblet squat works as a leg-day warm-up, as the main exercise at home and as an accessory after the bar. The [home dumbbell routines](/en/home-dumbbell-routines/) already place it in the week and explain how to chain it with the rest of the plan.

To change the stimulus, the [dumbbell sumo squat](/en/dumbbell-sumo-squat/) opens the stance and the [overhead dumbbell squat](/en/overhead-dumbbell-squat/) adds shoulder and trunk demand. The [barbell squat](/en/barbell-squat/) is the next step when the dumbbell load runs short, and the [leg exercise catalogue](/en/leg-exercises/) orders the other variants.
