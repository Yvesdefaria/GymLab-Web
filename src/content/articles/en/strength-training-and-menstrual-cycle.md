The same table every month works for some people and falls short for others. Strength is not a straight line and the cycle adds one more variable, small for some and huge for others. This guide separates what the evidence supports from what gets repeated without checking.

The days are the average of a 28-day cycle and yours may move. Research on strength and cycle phase is small and uneven: some studies find differences and others do not. What does repeat is that variation between people exceeds variation between phases. The map is for observing yourself, not for programming by decree.

## Symptoms are the compass, not the calendar

Keep the exercises and adjust the load or the volume. If your 3x8 at 60 kilos usually ends with two reps in reserve and today ends at RIR 0, drop to 55 kilos or remove one set. The stimulus still arrives without piling on extra fatigue.

That adjustment is called autoregulation and it works better than a fixed plan by phase. In practice: same table, same order, different load. If your cycle is irregular or you do not track it, the rule is the same, because it comes from what you feel in the set and not from the calendar.

You do not have to stop for your period. If the pain is mild, moving usually helps; if it is intense, swap the session for mobility or postpone it, and if it repeats every month, see a doctor.

## Temperature, sleep and perceived effort

In the luteal phase basal temperature rises by about three tenths of a degree. That is little on a thermometer and a lot in a room without ventilation: you sweat sooner, your pulse runs a little faster and the same weight feels heavier. Poor sleep multiplies the effect.

The practical answer is simple. Rest longer, drink more and do not chase a record on four hours of sleep. The [central nervous system](/en/central-nervous-system-fatigue/) notices sleep before the muscle does, and a hard session on little rest leaves more fatigue than stimulus. If the tiredness lasts more than two weeks, the guide to [overtraining](/en/overtraining/) helps separate it from a bad streak.

When you measure progress, compare the same phase with the same phase: your luteal session this month with last month's, not with last Sunday's. An RPE 8 in the luteal phase can be an RPE 7 in the follicular phase at the same load.

## Bleeding, iron and fatigue

Heavy bleeding, with clots or lasting more than seven days, is associated with low ferritin. Fatigue that will not lift, breathlessness on stairs or pallor are reasons to ask for a blood test. Iron from food (legumes, red meat, sardines) absorbs better with vitamin C. Iron supplements should follow a blood test, because excess has a cost too.

## When to stop and see a doctor

Amenorrhoea, meaning three months without a period and no pregnancy, is a sign that energy balance or the hormonal axis is off, and it needs a doctor. So does pain that stops you training, bleeding between periods or bleeding that soaks a pad or tampon every hour.

## What not to change

The exercise table stays: squat, deadlift, press, row. Changing the programme every week prevents comparison and breaks progression. A deload is not mandatory before your period; it is a tool for when performance drops two weeks in a row, and it has its own guide in the [deload week](/en/deload-week/).

There is also no diet to sync with the phase. Calories and protein are set by goal and body weight, not by cycle day. With combined hormonal contraception the natural cycle is switched off and fluctuations are smaller; autoregulation from symptoms still applies.

If you do cardio alongside weights, the guide to [cardio and weights for women](/en/cardio-and-weights-for-women/) lays out the week. For a template that does not change with the phase, [women's weight routines](/en/women-weight-routines/) offers three options by days per week. And to understand what strength does across a lifetime, [women and weights](/en/women-and-weights/) covers bone, muscle and function.
