Creatine and caffeine are two of the few supplements with a real effect on performance, and for decades they were said to get in each other's way. The story is worth telling in full, because the rule in circulation comes from one specific experiment rather than a general law.

## The study that created the rule

In 1996, a group of Belgian researchers gave creatine to a small group of men for six days, 45 grams in total. Strength went up. When they added 450 milligrams of caffeine a day, the gain disappeared.

The finding drew attention and became advice. Do not mix creatine and caffeine. The design deserves a look before accepting it. There were few subjects, the caffeine came in a high constant dose, and the outcome was strength, not muscle mass. A protocol like that describes what happened in that lab, not what happens to someone who drinks coffee every morning.

## The British study of 2002

Six years later, a team at the University of Luton asked the question again with a different design. They loaded creatine and added a single caffeine dose before an endurance test. Performance improved rather than declined.

The difference was the pattern. It was one caffeine dose instead of a high daily intake throughout loading. The authors concluded the two could be used together, and that reading has held.

## The Japanese flask study of 2009

A Japanese paper added a piece to the puzzle. In rodent muscle exposed to caffeine in a test tube, the AMPK enzyme switched on and creatine content fell within minutes.

The finding was cited as proof of antagonism, and it is not. The concentrations in the experiment were far higher than caffeine reaches in the blood of a person drinking a coffee. An effect in an isolated cell does not transfer to an adult with two cups in.

## The current reading

At habitual doses, there is no relevant interaction between creatine and caffeine. Creatine fills the phosphocreatine tank and caffeine blocks adenosine receptors; they act through different routes and do not compete for the same transporter.

That does not make caffeine free. Its cost shows up in sleep, anxiety and heart rate, and those effects can affect training and recovery.

## How much caffeine is reasonable

The practical reference is up to 3 milligrams per kilo of body weight a day. For a 70 kg person that is about 210 milligrams, the equivalent of two filtered coffees. The figure rises to 400 milligrams in most health guidance for healthy adults, and staying at the lower one is sensible.

## Timing matters more than mixing

Caffeine has a half-life of about five hours, and in some people it runs longer. A coffee at six in the evening is still in the blood at midnight and reduces deep sleep even if you fall asleep without trouble.

The rule that works is simple. The last dose, about eight hours before bed. If you train in the afternoon, a small coffee before the session is reasonable; a loaded pre-workout at eight at night is not. The guide to [how much sleep you need to progress](/en/sleep-for-muscle-growth/) explains why sleep matters more than any supplement.

## Fitting it into the day

Creatine is taken at 3 to 5 grams a day, at any hour, with or without coffee. There is no window to respect and no required gap between them.

Coffee works best before training, 30 to 60 minutes out, and it is worth not stacking doses to cover fatigue. If the session is strength work with long pauses, caffeine helps keep set quality; the guide to [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) adjusts those times.

## What you do not need

You do not need a pre-workout with creatine and caffeine together to get either benefit. Nor do you need to give up coffee while loading. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) reviews the combinations that only add a label.
