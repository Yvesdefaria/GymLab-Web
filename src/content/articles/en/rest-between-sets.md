**Muscle fibres** only go through physiological change if they are recruited and left exhausted. If too much **time passes between sets**, the exhausted fibres can recover and be used again on the next set, so the muscle group keeps working with fibres that have already been used.

**Rest times between sets** are a much-debated topic. Rest periods in many routines draw comments when they do not match the rep range or the loads used.

In theory there are **rest times** you should respect for your goal. That is true, but not everything is black and white: there are grey areas where you can change those periods to add variety to the routine.

For example, a volume routine can use rep ranges, sets and rests more typical of strength and definition work. That is not wrong. It is a variation that forces the muscles to adapt to something they have not met before. It is a routine for advanced lifters, but its final goal is still **hypertrophy**.

The rest periods below are the ones most accepted by the fitness community. They are the periods every beginner should respect. If you are a **beginner or intermediate**, stick to them: in general they give the best results.

## Rest between sets for each training goal

Rest length follows the **rep range** you are working in. If you train strength in the 3 to 6 rep range, rest longer between sets, around 2 to 5 minutes. To gain strength, make sure the muscles are as rested as possible on every set.

Do not risk less recovery, with shorter rest periods, just to speed up the session when you are training for strength.

If you do 8 to 12 reps to **build hypertrophy**, the optimal window is 60 to 90 seconds. In **volume** or **hypertrophy** training the muscles should not be fully rested, as in strength training. But if rest periods are excessively short, the number of reps you can do on the following sets will drop.

At the other end, **endurance development** calls for continuous or almost continuous sets. If you train **muscular endurance** with high reps, short rest periods of around 30 seconds are best. Resting longer than that can change the purpose of the exercise.

## Why rest changes the intensity of the session

The time you take between sets has an important relationship with the **intensity** of the session. **Power** is the percentage at which the work is done. The faster you do the work, the more power you produce. Your **training session** will generally be more intense when you complete it in less time.

The time it takes you to complete **one working set** depends on the **rest intervals between sets**. When the rest period is minimal, you complete the work in less time and the session is more intense.

## Why do more than one set

The theory of **multiple sets**, that is, doing more than one set per exercise, rests on fibre recruitment. When the **time between sets** is not enough for the **muscle fibres** to recover from **fatigue**, a different group of fibres is recruited and **overloaded** on the next set.

> That way, the **multiple-set** method can overload **more** muscle fibres and produce greater adaptations than the **single-set** protocol.

## When to leave the rest table

These times should be respected whenever possible. You may need to **increase** the rest on an especially hard set, or shorten it on a set you took at half effort for whatever reason.

As you gain training experience, you learn to use your **instinct** to improve your sessions in the **gym**.

## In short

Rest follows your goal: 2 to 5 minutes for strength, 60 to 90 seconds for hypertrophy and 20 to 45 seconds for endurance. Respect those windows when you can and adjust them on the sets that ask for it. Experience teaches you when the clock falls short and when it should run longer.
