The pectoralis major is the muscle you can see and the one that answers the press. Underneath there are two layers almost nobody trains on purpose, and both explain why the shoulder sits the way it does on the bench.

## The pectoralis major and its two heads

The pectoralis major starts on the clavicle, the sternum, the first ribs and the aponeurosis of the external oblique. It ends on the humerus, at the crest of the greater tubercle. That wide base gives it the fan shape that covers the whole front of the chest.

Two heads are described. The **clavicular** head sits high, runs from the clavicle to the insertion and pulls the arm up and in. The **sternal** head is considerably larger, runs from the sternum and the lower ribs and pulls the arm down and in. Both produce adduction and internal rotation of the humerus, and both take part in any horizontal press.

That division is not a neat border. The fibres blend at the insertion and the tendon is shared, so which head does more work depends on the angle of the arm, not on a conscious choice.

## The pectoralis minor and the serratus anterior

The pectoralis minor does not move the arm. It starts on the third, fourth and fifth ribs, near their cartilages, and inserts on the coracoid process of the scapula. Its action is to pull the shoulder blade forward and down: protraction and depression.

That is the opposite of what you want in a press. If the pectoralis minor drags the shoulder blade forward while you push, the shoulder loses its stable base and the front deltoid takes on more load. That is why the shoulder blades come together and down before the set starts, and stay there until it ends.

The serratus anterior runs from the first ribs to the inner face of the shoulder blade. Its job is to keep the scapula against the rib cage and to rotate it as the arm rises. It is trained with straight-arm pushes and stability work, not with heavy presses.

## What the bench angle changes

The map repeated in every gym says: incline for the upper chest, flat for the middle, decline for the lower part. It is a useful guide once you understand what each position does.

An inclined bench puts the arm on a line that favours the clavicular fibres. The effect is real up to about 30 degrees; above that, the front deltoid takes a growing share of the work. The guide to [incline chest exercises](/en/incline-chest-exercises/) works through the angle and how to check it on your own bench.

A decline bench and pulleys pulling from above load the lower part of the sternal head, the portion that sits next to the abdominal wall. A low pulley pulling upward does the opposite and moves closer to the upper chest.

## What the grip changes

A grip wider than the shoulders shifts the emphasis towards the outer fibres, the ones next to the armpit. A slightly narrower grip moves it towards the centre of the chest.

The difference exists and it is small. It does not change the shape of the muscle in a few weeks and it does not make up for a weak plan. If a narrow grip bothers your shoulder, there is nothing to gain by insisting: go back to the width that lets you lower the bar without pain.

## Emphasising is not isolating

Both heads end on the same tendon. Any exercise that brings the arm towards the body recruits them together, and no hand position disconnects one so the other works alone.

What angle and grip give you is a different share of the effort, not a muscle split into pieces. It is the same principle that governs the quadriceps, where four bellies share one tendon and posture changes the emphasis without dividing the group. The guide to [quadriceps myths](/en/quadriceps-training-myths/) develops that point.

## How to use the map in a session

One incline press and one flat press cover both heads under load. A flye or a cable crossover adds the adduction movement with straighter arms. With that, the pectoral gets work at both ends of its range.

Order matters more than variety. Presses come first, when you can move weight and control technique; isolation comes after, when fatigue no longer allows load. The guide to [machines and free weights](/en/machines-vs-free-weights-chest/) explains how to divide the equipment in a session.
