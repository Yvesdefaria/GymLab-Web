Fruit rarely appears on lists of food for building muscle, and cherries have a reason to be there. They provide carbohydrate, digest well and add plant compounds that research has looked at with interest. None of that data turns them into a treatment.

## What a serving of cherries provides

One hundred grams of sweet cherries provide about 13 grams of carbohydrate, almost all natural sugar, and around 1.5 grams of fibre. They also carry water, potassium and vitamin C, and their red colour comes from anthocyanins, a group of phenolic compounds.

As fruit, their role in the diet is clear: they add carbohydrate and micronutrients with little volume. They are not a protein source and do not replace a meal. One cup of pitted cherries provides around 20 grams of carbohydrate, a normal figure for any fresh fruit. The season is short, so frozen cherries or juice with no added sugar cover the rest of the year.

## The 14-person trial

A small study gathered 14 men and gave them 450 millilitres of cherry juice or a placebo twice a day for eight days. On the fourth day they performed eccentric biceps curls, a protocol designed to cause muscle damage and soreness. Over the following four days, the juice group reported less pain and lost less strength than the placebo group.

The results are interesting and limited. The sample was 14 people, the product was juice rather than fruit, and it is a single trial. No study of that size proves that cherries repair muscle or speed recovery in general. The signal is small and points in a reasonable direction.

## What it means for your training

The practical reading is simple: half a cup to one cup of cherries is a reasonable portion, and it fits as a carbohydrate source in any meal. If the goal is to cover recovery, the priority remains protein and the daily total.

Cherries do not replace the 20 to 40 grams of protein in the meal afterwards. Nor are they necessary to train: anyone who does not tolerate them or does not like them gets the same carbohydrate from a banana, an orange or any other fruit. The guide to [the post-workout meal](/en/post-workout-meal/) explains how to build that meal.

Cherry juice concentrates the sugar and loses the fibre of the fruit. If it is used, it is better treated as an occasional drink than as a serving of fruit. Frozen cherries work just as well and cost less out of season.

## What they do not do

Cherries are not a medical treatment and do not replace medication for any condition. Nor do they refill glycogen on their own or offset a diet low in carbohydrate. A serving of fruit provides anthocyanins in small amounts, far from the dose of an anti-inflammatory.

The confusion comes from mixing laboratory studies with results in people. Phenolic compounds have activity in a test tube, and that does not translate automatically into less pain or more muscle. Rest and protein do more for recovery than any fruit. The page on [supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) reviews the same trap with recovery products.

## The rest of the week

Recovery is built with sleep, enough protein and sensible progression. Cherries add a small and pleasant part of that set. The guide to [diet discipline](/en/diet-discipline/) helps fruit form part of the plan instead of being an extra that gets forgotten.

If you train hard and soreness does not settle, review volume and sleep hours before looking for a miracle fruit. The [basic nutrition rules](/en/bodybuilding-nutrition-basics/) set the protein and fat for the day. The guide to [carbohydrates](/en/carbohydrates/) explains how much you need, and the one on [homemade shakes](/en/pre-and-post-workout-shakes/) gives ideas for combining them with protein.

Half a cup or one cup of cherries is good fruit. Nothing more, and nothing less.
