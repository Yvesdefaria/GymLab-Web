Larry Scott was born on 12 October 1938 in Blackfoot, Idaho, and died on 8 March 2014. He was the first Mr. Olympia champion and one of the bodybuilders who turned arms into a spectacle in their own right.

In five years he reached the sport's biggest title and retired earlier than anyone expected.

## The teenager with the tractor axle

As a teenager Larry was a gymnast. He was sixteen when, taking out the rubbish, he found a bodybuilding magazine with George Paine on the cover doing a side triceps pose. He had never seen anything like it.

The magazine promised that those exercises built the arms on the cover. Larry decided he wanted arms like that. He had no equipment, but he did have the axle of an old tractor.

With that homemade device he got his first pump. By the end of the summer his arms had grown to 31 centimetres and he could not stop.

## The road to California

At high school he trained every day with such enthusiasm that his body could not keep up. He swapped the tractor axle for the barbells and dumbbells at the local gym and devoured every issue of Muscle Builder, Joe Weider's magazine.

To improve he had to get to California, but he needed to make a living. The solution appeared on the back of a matchbox, in an advert promising money in electronics and a place at California Air College. He was interested in the school because of where it was, Hollywood.

He convinced his parents and got on a train. Seventeen hours later he was in California.

## Vince Gironda's apprentice

He had no car, but he could walk to school, and Bert Goodrich's Health Club was a kilometre and a half away on Hollywood Boulevard. The gym was full of bodybuilders, and the one who caught his eye was Lou Degni: a 73-centimetre waist, deep abs and 48-centimetre arms.

Larry weighed 68 kilos when he arrived. Even so, his progress caught Degni's attention and they ended up training together. When Degni was offered a role in an Italian film, Larry moved to Vince's Gym in the San Fernando Valley.

Vince's Gym was one of those [old-school gyms](/en/old-school-gym-training/) where agents sent their actors to get in shape. Vince Gironda had a reputation for a difficult character and for knowing more about training and nutrition than anyone. He helped Larry with both.

From Vince he learned to pose between poses. The line he repeated years later sums up the lesson: what you do between two poses matters more than the pose. He also began to take nutrition seriously.

## The titles before the Olympia

Larry won Mr. Idaho in 1959 and knew California was another matter. In 1960 he placed third at Mr. Los Angeles and won Mr. California. In 1961 he won Mr. Pacific Coast and in 1962 the overall Mr. America.

The career continued with the 1963 Mr. Universe, medium height class, and the 1964 edition, where he took the overall title. He went from a state champion to the biggest prize in bodybuilding at the time.

## The first Mr. Olympia

Several organisations ran Mr. America and Mr. Universe contests. In 1965 Joe Weider decided to create an event that would settle who was the best bodybuilder alive. He called it Mr. Olympia and the first edition was held at the Brooklyn Academy of Music.

To compete you needed a major title. Harold Poole and Earl Maynard arrived in great shape but could not stop Larry from entering the history books. He won in 1965 and repeated in 1966. The prize was 1,000 dollars and a trophy shaped like a crown.

Before the 1965 final he remembered the friends who told him he would never make it because he was too small. He weighed 55 kilos when he arrived in California and now passed 90, with arms bigger than most men's legs.

The full list of champions is in the guide to [Mr. Olympia winners](/en/mr-olympia-winners/).

## Hollywood, retirement and death

Larry's physique opened the door to photography: he was one of the first bodybuilders to work as a model, and Pat Milo's photos are archive pieces today.

He retired after the 1966 Mr. Olympia, when a long reign seemed to be starting. He said he looked at himself in the mirror at two in the morning, decided he had already done it and chose to get on with his life. Trophies did not pay the bills, so he went into several businesses.

In 1991 he published Loaded Guns, which introduced him to a new generation, and in 1999 he entered the Joe Weider Hall of Fame. He died on 8 March 2014, aged 75, after a battle with Alzheimer's disease.

## The arms: his biceps routine

Larry had long biceps and credited his shape to the incline bench curl now known as the Scott curl. The movement spread when he put it at the centre of his routine, and the bench ended up carrying his name.

His biceps work was organised in tri-sets: three exercises back to back with no rest. At the end of each set he added five partial reps to squeeze the muscle.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scott bench curl with dumbbells | 3-4 | 6+4 + 5 |
| Scott bench curl with a straight bar | 3-4 | 6+4 + 5 |
| Reverse Scott bench curl with an EZ bar * | 3-4 | 6+4 + 5 |

> **\*** Larry also did this exercise standing.

The 6+4 scheme means six strict reps and four with a small swing, plus five partials. Partials get copied a lot and performed badly; the guide to [partial reps](/en/partial-reps/) explains where technique ends.

## The shoulders

Larry had a narrow shoulder structure and made up for it with muscle. He trained his delts two or three times a week with dumbbells and barbells, and finished sets with partials.

His two favourite exercises were lateral raises and the dumbbell press. On the press he stripped plates after each set and kept going down in weight, a form of descending sets he also used elsewhere.

| Exercise | Sets | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dumbbell lateral raises | 5-6 | 8-10 |
| Dumbbell press, descending sets | 5-6 | 8-10 |
| Dumbbell front raises | 5-6 | 8-10 |
| Upright row | 5-6 | 8-10 |

The upright row closes the original routine. It deserves care: when the elbows rise above the shoulder, the supraspinatus is compressed against the acromion. The guide to [the most injurious gym exercises](/en/most-injurious-gym-exercises/) explains how to reduce that risk.

## The legacy

Larry Scott was the first name on a list that now runs past sixty editions. He also showed that a well-built arm could fill a stage, and his preacher bench routine is still in gyms sixty years later.

The rest of his method is a lesson in order: few exercises, long sets, partials at the end and shoulders two or three times a week. It was a competitor's routine built on years of base and a body used to that volume. For anyone starting out, the useful part is the least glamorous: four exercises repeated for years.

The ideas Arnold took further, such as descending sets and partials, are in the guide to [Arnold's shoulder tricks](/en/arnold-shoulder-training/), which places those techniques in context.
