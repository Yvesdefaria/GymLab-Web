The category mixes very different things. A protein sachet with vitamins, a ready-to-drink bottle and a cereal bar share a shelf and do not share a composition. All of them promise to cover a meal, and only some of them manage it.

## What it actually is

A meal replacement product delivers protein, carbohydrate, fat and micronutrients in a serving mixed with water or milk. It comes as powder, ready to drink, or bar. The advantage is logistical: it takes a minute to prepare and does not need a kitchen. The bar is the easiest form to carry in a pocket and the closest to a sweet: that is where sugar deserves an extra look.

What it does not do is improve performance. There is no ergogenic effect in a shake that replaces a meal. If training goes well, it is because of the calories and protein it supplies, not because of a special formula.

## The label, per serving and per 100 grams

Labels are read per serving and per 100 grams. The serving is what you will actually take; the 100-gram figure lets you compare two products with different scoop sizes. A tub can declare 40 grams of protein per 100 and serve 25 grams per scoop: the number that counts is the serving.

## The arithmetic of a meal

An example with round numbers. A 52-gram sachet with 24 grams of protein, 8 of sugar, 4 of fibre and 210 calories is mixed with 300 ml of water. For an 80-kilo adult chasing 144 grams of protein a day, those 24 grams cover a sixth of the total. The 210 calories, by contrast, do not cover a meal in a 3,000-calorie plan.

To turn it into a meal, add: 60 grams of oats (around 220 calories and 8 grams of protein), a banana (around 100 calories) and 200 ml of milk (130 calories and 7 grams of protein). The total lands around 660 calories and 39 grams of protein, which is a meal.

## When it makes sense

It works when a meal is impossible: a trip, a night shift, a move or a day without a kitchen. It also works when appetite is low and solid food is hard to get down.

It does not work as a weight-loss method. Losing weight depends on a calorie deficit and protein, not on drinking food. Nor does it replace three meals a day: solid food brings fibre, volume and satiety that a shake does not replicate. If two meals a day depend on the shake, the problem is organisation, not the product.

## Powder, bottle or bar

The format changes the logistics and the price, not the nutrition. Powder is the cheapest per serving and the easiest to adjust. A ready-to-drink bottle opens and goes, usually with more sugar and more packaging. A bar survives in a bag and looks less like a full meal. Choose by the situation, not by the promise on the pack.

## Price per meal

The price of the tub says nothing. Divide the price by the number of servings and compare that number with the cost of a meal cooked at home. Per meal, a replacement can be cheaper than a restaurant menu and more expensive than rice, eggs and vegetables. Count what you add as well: milk, oats and fruit are part of the real cost of the meal.

That is the honest comparison: convenience against price, with calories and protein on the table. A cheap product that does not satisfy is expensive if it forces another meal an hour later.
