The male chest holds a small amount of glandular tissue and some fat. When the gland grows, the picture is true gynecomastia. When only fat increases, it is called pseudogynecomastia. The two look similar from the outside and are handled differently.

## Gland or fat

On palpation, the gland feels like a firm, mobile disc behind the nipple; fat is soft and diffuse. Examination and, if needed, an ultrasound establish which is which.

The two can coexist. A person with excess weight and gynecomastia has fat and gland at the same time. Losing weight improves the fat part and can reduce apparent volume, but it does not remove glandular tissue.

A body-fat scale says nothing about the gland, and photographs change with light and posture. The distinction is anatomical, and only touch and imaging settle it.

## Why the gland grows

Growth depends on the balance between oestrogens and androgens. Any situation that raises oestrogens, lowers testosterone or blocks its action can produce it. The most cited causes are these:

- Puberty: it appears in many boys and usually resolves within months or a couple of years.
- Medication: some diuretics, antiandrogens, antidepressants and digestive treatments are among those described.
- Alcohol and drugs: heavy alcohol use and cannabis are associated with the picture.
- Disease: liver, kidney, thyroid, hypogonadism and obesity.
- Anabolic steroids: they convert into oestrogens and are a common cause in people who train.

The list is not for self-diagnosis. It explains why clinical history and a blood test weigh more than appearance. Timing also gives a clue: puberty, a change of medication or the start of a steroid cycle are different leads, and the appointment sorts them out.

## What not to do

Antioestrogens and chorionic gonadotropin are medicines with their own indications and side effects. They are not used on your own for a lump in the chest, and certainly not as part of a steroid cycle. No supplement shrinks the gland either, and no cream changes the tissue.

Self-medication delays the appointment and can make the picture worse. The only real shortcut is to seek advice early: the longer the gland has been there, the less room there is to treat it without surgery.

## When to seek advice without waiting

A hard, fixed lump, nipple retraction, discharge, skin changes or rapid one-sided growth in an adult call for a prompt appointment. Male breast cancer is uncommon, and precisely for that reason it is diagnosed late. Most lumps are not cancer, but the only way to know is to examine and, where appropriate, test.

Hormone blood tests, ultrasound and mammography are the usual tools. The doctor decides which are needed from the history and the examination, and rules out other causes of breast enlargement.

## What treatment can offer

Gynecomastia of puberty usually resolves on its own. When there is a pharmacological or toxic cause, removing it is the first step, and that decision is made with the doctor who prescribed it. If the gland has become fibrous and has been established for years, surgery is the option that removes it; the technique and the timing are assessed by a surgeon.

Chest training does not reduce the gland. It strengthens the pectoral and can improve the overall shape, but it does not replace treatment. Lowering body fat reduces pseudogynecomastia and makes the contour more visible. Rapid growth can leave [stretch marks](/en/prevent-stretch-marks/) on the skin of the chest.
