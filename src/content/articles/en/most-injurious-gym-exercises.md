These exercises share a problem: they put the joints through a range or a pressure they are not prepared for. Many people do them without trouble, and that does not mean the risk is absent. It means their anatomy, their flexibility and their load let them tolerate it.

The list is a scale of risk, and each section explains what you can keep, what you should change and what to use instead.

## Behind-the-neck press

To perform it, the shoulders go into extreme abduction and external rotation, and the joint works in a range it is not prepared for. The arm's range is 180 degrees, meant for eating and for lifting things in front of you. Any movement that takes the load behind the head falls outside that stable range.

The direct alternative is the military press, with the bar in front of your face. The seated dumbbell press also works, since it lets you adjust the path without forcing the shoulder.

## Behind-the-neck pulldowns

Pulldowns are an excellent back exercise because they activate almost every fibre of the lats. The problem appears in the version that takes the bar behind the head.

As in the behind-the-neck press, the shoulder reaches the final phase in external rotation, and the risk of injury grows for no reason. The combination of abduction above 80 degrees and forced external rotation can favour anterior hyperlaxity, joint instability, suprascapular neuropathy or rotator cuff tendinitis. That possibility increases when shoulder flexibility is limited.

The front version is the default. If you still do the behind-the-neck pulldown, do it exactly as the guide to [behind-the-neck pulldowns](/en/behind-the-neck-lat-pulldowns/) describes, with a strict path and never as a maximum-load exercise. In practice, that means a moderate weight and no rep that forces the bar out of its path.

Pull-ups and front pulldowns are the alternatives.

## Upright row

It is a very effective exercise for the lateral head of the deltoid and, at the same time, one of the riskiest. Poor execution can injure the supraspinatus and keep you out of the weight room for weeks.

The cause is elbow height. When the arms rise above 90 degrees, the supraspinatus tendon is compressed again and again against the acromion, the bone just above it. The inflammation comes from repetition, not from a single gesture.

You can reduce the risk with a grip wide enough, at shoulder width or wider, which also takes load off the wrists. During the movement, keep the bar close to your body and never take the elbow above shoulder height. Locking the knees is also common, and it adds unnecessary tension in the lumbar area: keep them slightly bent.

The cleanest alternative is lateral raises in any of their variants, which work the same deltoid without taking the shoulder into that position.

## Stiff-leg deadlift

Here the risk is in the spine. Rounding the back under load puts a lot of pressure on the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs. Add compression to that flexion and the result is a combination that, repeated over months, can end in a disc herniation.

With a neutral spine, a controlled hip hinge and a moderate load, the exercise changes profile. The problem appears when the weight forces you to round or when you chase maximum stretch with a curved back.

If you use it only to stretch the hamstrings, do it with a straight back, without curling the spine, and with a maximum of 20 to 30 kilos in two sets of six reps. For strength work, the Romanian deadlift is the safer option. Both published variants, [with a barbell](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-barbell/) and [with dumbbells](/en/stiff-leg-deadlift-dumbbells/), explain how to set the back and how far to go. If you cannot hold a neutral spine, stay with the Romanian deadlift.

## Horizontal leg press

Heavy leg work often leans on the leg press because there is no squat rack. The problem is how you get into the machine and how you execute.

Simply going from standing to sitting raises disc pressure by around 40%, because the weight of the trunk no longer rests on the legs. Sitting also deforms the natural curves of the spine and flexes the lumbar area, which puts tension on the discs too. And the core and glutes, which stabilise the back when standing, stop working.

The leg press does not need to be removed. The fix is in the depth and the pelvis position: go down only as far as you can keep the lumbar area supported and the sacrum in contact with the backrest. People with limited flexibility, or those who go too deep to get close to the platform, round the lower back with a large load on top. If you have any lumbar problem, sacrifice kilos.

To work the squat safely, the guide to [squat safety](/en/squat-safety/) covers depth, control and progressions.

## How to keep what works

No single exercise builds a physique. The sum of orderly sessions over years does, which is the logic of the [Weider principles](/en/weider-principles/). And that sum requires reaching the next session without injuries: the [most common gym injuries](/en/common-gym-injuries/) share a handful of causes.

The practical rule is simple: keep the exercise that loads the muscle in a position your joint tolerates. If the range hurts, if the back rounds or if technique breaks as the weight goes up, change the exercise or lower the load. [Exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) matters more than any fashionable variation.
