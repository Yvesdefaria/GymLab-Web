The front of the upper arm holds two main muscles, the biceps brachii and the brachialis, and the forearm adds a dozen small muscles that move the wrist and the fingers. Knowing what each one does turns the curl into a decision instead of a habit.

## The biceps brachii: two heads and three actions

The biceps has a long head and a short head. The long head starts at the supraglenoid tubercle of the scapula and crosses the shoulder joint. The short head starts at the coracoid process, lower and further in. Both join and end at the radial tuberosity, with a sheet of tissue that fades into the fascia of the forearm.

That path defines its functions. It flexes the elbow, it is the strongest supinator of the forearm and, through the long head, it takes part in shoulder flexion. When turning the palm up becomes hard, the biceps is working.

The long head also helps keep the head of the humerus centred in the glenoid cavity. That role explains why the incline curl, with the arm behind the body, stretches it more than a standing curl.

## The brachialis: the flexor you cannot see

The brachialis sits under the biceps. It starts on the lower half of the front of the humerus and ends on the ulna, near the coronoid process. It crosses the elbow and never touches the radius, so it flexes and nothing else: it does not supinate.

That is why the hammer curl loads it so well. With a neutral grip, the biceps loses part of its advantage as a supinator and the brachialis takes a good share of the flexion. A large brachialis pushes the biceps up and adds thickness to the arm.

## The brachioradialis and the forearm muscles

The brachioradialis runs from the lateral supracondylar ridge of the humerus to the base of the styloid process of the radius. It is the muscle that shows on the thumb side, high on the forearm, and it flexes the elbow mainly when the hand is neutral or pronated.

The wrist flexors occupy the palmar side of the forearm and the extensors the dorsal side. Almost all of them start on the medial or lateral epicondyle and cross the wrist to the hand. They are too small to treat as individual targets: they are trained as a group with wrist curls and grip work.

## Which grip loads each muscle

- **Close grip, inside shoulder width:** loads the long head more, the one that crosses the shoulder.
- **Wide grip, outside shoulder width:** shifts the emphasis to the short head.
- **Neutral or hammer grip:** brachialis and brachioradialis, with the biceps in the background.
- **Pronated grip, palms down:** brachioradialis and forearm extensors, as in the reverse curl.
- **Scott or preacher bench:** the elbow sits in front of the body, the long head is shortened and the short head does more work.

Both heads share one tendon and one nerve. You can load one more than the other through arm position, but you cannot fully isolate them. The idea of emphasising without separating is the same one that applies to any muscle group.

## The biceps peak is genetic

The shape of the muscle, its length and where it inserts are inherited. No exercise changes those traits. A long, flat biceps stays long and flat whatever routine you follow; the only thing you can change is its size.

Magazines sell peak routines because they sound good, not because they work. They train the long head, the one that forms the rise when the arm contracts, and that improves how the arm looks. The peak itself does not appear from preacher curls. Larry Scott had his from genetics; he would have had it with any other biceps exercise.

## How to apply anatomy to your routine

With the map clear, exercise choice stops being random. A close grip and an incline curl load the long head; a wide grip and the Scott bench load the short head; the hammer and the reverse curl take care of the brachialis and the forearm.
