A calorie calculation gives you a number to start from. Without it, the diet is adjusted by feel and something different changes every week. With it, the starting point is reasonable and the margin of error narrows after two weeks of data.

## What maintenance calories are

They are the calories that match daily expenditure and keep weight stable. Four parts make up the sum: basal metabolism, everyday activity, exercise and the digestion of food. Eating above that figure leads to weight gain; eating below it, to weight loss. Maintenance is the zero point from which any goal moves.

## The Mifflin-St Jeor formula

The most widely used estimate in clinical nutrition is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990. It replaced Harris-Benedict, which tends to overestimate expenditure. It needs weight in kilos, height in centimetres and age in years.

- Men: (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) - (5 × age) + 5
- Women: (10 × weight) + (6.25 × height) - (5 × age) - 161

The result is basal metabolic rate in kcal per day, what the body spends at rest. Then activity multiplies it.

The factor is the most common source of error. Choose it for the whole week, not for the best day. Someone who trains four times and sits for eight hours is not a very active person, even if the sessions are hard.

## A worked example with correct units

Fran is 31 years old, weighs 79 kg and is 1.78 m tall. He works on his feet, trains strength and cardio three or four times a week and rides a mountain bike at weekends.

The basal calculation runs like this: (10 × 79) + (6.25 × 178) - (5 × 31) + 5 = 790 + 1,112.5 - 155 + 5 = 1,752.5 kcal, rounded to 1,753.

Fran trains three or four times and moves around at work, so the moderate factor of 1.55 fits. Maintenance is around 2,716 kcal a day. It is an estimate.

Units deserve a check. Height goes in centimetres: 1.78 m is 178 cm. An old version of this calculation circulated with height written as 1.78 cm, which made the result come out far too low.

## The scale corrects the formula

The calculated number is a hypothesis. The weight is the test. Weigh yourself daily under the same conditions, fasted and after the bathroom, then compare one week's average with the previous one. Two stable weeks confirm maintenance; a clear trend disproves it.

Daily weight moves with glycogen, sodium, fibre and stress. The weekly average is the useful data; a single morning is noise.

## Deficit and surplus from that point

To lose fat, subtract 300-500 kcal and check the result after two weeks. To gain mass, add 200-300 kcal, the margin that best splits the gain between muscle and fat. Both adjustments use real maintenance, not the calculated figure. The guide to [losing fat and gaining muscle](/en/lose-fat-gain-muscle/) explains how to lose fat without losing strength.

A larger deficit does not speed up the process. It takes energy away from training and raises the risk of losing muscle.

## The errors that skew the count most

- Choosing an optimistic activity factor because training is hard.
- Using goal weight instead of current weight.
- Adding back the calories a watch reports.
- Changing the diet and the training plan in the same week.

## When to recalculate

Every four to six weeks, or when weight changes by five kilos. Expenditure falls with weight and rises with training volume. A calculation from six months ago describes a different person.
