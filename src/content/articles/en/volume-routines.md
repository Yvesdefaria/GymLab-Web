A volume routine organises the work to stimulate every muscle with the frequency and volume it can recover from. The goal is hypertrophy: more muscle tissue, not just more weight on the scale.

Diet decides how much of the gain is fat. With enough calories and protein, the plan builds; without them, it only accumulates fatigue.

## What a volume routine is

Hypertrophy means increasing the size of muscle fibres. It comes from sets that recruit fibres and leave them fatigued, spread across the week and repeated for months.

The 8 to 12 rep range is the most used because it lets you load enough without stretching sets to failure. Moving above and below it also works: a set of 5 to 6 with more weight and one of 15 to 20 with less cover the same muscle from different angles. What does not change is the need to leave one or two reps in reserve on most sets.

A volume routine distributes sets, reps, rests and days so you can progress without arriving at the next session broken. That is why the plan changes exercises every few weeks and the log matters more than the mirror.

## The rules the plans share

Three to five days a week. Fewer days leave little room for hypertrophy; more days compromise recovery if you do not sleep and eat for them.

Every muscle twice a week. Frequency spreads the volume and avoids concentrating twenty sets in a single session.

Between 9 and 16 sets per muscle per week. It is a starting point, not a law: the harder each set, the fewer sets you need. The [training volume](/en/training-volume/) guide explains how to count them.

Eight to twelve reps as a base, with heavier and lighter sets around it. The range is a reference; the effort is what counts.

Rests of 1 to 3 minutes depending on weight and reps. The [rest between sets](/en/rest-between-sets/) guide has the table by goal.

Cardio is optional: two or three days of 20 to 30 minutes at an easy or moderate pace. A block lasts eight weeks or less; after that, change exercises, split or scheme.

With three days, the three-way split or the push/pull routine. With four, Arnold, high intensity, ABBH1 or the annual plan. With five, the five-month plan or the twelve-week specialization.

If you have been training for less than six months, pick the plan with less volume and fewer techniques. Plans with tri-sets, giant sets or 10x3 ask for technique and recovery.

If your priority is legs, the full leg routine is added to an upper-body plan or replaces one of its days.

## How to start the first block

Choose one routine and run it for eight weeks without changing it. Write down the weight and reps of the first session; that is your reference. If a week is broken by travel or illness, pick up where you left off instead of starting from zero.

The first block does not need advanced techniques. Straight sets, enough rest and two or three exercises per muscle are enough to know whether the plan fits.

## What this selection does not include

The archive for the block announced a four-day example that was never published. This page does not invent it: if you need four days, use one of the four-day routines in the table.

The numbers are a starting point that you adjust with what you log, with no promises of results.

## How to know the plan is working

Log sets, reps and weight in every session. At the end of the block, compare: more reps with the same weight or more weight with the same reps is progress.

If the weight does not move for three weeks, check sleep, calories and the number of sets before changing the routine.
