On the high pulley, the pushdown is an elbow extension with the arm pinned to the body. Turning the hands up changes the wrist position and the demand on the forearm, which is why some lifters find it more comfortable and others find it harder.

## How to perform the reverse-grip pushdown

Clip a straight bar or a single handle to the high pulley and set the pulley at chest height. Take it with the palms up, at shoulder width or slightly narrower.

Pin the elbows to the torso and stand with the trunk upright and the abs braced. Extend the elbows all the way down, to the end of the range, and let the bar come back under control.

The movement happens at the elbow. If the shoulder joins in or the trunk leans towards the pulley, bodyweight pushes the bar and the triceps stops being the muscle at work.

## What the reverse grip changes

With the palms up, the wrist is supinated and the thumb wraps under the bar. That position asks more of the forearm extensors and of the muscles that close the hand, which is why the exercise feels harder at the same weight.

What does not change is the joint that moves or the muscle that moves it: the elbow extends and the triceps contracts. All three heads of the triceps share one distal tendon and work together on every rep; no grip isolates one. What the grip changes is wrist tolerance and the accessory work of the forearm.

That is why the reverse grip sits well next to the overhand grip. Overhand, with the palms down, is stronger and moves more weight. Reverse asks for less load and adds forearm work. Together they cover the exercise from two wrist positions.

## The anatomy that matters here

The medial head of the triceps is the deepest of the three. It starts on the posterior surface of the humerus, below the radial nerve groove, and sits covered by the lateral and long heads, close to the elbow.

Its position explains two things. The first is that it works on almost any elbow extension, including with the arm pinned to the torso. The second is that it does not need an exercise of its own: it shows up in the pushdown, the press and the overhead extension.

The full anatomical detail, with the origin of all three heads, is in [triceps anatomy](/en/triceps-anatomy/).

## Common mistakes

The first is letting the wrist drop while the bar rises. The wrist loses alignment with the forearm and the grip becomes uncomfortable.

The second is letting the elbows leave the torso. When they open, the chest joins the movement and the triceps range gets shorter.

The third is using bodyweight. Leaning into the pulley as you push turns the pushdown into an incline press with straight arms. Keep the trunk vertical and take weight off if you need to.

The fourth is cutting the range. Ending the rep with the elbows half extended leaves out the end of the movement, which is where the triceps contracts fully. The [exercise technique](/en/exercise-technique/) review covers that point across the rest of the catalogue.

## How to combine both grips

The simplest way is to alternate: one session with the overhand grip and one with the reverse, or two sets of each in the same block. The overhand grip handles more weight; the reverse adds the wrist and forearm work.

The overhand version is the [straight-bar triceps pushdown](/en/cable-triceps-pushdown/). The difference between the two is forearm rotation, not the muscle being trained.

## Sets, reps and placement

A recommendation you can apply as written: three or four sets of 8 to 12 reps, with 60 to 90 seconds of rest. Add weight when the last set comes out clean with the elbows still.

The pushdown goes at the end of the triceps session, after the barbell or dumbbell exercises. It is an isolation move and needs no specific warm-up if you already have two or three pressing exercises behind you.
