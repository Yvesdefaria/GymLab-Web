It is a detail exercise, and that does not make it minor. The hamstrings need knee flexion under load, and the standing machine delivers it to one leg with the torso stable.

## How to do the standing leg curl

Stand facing the machine, put your chest against the support and take the handles. The front of the thigh rests on the pad and the knee stays free to move.

Place the resistance roller just above the ankle, on the lower part of the calf. Breathe in, bend the knee and lift the foot until the shin is roughly parallel to the floor. Hold for a moment and lower under control.

The torso never leaves the pad. If the chest peels away, the movement becomes a swing.

## The hamstrings cross the hip

This is the point that changes how you see the exercise. The hamstrings cross two joints: the knee and the hip. On the standing machine, the leaned torso increases hip flexion and stretches the muscle before the knee bends.

That is not the same in the lying version, where the hip is extended and the starting stretch is smaller. The three usual positions differ by that angle:

If the machine lets you lean the torso from the hips, do it. That lean is what forces the hamstrings to stretch before the knee starts to bend, and it explains why the same weight feels heavier standing than lying. The anatomy of the area is in [hamstring anatomy](/en/hamstring-anatomy/).

## The shin parallel is a starting point

Shin parallel to the floor is a reference for beginners, not a mandatory end point. It is the point where the lever is closest to horizontal and the resistance stays high.

Going past it only makes sense if the machine allows it and the pelvis does not move. On many machines the resistance drops beyond that point and the foot travels towards the glute with no real load. When that happens, the rep is already over.

The full range of the hamstring includes knee flexion and the stretch at the hip. The general criteria are in the [hamstring training guide](/en/hamstring-training-guide/).

## Tempo and breathing

The lowering phase takes two to three seconds. That eccentric is the part that leaves the most stimulus and the one almost everyone cuts short out of haste.

At the top there is a short pause, about one second, with the hamstring contracted. Breathe: take air before lifting and let it out at the end of the flexion.

Speed is moderate in both directions. A quick jerk moves the roller, but the muscle travels the range without loading it.

## Sets, reps and rest

The recommendation for this page: three sets of 10 to 12 reps per leg, resting 60 seconds between sets. If the weight is light and the reps feel easy, go to 12 or 15 before adding plates.

Start with the weaker leg and match the rep count on the other. The goal is for both to reach the same effort, not for one to do more.

Progression is the same as in any isolation exercise: add weight once the last set is clean, without moving the torso or bouncing. The criterion is in the [progressive overload principle](/en/progressive-overload-principle/).

## Common mistakes

Too much weight is the usual error. With a heavy load the hip drifts back and the torso peels away to move the roller.

The second is leaving the foot in plantar flexion. If the ankle extends, the gastrocnemius joins the knee flexion and competes with the hamstring. The foot stays relaxed.

The third is moving the hip. The standing machine fixes the torso, but not the pelvis. If the hip drifts forward or back, part of the range is lost.

## Where it fits in the routine

It goes at the end of a leg or hamstring day, when the weight is no longer the priority. It also works as a second exercise after a big movement, with two sets and less load.

The lying version, which has a different tension curve, is in the [lying leg curl](/en/lying-leg-curl/). Alternating the two through the week covers the muscle at two different hip angles.
