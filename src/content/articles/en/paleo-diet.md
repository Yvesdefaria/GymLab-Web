The paleo diet is sold as a return to prehistory. What works is simpler: it removes long-label products from the cupboard and puts home-cooked food on the plate. It is a swap, and the scale shows it when there is a deficit.

## The swap that works

The story about genes that have not changed in ten thousand years sounds good and explains nothing. What explains the result is the shopping list: meat, fish, eggs, vegetables, fruit, tubers and good fat. On that base, nutrient density goes up and empty calories go down.

The original article insisted you do not need to count calories. That is half true. Protein and fibre fill you up, and well-built plates regulate hunger almost on their own. When the margin is small, counting helps; the [calorie deficit](/en/calorie-deficit-for-weight-loss/) guide explains how to do it without obsession.

Legumes and dairy are out in the strict version. Both are cheap sources of fibre, calcium and protein, so the decision has a price worth knowing. A flexible version admits them and keeps the rest of the approach.

## What the evidence says

Trials compare paleo with Mediterranean and low-fat diets, and at six and twelve months weight loss is similar. The difference shows up in dropouts: the people who stay are those who find a filling plan they can repeat. Paleo does that when vegetables and protein lead.

Markers improve in some studies: blood pressure, triglycerides, fasting glucose. The changes are modest and disappear if the weight returns. What sits behind them is less ultra-processed food, more fibre and a deficit that holds.

The mistake is selling fat loss as automatic. Without a deficit there is no fat loss, paleo or otherwise.

## The cost: fibre, calcium, iron and eating out

Removing wholegrains and legumes cuts fibre at a stroke. Vegetables, fruit, skin-on tubers and nuts put it back, but you have to add them on purpose.

Eating out is the other cost. Almost every dish on a menu comes with bread, rice, sauce or seed oil. The fix is grilled food and salad, plus accepting that the weekend will not be perfect.

Price per calorie goes up: fresh meat and fish cost more than pasta. What you save on delivery and snacks does not always make up for it, and it is worth knowing before you start.

## The two usual mistakes

The first is the dinosaur plate: steak, bacon and nothing green. Vegetables have to take half the plate, or the result is a diet high in saturated fat and low in fibre.

The second is paleo-labelled snacks. Biscuits, bars and syrups that arrive in a shiny packet, last two years and carry fifteen ingredients. Swapping sugar for agave syrup does not turn the product into broccoli.

The useful move from the original still stands: clear the cupboard and shop the perimeter of the supermarket, where the fresh food is.

## The 80/20 rule

Eighty per cent whole food and twenty per cent flexible. A pizza on Friday or a yoghurt breaks nothing. The rule exists so the plan lasts.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it low carb?** Not necessarily. Potato, sweet potato and fruit are in. If you train hard, adjust the amount to your workload.

**Do you need to fast?** No. [Intermittent fasting](/en/intermittent-fasting-and-weights/) is a separate tool that fits this base, and it is not a requirement.

**Can you gain muscle?** Yes, with a surplus and progressive overload. Quality protein is not missing.

**What about supplements?** None fixes a weak diet. If the plan cuts whole food groups, check the food first.
