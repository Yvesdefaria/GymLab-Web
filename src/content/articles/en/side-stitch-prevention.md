## What a side stitch is and why it appears

A side stitch is abdominal pain that appears during exertion and that science still does not fully explain. What has been observed is which factors favour it: a **posture** with the trunk very upright and the **jolts** you get while running or horse riding. In cyclists, whose trunk is inclined and who receive fewer shocks, the incidence is **thirteen times lower**.

The stitch affects athletes at every level. The more you train, the less susceptible you are, and the incidence also falls with age. Spinal problems favour its appearance.

## Can you get rid of a side stitch?

The usual manoeuvres bring **momentary relief**, not a permanent fix. That is also why leaning forward is recommended to make the pain disappear. Cyclists are not immune: other factors play a part too.

## What to do during the run

Lean your trunk forward, contract your abs and increase the intensity of your breathing. These are immediate, temporary relief manoeuvres.

Plunkett also recommends a **wide, light belt with a velcro closure** that you can tighten during a stitch and take off afterwards. During exertion, take small frequent sips instead of drinking a lot in an irregular pattern.

## The two case studies

### Case study 1: posture and jolts

*Morton O.P., et al. Effect of ingested fluid composition on exercise-related transient abdominal pain. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab. 2004 Apr; 14(2):197-208.*

In a survey of almost a thousand endurance athletes, **61%** reported having had the problem at least once in the past year. A posture with the trunk very upright and the jolts of running or horse riding are the factors that favour it most, which explains the difference with cycling, where the trunk is inclined and the shocks are smaller.

### Case study 2: food and drink

*Plunkett B.T., et al. Investigation of the side pain "stitch" induced by running after fluid ingestion. Med Sci Sports Exerc. 1999 Aug; 31(8):1169-75.*

Plunkett studied athletes who suffered the problem regularly and confirmed that incidence is higher when subjects have **eaten** or **drunk** before running. During exertion, drinking water increases the risk, how early the pain appears, its intensity and its duration. When the drink is very sugary (10% carbohydrates), the risk **doubles**, and the worst situation comes when that 10% concentration is exceeded.

Low-sugar drinks are preferable to drinks very rich in carbohydrates or to concentrated fruit juices. **Hypertonic** drinks, very rich in sodium, also increase the risk compared with isotonic or hypotonic ones. Starting dehydrated will make the strongest forms of stitch more likely. The good news is that gradually habituating your body to energy intake reduces the incidence during training.

## How to prevent a side stitch

Prevention rests on what you already know: train regularly, avoid eating or drinking right before running, choose low-sugar drinks, take frequent sips and start the session well hydrated. If you have spinal problems, that is another factor worth addressing. And remember that the manoeuvres during the run relieve the problem but do not eliminate it.
