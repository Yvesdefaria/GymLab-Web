The body rests on one forearm and on the edge of the lower foot. The hip tries to drop, the shoulder tries to climb towards the ear and the trunk stays still in between. That sustained resistance, with calm breathing, is the whole exercise.

## What the side plank is and what it trains

It is an anti-lateral-flexion isometric: the spine does not bend to the side because the muscles along the flank prevent it. The internal and external obliques, the gluteus medius and the quadratus lumborum are the main players. The deltoid, the lats and the transversus come along as support, which is where the work you feel in the shoulder and upper back comes from.

The pattern shows up in any task with an uneven load: carrying a bag in one hand, pushing a door with one shoulder or holding your balance when one foot takes more weight than the other. It also shows up in heavy lifts, where the trunk corrects without you noticing.

The standard version is done on the forearm with the feet stacked. The loaded version, the knee version and the wall version are steps of the same movement, not different exercises.

## Who it is for and who should adjust

It fits if you want stability for the squat, the overhead press or the deadlift, if you spend long hours seated and feel the flank switched off, or if you want core work that does not load the spine with repeated flexion.

Adjust it or leave it for later if there is acute shoulder pain, a diagnosed impingement or joint instability. The same goes for recent spinal pathology without medical clearance for isometric work, or if the hip will not stay up even with the knee down. In those cases, the regression comes first and a professional assessment before pushing.

## Step-by-step technique

1. Lie on your side with the forearm on the floor and the elbow exactly under the shoulder.
2. Stack the legs, one foot on top of the other. If you need a wider base, bring the top foot forward and rest it on the floor.
3. Squeeze the glutes and the abs, and lift the hip off the floor.
4. Form a straight line from head to heels, without the hip rising too high or dropping.
5. Breathe into the abdomen, without puffing the chest and without holding the air.

Three references sort out the position:

- **Push the floor** with the forearm, as if you wanted to shove it away. The lats switch on and the shoulder stops sinking.
- **Squeeze the top glute.** The hip is held from there, not from the lower back.
- **Stay between two glass walls.** The chest does not touch the front one and the back does not touch the rear one.

What you should feel is deep tension in the flank facing the floor and work in the side of the glute. What you should not feel is a sharp pain in the shoulder joint, tightness in the neck or discomfort that grows in the lower back as the seconds pass.

## Warning signs and safe adjustments

Three things are worth avoiding during the set. Holding your breath until you run out of air, because pressure climbs higher than needed and the set gets shorter. Letting the head hang towards the floor, because it breaks the line of the cervical spine. And chasing a time record with the position already broken: if the hip drops and the shoulder suffers, the set is over.

The warning signs are clear. Sharp pain inside the shoulder joint, tingling or numbness running down the supporting arm, and lower-back discomfort that grows as the seconds pass. Any of the three means stop and check the position.

There are three safe adjustments: rest the bottom knee on the floor instead of the feet, raise the forearm on a bench or a box, and shorten the set so you can do more short sets of high quality. All three lower the demand on the shoulder and keep the stimulus in the flank.

## Alternatives in the same pattern

- **Single-arm farmer's carry.** Trains anti-lateral flexion dynamically, with load and walking. It is the most transferable version to daily life.
- **Standing side plank against a wall.** Removes almost all the stress from the shoulder and works as an entry point after a minor strain.
- **Knee side plank.** The technical regression: it lets you feel the obliques and the glute without fighting the full line.
- **[Pallof press](/en/pallof-press/).** With a cable or a band, it trains anti-rotation and anti-lateral flexion standing up.

## Progressions and regressions

The order is this: knee down, standard with stacked feet, star with the top leg and arm raised, and finally load overhead.

The rule for moving up a step is concrete: three or four sets of forty to forty-five seconds per side, with a clean position and no hip drop. When that is in place, the next step is justified.

The version with a dumbbell or a kettlebell held overhead demands control of the shoulder and the trunk at the same time. Before adding it, the star version should be stable across several sessions.

## How to programme it in the week

**Hypertrophy.** Three to four sets of twenty to forty seconds per side, stopping a couple of seconds before technique starts to fail. Isometrics are not the main route to muscle mass; dynamic work through a full range contributes more.

**Strength and stability.** Three to four sets with micro-pauses: ten seconds of hard work, three seconds of rest, repeated three or four times to complete one set. Total time is short and tension stays high.

**Frequency.** Two to four times a week. The demand on the nervous system is low: it fits in the warm-up as activation or at the end of the session as a finisher. Every day is not needed, and the core recovers too.

## FAQ

**How long should I hold the side plank?** Between fifteen and forty-five seconds at maximum tension. Two minutes with a sagging hip trains something else: tolerance to discomfort.

**Is shoulder pain normal during the side plank?** No. If the pain is sharp, the set-up is wrong or the muscles that stabilise the scapula are not strong enough. Elbow under the shoulder and an active push into the floor.

**Does it burn belly fat?** No exercise burns fat from one spot. The side plank strengthens the muscle and improves posture; abdominal fat comes off with a sustained calorie deficit.

**How should I breathe during the set?** With fluid breathing directed into the abdomen, without holding the air. Breath-holding raises blood pressure more than necessary.

**Can I do it every day?** You can, but you should not. The core muscles also need to recover, and two to four days a week are enough to progress.
