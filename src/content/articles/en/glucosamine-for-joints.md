Glucosamine is sold as a joint protector and taken as if it were a painkiller. The two ideas need separating. In a knee with diagnosed osteoarthritis there are studies showing some relief. In a tendon that hurts from training, or in a healthy athlete trying to prevent trouble, no trial supports the expense.

## What it is and where it comes from

The body makes it from glucose and glutamine. Supplements are manufactured from shellfish shells and sold as sulfate, hydrochloride or N-acetylglucosamine. The form used in most trials is the sulfate. The hydrochloride form has also been tested, with less consistent results.

The sulfate matters: some work suggests the sulfate group takes part in the effect. That is why it is worth checking the salt on the label, not just the milligrams.

## What the knee evidence says

The most cited trial ran for three years and compared 1,500 mg a day with placebo in people with knee osteoarthritis. Pain and mobility improved, and joint space stayed more stable in the treated group. Other trials found no overall benefit, and a large US study saw improvement only in the subgroup with moderate or severe pain.

Reviews pool all of that and reach a modest conclusion: it may reduce pain slightly more than placebo in some people, with few side effects. Whether it modifies cartilage structure remains unclear. Later trials with other preparations did not reproduce the structural result, which is part of why the reviews disagree.

## Why the effect is small

Oral glucosamine is almost completely absorbed, but only a tiny fraction reaches the joint fluid. Published estimates put it around 0.4% of the dose. That would explain why results are inconsistent and why the effect takes weeks or months to appear. The gap between the dose on the label and the amount that reaches the tissue is the whole story.

It also explains why it does nothing for acute pain: if the problem started yesterday, the capsule will not solve it today.

## Tendons, athletes and prevention

A tendinopathy is not osteoarthritis. The tendon that hurts at the knee or the heel responds to progressive loading, not to a supplement. No trial shows that glucosamine repairs a tendon or prevents injury in a healthy athlete.

In that territory, what works is well-programmed loading exercise and, if pain becomes chronic, a professional assessment.

## Dose, form and safety

The trial protocol is 1,500 mg a day of glucosamine sulfate, split into three 500 mg doses. Chondroitin sulfate, 1,200 mg a day, has been tested alongside it and does not change the picture: the benefit stays small. Trials usually run for at least three months, so one good week proves nothing. A fair test therefore means three months, not three days.

Reported side effects are similar to placebo. Two reasonable precautions: anyone on anticoagulants should ask first, especially when adding chondroitin, and anyone with a shellfish allergy should check where the product comes from. In pregnancy and breastfeeding, the decision belongs to a doctor.

## How to read the label

Look for the salt in the name: glucosamine sulfate is the form used in the trials, and the hydrochloride does not have the same support. Check the milligrams per serving, not per container, and if the product adds chondroitin, both doses should be listed. A third-party batch analysis answers what the label does not: whether the content matches the claim.

## What to do about knee pain

If the knee hurts, the order is: assessment, strength and load adjustment. Glucosamine is a low-risk option that some people notice and many do not, and it comes after the rest. A capsule does not replace a diagnosis. Imaging and pain often disagree, so the diagnosis matters more than the supplement.
