A medium egg weighs about 50 grams without the shell. The white takes two thirds of that weight and almost all the protein, while the yolk keeps the fat and a good share of the vitamins. Separating them is a simple way to raise protein without raising calories.

## What egg white protein brings and why it is used so much

A medium egg white provides about 3.6 grams of protein, 17 kcal and no fat. That ratio explains its popularity in cutting phases: it adds protein to a meal without displacing carbohydrates or pushing the calorie total up.

Egg white protein is complete. It contains all nine essential amino acids in proportions the muscle uses well, with high digestibility once cooked. Its main protein is ovalbumin, around half the total, and that is why it is treated as a quality reference.

## How much protein a serving holds

The typical serving is four to six whites, between 120 and 180 millilitres if liquid, or between 25 and 30 grams if powdered. That volume covers about 15 to 22 grams of protein, a useful part of the 25 to 40 grams worth having per meal when you train strength.

The figures vary little between brands and formats, but it is worth reading the label: some powdered whites carry flavours, sweeteners or thickeners that add carbohydrates without announcing it.

## White, whole egg or whey

The white wins on protein per calorie; the whole egg wins on nutrients. The yolk concentrates vitamin D, choline, lutein and fat, and removing it from every egg of the week impoverishes the diet for no reason. The honest comparison is per serving and per goal, not per reputation.

Against a whey supplement, the white offers food protein, more satiety and a price per gram that is usually lower. Whey wins on convenience and absorption speed, a minor advantage when the day's food already covers protein. ## Avidin and biotin: why the whites get cooked

Raw egg white contains avidin, a protein that binds biotin and blocks its absorption. Cooking denatures avidin and removes the problem. Eating raw whites regularly, in shakes for example, is the only situation that can create a biotin deficiency with symptoms such as hair loss or skin irritation.

Pasteurised whites are safe from a microbiological point of view, so there is no obligation to boil them. Cooking improves the digestibility of their protein and the taste, and it remains the most practical option for most recipes.

## Fresh, pasteurised or powdered

A fresh egg is the cheapest option and the one that generates the least packaging. Separating the whites takes a minute and the yolk can go into another preparation. Pasteurised white saves that step and keeps for weeks in the fridge unopened.

Powdered white is the most convenient for travel and usually the worst value per gram of protein. Rehydrated, it works in shakes and pancakes, though the taste changes. None of the three is better nutritionally: the difference is in the use you give it.

## How to fit it into the day

At breakfast, scrambled whites with oats and fruit cover protein and carbohydrates at once. After training, an omelette of whites with potato or wholemeal bread replaces what was used. At dinner, a scramble with spinach and mushrooms leaves a light meal without falling short on protein.

The white goes with almost anything, but it gets boring if repeated. Alternating with whole eggs, fish, legumes and dairy keeps variety and covers micronutrients the white alone does not bring. ## Common mistakes

The first is believing the white burns fat. It brings protein with few calories, and that helps fit a diet, but it does not burn fat or replace a deficit. The second is relying on it alone: a monotonous diet ends in deficiencies and in quitting.

The third is eating raw whites out of habit. The fourth is buying powdered white without checking the price per gram of protein, which sometimes beats a steak. [Supplement mistakes](/en/supplement-mistakes/) go over the rest of the decisions that do not change the result.
